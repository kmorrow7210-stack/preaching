# Preaching Diagnostic: Manuscript vs. Transcript

**Seven sermons analyzed:** 1 Samuel 1, 2, 3 · Acts 27 · Luke 19 (Palm Sunday) · Matthew 27 (Easter) · Revelation 12

---

## The headline finding

Your sermon has a predictable geography, and it maps exactly onto your movement in the room. What survives from manuscript to pulpit is determined almost entirely by **where you are standing when the line is due.**

What consistently survives, often nearly verbatim: the cold open, the scripture reading, the opening prayer, the first movement, and the final move/altar call — everything delivered at or near the pulpit. Your Acts 27 invitation ("the money math," "2 a.m. courtroom," "your nervous system is on fire") tracked the manuscript almost line for line. So did the Bunyan prison quotes, the Chilean miners open, and the Sutcliffe snake story.

What consistently dies: **the apex insight of the middle movements** — the exact stretch where you're off the stage, pacing, away from your notes. This is not an occasional loss. It happened in all seven sermons, and what you lose is disproportionately your best material, because you naturally place your crown-jewel discovery at the climax of the exposition — which is the point in the service where you're furthest from the manuscript.

## Bad habit #1: The ending pays for the middle's tangents

Your altar calls are planned at 3–5 minutes and repeatedly get 60–90 seconds. The worst case is Easter: the entire composed communion section — Gilgal stones, Ebenezer, the Revelation 5 scars meditation, "Rome's seal was wax; this seal is blood" — **does not appear in the transcript at all.** Some of your strongest writing of the year was in that section. The Rev 12 altar call (the two groups, Peter on the beach, "Do you love me?") suffered the same fate; the transcript ends before any of it.

Related tell: "If you want to play something, I'm closing" appears in five of seven transcripts — and in Luke 19 and Rev 12 it precedes eight to ten more minutes of preaching. The congregation and the musicians have learned that signal means nothing. The tangents feel free in the moment because the cost is deferred to the one part of the service that can't be compressed without losing people at the altar.

## Bad habit #2: Unplanned material clusters into culture-war territory

Compare the two columns honestly: **zero** of the seven manuscripts contain political or culture-war content. The transcripts contain, all ad-libbed: the rainbow/pride-month run (1 Sam 2), abortion twice plus the sexual-revolution run and separation-of-church-and-state excursus (Rev 12), "brainwash our children with sexual imagery" (Matt 27), the Trump/Barron motorcade illustration (Luke 19), the 2008 bailout and both-conventions material (Acts 27), Israel-1948/last-days insertion (Luke 19), the "why do they name hurricanes after women" quip (Acts 27), and "you want to go to hell because that's who's going to be there?" (1 Sam 1).

This is not a comment on your convictions — it's a comment on *process*. If a cultural point deserves the pulpit, it deserves to be weighed and worded in the study like everything else you say. Right now 100% of this material is reflex, not decision. Two specific costs showed up in the data:

1. **It breaks your emotional arcs.** In Luke 19, the manuscript's most devastating section — Jesus *klaio*-weeping over the city, the collateral-damage-of-pride build — got interrupted mid-lament by an inserted end-times run ("you can't look at that and say we're not in the last days"). The manuscript knew better than to put anything between the tears and "you and your children within you."
2. **It's where your factual errors live.** Your scripted facts are consistently solid — the research holds up. Your improvised facts drift: the Maccabean revolt dated "half a century" before Jesus (off by ~150 years; the manuscript wisely didn't date it), the John Laurens story delivered as "John Lawrence, son of a senator, an abolitionist" with garbled details, Palm Sunday described as "a crisp fall morning" (the manuscript says hot spring air). None of these are fatal individually; the pattern is that ad-lib mode turns off your own fact-checking.

## Bad habit #3: Structure scrambles when you're recalling instead of reading

- **1 Sam 1:** The manuscript sets the national context (Judges, corrupt priesthood, "word of the LORD was rare") *before* introducing Hannah. In delivery you started with Hannah's family drama, then backtracked mid-sermon ("Now, First Samuel, it follows Judges…") — a visible seam where you rebuilt the outline from memory.
- **Matt 27:** The three-stones structure tangled; you said "that's cry number two" twice for different things, and Stone #2's punchline ("they sealed the Stone behind a stone") lost its landing.
- **Luke 19:** The future-indicative grammar point got inverted in delivery — the manuscript's point is the certainty of the *praise* ("the stones WILL cry out — not might"), but you delivered it as certainty of the *judgment*, conflating verse 40 with verse 44.

## Bad habit #4: Illustration stacking

When an ad-lib illustration occurs to you, it gets *added* to the planned one rather than replacing it. Rev 12 ran four illustrations for one point (defeated enemy still fighting): the dead snake, the Dead Hand doctrine, John Laurens, and the Japanese holdout soldier. The manuscript had two, and the extra two diluted the snake callback that the whole sermon was named for. Acts 27 added Elijah's still small voice (thematically good, but another 90 seconds), the pot-bellied stove stories, and the Camp Creek history on top of a full manuscript.

## Bad habit #5: The scripture reading is your roughest two minutes

For a preacher whose entire method is "get in the text," the reading itself is consistently the least rehearsed moment: "the air of the lamp of God went out" (1 Sam 3 — which momentarily *reverses* the verse your whole sermon hangs on: the lamp had NOT yet gone out), "went to see the supper" for sepulchre, "rammit" for raiment, a dropped clause in Hannah's vow ("and remember me and not forget thine handmaid" vanished mid-verse), "Matthew 24 — I mean 27," "Luke 19:20" for 19:40, "Peter writes in Ephesians 4 — I mean Paul." The KJV's syntax is fighting you in real time. Options: rehearse the reading aloud once Saturday night, print it in large type double-spaced, or read from a modern translation and quote KJV where the cadence matters.

## Bad habit #6: Housekeeping smothers the cold open

You write genuinely strong cold opens — the miners, the severed snake head, Leipzig, two parades. On Easter, the miners open was preceded by ninety seconds of series scheduling ("we're going through Revelation… next week we're dealing with heaven…"). A cold open only works cold. Announcements belong before the reading or after the sermon, never between "You can be seated" and the hook.

## Smaller tics worth knowing about

Greek terms garble under pressure when unscripted (*epitimao* → "epiteo," *klaio* → "cleo," *aphiēmi* → "aime") — where the manuscript scripted the explanation plainly (the *dia*/basis point in Rev 12), it survived fine. Name fumbles recur in narration: Adonijah took four attempts in Luke 19, you said "Samuel prayed… God heard *her* prayer" (1 Sam 3), and "I wonder if Samuel was thinking" when you meant Eli. And the volume peaks (visible as the all-caps runs in the transcripts) land disproportionately on ad-libbed riffs rather than the planned climaxes — you're spending your vocal intensity on the tangents and often delivering the manuscript's engineered peak flat or not at all.

## What's working — protect these

**Your planned-extemporaneous zones work.** The Easter manuscript's "[EXTEMPORANEOUS — Silence of Saturday]" runway with cue options is the single smartest device in these seven manuscripts, and the transcript proves it: you took the runway, used the empty-seat and biopsy-envelope cues, added the tombstone image, and landed the anchor phrase. That's the model for taming everything else.

**Your unplanned personal testimony is elite.** The Aunt Audrey story (1 Sam 3), the pastoral-numbness confession (1 Sam 2), and especially the COVID/wilderness testimony (Rev 12 — dad's death, three kids, the gooseneck trailer church, "that pastor couldn't pastor this church") are among the strongest moments in any of these transcripts, and none were in the notes. This isn't material to suppress; it's material to *schedule*. Give testimony a planned slot and it stops competing with the altar call for time.

**Your localization instinct is a genuine gift.** James Spann, Terrence Cody blocking the kick, Old Charlie's soup, "those of you that know where we live, there's creeks all over here," Fair Havens as "Minnesota vs. Alabama." Nobody can manuscript that for you and you shouldn't try.

**Some ad-libs beat the manuscript.** "Death, like a viper, latched itself to him… and on the third day he flipped his wrist and put that viper in the fire" (Acts 27) improved the written version. "He didn't just sleep near the ark — he IS the mercy seat. He IS the tabernacle" (1 Sam 3). The head-counting bit ("275… and Paul said, 276, I'm right here. I told y'all"). The instinct is good; the problem is only that it's ungoverned.

## Prescriptions

**1. Build the sermon around your geography, not against it.** You already know the pattern: pulpit → floor → occasional glance → pulpit for the last move. So stop putting must-say-verbatim material in the middle of movements you preach from the floor. Three options, use any mix: (a) move each crown-jewel insight to the *start* of a movement, delivered from the pulpit before you walk; (b) make the walk back a deliberate beat — "Come here, look at this — I want to get this exactly right" is a credibility device, not a weakness; (c) accept that mid-floor content is paraphrase territory and design it that way.

**2. Carry a spine card instead of finding your place.** A single half-sheet on the pulpit — not the manuscript — with the five to seven lines that must be said verbatim ("First person in the Bible to call God LORD of hosts… and it's a barren woman on the floor"; "Heaven used Rome's seal as furniture"; "It's a shepherd's rod"). When you come back to glance, you're not hunting for your place in eight pages; you're checking off jewels. If it's not on the card, losing it is acceptable.

**3. Write to 70% of your slot.** Your delivered length runs 1.2–3x the manuscript. A 30-minute slot needs about 20 manuscript-minutes plus two or three *planned* extemporaneous runways (the Easter model). You've proven you'll fill the space; decide in the study where it gets filled.

**4. Make "I'm closing" mean three minutes.** Hard personal rule. And budget the ending *first*: on communion Sundays and altar-call-heavy texts, the closing liturgy gets its minutes before the exposition does. The Easter communion meditation should never again be the casualty.

**5. Pre-decide the cultural material.** New rule for yourself: no unscripted hot-topic content, period. If it belongs in the sermon, it goes in the manuscript by Friday, where you can weigh whether it serves the text, word it pastorally, and place it where it won't sever an emotional arc. What survives that filter you'll preach with more authority, not less — because it will be aimed.

**6. One illustration per point.** If a story occurs to you mid-sermon and the point already has one, the new story goes in a note for next week, not in this sermon. The ad-lib replaces or it waits.

**7. Rehearse the reading once.** Out loud, Saturday. Large-print the text. Verify the reference before you announce it. Two minutes of prep protects the two minutes where you most represent the text itself.

**8. Keep the fire.** Nothing here should sand off what makes you *you* in that room — the pacing, the volume, the "y'all know me," the willingness to bleed a little in front of your people. The diagnosis isn't that you leave the manuscript. It's that the manuscript and the man currently compete for the same minutes, and the material that loses is disproportionately your best study and your planned landing. Fix the geography, guard the ending, script the hot takes, and the same energy starts compounding instead of leaking.
