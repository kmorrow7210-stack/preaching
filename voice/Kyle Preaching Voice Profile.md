# Kyle's Preaching Voice Profile

**What this is:** a description of how Kyle (Straight Creek Church of God, Pentecostal) preaches, built from the words of eight Sunday sermons. Any Claude thread drafting a sermon manuscript, outline, illustration, or altar call for Kyle should read this first and write to match it. Kyle can read it too, as a mirror of his own habits.

**Two cautions about the source:**
1. **Seven of these are YouTube auto-captions; the eighth (Mark 9, October 4) is a speech-recognition transcript of the church's audio.** Odd words are transcription errors, not Kyle's words. Don't copy them as style. Examples: "Catholics" (Decapolis), "Coffin's head" (John's head), "Samson" (*korasion*), "dolls" (dogs), "Harry" (Herod), "Jaboth Gilead" (Jabesh-Gilead), "quatronians" (quaternions), "Bluffenia" (Bithynia), "obligata" (amygdala), "Cama Desert" (Atacama), "epheme" (*aphēken*), "lithios" (*lithos*), "korygma" (*charagma*), "sandstone" (seeing stone), "Lambie" (lamb beast), "Paul in silence" (Paul and Silas), "prosecutors" (prostitutes).
2. **Delivery isn't in a transcript.** Tone, volume, pacing, pauses, the Pentecostal rise and fall, and congregational response can't be read from text. This profile covers words and structure only.

## 1. The big picture in one paragraph

Kyle is a **narrative, verse-by-verse storyteller who preaches through whole books in series** (Mark, 1–2 Samuel, Acts, Revelation). He opens with a vivid true story, retells the biblical passage as a scene, links it to the rest of the Bible and to history, and frames everything around one contrast (strong man vs. stronger man; taken vs. received; the loud kingdom vs. the quiet kingdom; the counterfeit lamb vs. the Lamb who bled). He boils that contrast down to a short, repeatable line. He moves between careful study (Greek words, Josephus, ancient customs) and down-home Appalachian conversation, often in the same minute. His deepest recurring theme is **God's kingdom against the spirit of empire**. He applies the text **all the way through**, with concrete pictures of ordinary hurting people, and lands at an open altar with an invitation shaped by that sermon's image.

## 2. The shape of a Kyle sermon

1. **Where we are in the series.** Every sermon opens by placing itself: last week, what's next, how long until the book ends, or why this week is a break ("We took a little bit of a break for Palm Sunday and Easter"). He often mentions Wednesday night. [7/8; not heard in the Mark 9 recording, whose first seconds are missing]
2. **Call to the text.** "Go with me to the book of…", "If you'll stand with me for the reading of the Word." [7/8]
3. **Scripture reading, in the King James.** [8/8] He sometimes stops on purpose: "We'll stop right there." [2/8]
4. **Title announced just before the prayer.** [8/8] The wording varies: "I want to preach to you this morning on…", "I want to talk to you about…", "pray for me as I prepare to preach on…"
5. **Pulpit prayer** (see section 7). [8/8]
6. **"You can be seated (in the presence of the Lord / here in the Lord's house)."** [5/8]
7. **Opening move.** Usually **a true story told at length**, with dates, names, numbers and places, then bridged to the text. [6/8]
   - History: D-Day (1944), the Hatfields and McCoys (1878), Bletchley Park and the Enigma code, the 33 Chilean miners (2010).
   - Story: Tolkien's *palantír* (Lord of the Rings).
   - Personal and funny: at a ministers' meeting he compared radio clips to dove hunting ("I have never been dove hunting in my life"), then bridged to Peter, who "did not know what to say" (Mark 9:6).
   - Or, less often, a recap of last week [1/8] or a direct question ("What do you do when obeying God leads you straight into suffering instead of success?") [1/8].
8. **The bookend.** In the newer sermons, the opening story comes back at the very end with a twist that lands the point. [3/8; the Mark 9 opener paid off early, at Peter on the mountain, not at the end]
   - Bletchley Park cracked Enigma through a phrase the enemy kept repeating, "Heil Hitler." *"His very own catchphrase led to his downfall. That's what pride does."*
   - The miners' note came up on a drill bit; the rescue capsule was named *Fénix* (Phoenix), but it went **down** first. *"The Lord's phoenix… started by going down."*
   - The surveillance company named itself Palantir. *"Didn't even try to hide it."*
9. **The story, retold scene by scene,** citing verses as he goes and filling in background. [8/8]
10. **Wide connections** to other Scripture, history and the author's design (see section 4). [8/8]
11. **The turn: "What I'm saying is…" / "I want you to understand this morning…" / "I'm here to tell you…"** States the big idea plainly. [8/8 in some form]
12. **Pastoral application with concrete lists** (see section 6). [8/8]
13. **The closing cue**, to the congregation and the musician: "If you'll stand with me, I'm closing." / "Sister Barbara, if you want to come and play, I'm getting ready to close." / "Brother, if you want to play, I'm closing." [7/8]
14. **Altar call and closing prayer** (see section 7). [7/8 seen]

**Length:** 32 to 62 minutes, usually around 40 (the Mark 9 sermon ran 43); roughly 5,500 to 10,400 spoken words. The four newest sermons run longer than the first three, and he jokes about it ("Last week I feel like I preached everything I knew and a few things I wasn't so sure about"). For a 40-minute sermon, a written manuscript should land around **4,500 to 5,500 words**, since he expands live. **Checked against the Mark 9 sermon:** a 4,850-word manuscript became about 7,400 spoken words and 43 minutes, so he adds roughly half again in the pulpit. Estimate time at about 110 manuscript words per minute, not 180.

## 3. How he names and repeats the big idea

- **One contrast frames the whole sermon.** [7/8] Strong man / stronger man; Herod's table / Jesus' table; taken / received; the loud kingdom / the quiet kingdom; the sealed stone / the rolled-away stone; the counterfeit lamb / the Lamb who bled; the mountain / the valley, "with him" / "for him."
- **A refrain comes back again and again,** including in the altar call. [8/8]
  - "The stronger one is here."
  - "A throne was received, not taken."
  - "Don't choose the room. Choose Him."
  - "The church was earnestly praying." / "Pray again."
  - "Can you sing at midnight?"
  - "The stones cried out."
  - "The beast keeps changing its clothes."
  - "Listen to him." / "That they might be with him." / "Who's in the room?"
- **Aphorisms built on a turn.** His most quotable habit. [8/8]
  - "Herod's power was built on fear. The church's power was built on prayer."
  - "Peace is not the absence of chains, but peace is the presence of prayer."
  - "The loud kingdom boasts while the quiet kingdom prays."
  - "The sea beast is the muscle. The land beast is the marketing."
  - "The land beast don't show up with monsters. He shows up with microphones."
  - "What Satan offered as a shortcut on the first mountain, the Father gave as a reward on the last mountain."
  - "If they can keep going with beaten backs, then I can keep going with a bruised ego."
  - "The propaganda machine don't recruit villains. It recruits people that think they're helping."
  - "You can have the title and not have the touch."
  - "It wasn't just so I could walk in the clouds. It's so that authority I got in the cloud could work in the valley."
  - "He gave them authority, but he did not make them independent of him."
- **He counts in threes.** Three tables, three stones and three cries, three knocks, three households, three levels of faith ("God can… God will… but if not"). [5/8]
- **Roll-calls that sum up a section:** "Abner was trying to save a house. Joab was trying to settle a debt…" / "In Egypt, the beast wore a Pharaoh's crown. In Babylon, he built a golden statue. In Rome, he said, give me a pinch of incense." [5/8] Mark 9: "They had watched demons go. They had anointed the sick… They had heard the wind and the waves go quiet at his voice."
- **Lines and sermons carried across weeks.** "The mercy that was on that shore did not stay on that shore" appears in both Mark sermons. The Palm Sunday sermon asked "Will these rocks cry out?" and the Easter sermon answered it. The Mark 9 sermon calls Jesus "the one who is a stronger man," from the Mark 5 sermon. [4/8]

## 4. How he handles the Bible

- **Reads in the King James; quotes a modern translation in the body.** [8/8] Body quotes are usually NLT wording ("There were so many people coming and going…"), sometimes ESV-like ("It was kind of you to share my distress," Philippians 4:14). **Kyle's stated rule (October 2026): KJV for the opening Scripture reading, then NIV for every reference in the body of the sermon unless he says otherwise.** Drafts follow that rule, not the NLT wording the captions show. In the Mark 9 sermon he quoted the NIV from the manuscript, as the rule says.
- **Cites verses out loud as he goes** ("Mark 6, 26 says…", "Acts 16:34 says…"). [8/8]
- **Hunts for patterns across the whole Bible.** He says so himself: "I love finding patterns in Scripture." [8/8] Examples:
  - 3,000 died at Sinai when the law came on stone; 3,000 were saved at Pentecost when the Spirit came.
  - The devil's mountain (Matthew 4) and the Great Commission mountain (Matthew 28).
  - Fire from heaven at Sinai, on Carmel and at Pentecost, and the beast's counterfeit fire.
  - Pentecost reversing Babel; the Lamb's seven horns (Revelation 5) vs. the beast's two (Revelation 13).
  - Genesis 1's chaos-sea behind the stilled storm; Genesis 2's "bone of my bones" behind Israel's covenant with David.
  - The Father's voice at the baptism (spoken *to* Jesus, Mark 1) and at the transfiguration (spoken *to the disciples*, Mark 9); Moses "about to be taken away" promising a prophet like him (Deuteronomy 18:15).
- **The same word, the same author.** He loves showing that a writer reused a word on purpose. [5/8] *korasion* for both girls in Mark; *symposia* at Herod's banquet and the feeding of the 5,000; Luke's "stones" on Palm Sunday and Easter morning; Luke's "earnestly" for the church's prayer and Jesus in Gethsemane.
- **Notices the author's design.** Why Mark "rewinds" to John's death; why 2 Samuel 4:4 sits in the middle of the assassinations; why Matthew left in "but some doubted"; why Mark "keeps showing us" Jesus in prayer. [6/8]
- **Original-language words, explained in plain English.** [8/8] Usually one to three per sermon: *symposia*, *ekballō* ("to drive out, to throw out"), *aphiēmi* (Jesus actively "released" his spirit), *lithos* vs. *petra*, *charagma* (the official stamp on coins and certificates), "python" for the spirit of divination, *metamorphoō* ("metamorphosis").
- **Ancient background in a sentence or two:** Josephus [3/8], honor-and-shame vows, cities of refuge, Roman seals, imperial cults and trade guilds, Roman citizenship, quaternions of soldiers, the Antonia Fortress, fasting as mourning at a wedding. [8/8]
- **Answers the objection before someone raises it:** "Somebody might say, well, preacher, don't the Bible say…?" / "We preach sometimes that the same crowd sang Hosanna and shouted crucify. Those were different crowds." / "We can say, well, he was physically present… but he wasn't physically present in Mark chapter 6." [5/8]
- **Fair to people on the wrong side:** Abner "is not trying to stop the Lord"; the deceived are "not the beast… they're the deceived"; "Some of them may believe they're doing something good." The nine disciples who failed: "If we're not careful we can say, well, these guys just wasn't the real thing." [3/8]
- **Revelation: pattern now, person later.** He reads Revelation as a pattern already at work ("The beast is here, and he's been here the whole time. He just keeps changing his clothes"), while holding a future Antichrist and a pre-tribulation rapture ("I believe we're going to be gone during the tribulation"). He gently mocks date-setting ("88 Reasons… 1988," barcodes, microchips). [1/8, so far]

## 5. Voice and sentence style

### Conversational, Appalachian, warm [8/8]
He talks like a neighbor on the porch who also reads Josephus.
- **Direct address:** "church" (constantly; 54 times in one sermon), "folks," "y'all" [3/8], "Buddy," "Hear me."
- **Local and family references:** a church on Sand Mountain in Alabama, the Hatfield-McCoy dinner show in Sevierville, the cemetery behind the church, Dora Wigley's prayer meeting, his wife and three kids.
- **Regional grammar in speech:** "don't" for "doesn't," "ain't," "come" for "came," "could have went," "they was," "needed saved," and idioms like "two pennies to rub together," "hat in hand," "on the lam," "over yonder," "hug some necks."

**For written drafts:** keep the warmth, the direct address, and words like *church*, *folks*, *Buddy*, *kinfolk*. Write the grammar standard and let Kyle add the vernacular as he preaches, unless he asks for it on the page. Never exaggerate the dialect; that would read as caricature.

### Short sentences in parallel [8/8]
- "He wasn't arrested for being rude. He was arrested for being right."
- "Your exhaustion does not make you invisible. Your grief does not make you unreachable. Your shame does not make you untouchable."
- "When I don't know what to do, what do I do? I pray. When I'm faced with tragedy, what do we do? We pray…"
- "They prayed through the Depression. They prayed through two world wars. They prayed through Korea…"
- "Go. I know you've had doubts. Go. I know you messed up. Go."

### Signature phrases (use them, but not all in one paragraph)

| Phrase | Job it does | Seen |
|---|---|---|
| "this morning" | Makes it present and personal | 8/8, constantly |
| "Notice…" / "Look at…" / "Watch this." | Points at the next detail or verse | 8/8 |
| "I love this part right here." / "I love what happens here." | Signals delight in the text | 7/8 |
| "Praise God." / "Hallelujah." | Spontaneous praise after a strong point | 7/8 |

| "Here's the thing." | Pivots to the insight (growing: 7 times in the Revelation sermon) | 4/8 |
| "I'm here to tell you…" / "Let me tell you something." | Raises the energy before a claim | 5/8 |
| "I want you to understand / know this morning…" | The main turn to application ("So here's what I want you to take with you") | 5/8 |

### Humor [8/8]
Light, quick, often at his own expense, often in the middle of a serious story.
- "This is half a kingdom for a man that don't even have one."
- Peter at Mary's door: "It's easier to get the door from Antonia Fortress open than it is to get past Rhoda." / "Hey! Seriously, y'all! I'm on the most wanted list here."
- "If I would have been a disciple, I couldn't have been a gospel writer. They'd be like, quit adding your details."
- "Before there was Dwayne Johnson, there was Simon Peter… he raises that one eyebrow."
- Paul and Silas in the cell: "I can't believe I listened to you." "Well, Silas, if you'd have been a better preacher…" "No, that's not what they did, is it?"
- "If he had a college football team, it would be Michigan."
- "It's our physical body that can finally eat carbohydrates and don't have to deal with acid reflux."
- On his long sermons: "I know some of you sitting there thinking, well, you sure do try."
- "I have never been dove hunting in my life." / Moses and Elijah as the disciples' trading cards / Jesus doesn't even use all three names: "like when your mama was mad at you" / "You eat the little barbecue meatballs and eat the mints and drink the punch."

## 6. Illustrations and application

### Kinds of illustrations he reaches for
- **True stories with real names, dates and numbers.** His strongest type. [8/8] D-Day and the Atlantic Wall; the Hatfields and McCoys; Henry VIII, John Fisher and Thomas More; Bletchley Park; the Chilean miners ("Estamos bien en el refugio los 33"); Pharaoh, Babylon and Rome as one beast in changing clothes; neighbors taking the curtains from deported Jewish families; Polycarp writing to Philippi; the Brownsville revival; his own church's prayer meeting through the Depression and two world wars; George VI, Lionel Logue and the September 3, 1939 broadcast (The King's Speech). **World War II is a favorite well** [4/8].
- **Pop culture, quick and nostalgic.** [7/8] *Lord of the Rings* (twice: "become who you were born to be"; Saruman and the palantír), *The Chosen*, *Fresh Prince of Bel-Air*, Homer's *Odyssey*, Star Wars ("a Jedi mind trick"), The Rock, zombies, UFC, college football, Faust, Batman/Superman/Spiderman and trading cards.
- **Recurring examples across sermons:** Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego's "but if not" [2/8]; the Herod family [2/8]; COVID [2/8]; Genesis 1's chaos-sea [2/8].
- **Personal stories.** [5/8] Usually brief (his sister in California, his uncle's Mephibosheth sermon, COVID as a young pastor, wood blood-drop signs on church members' homes). Once long and vulnerable: in "Singing at Midnight" he told about six minutes of his own story, deleting his Substack after his open rate fell from 40% to 4%, owning his "rejection sensitivity," and landing it: *"I'm a pastor that writes, not a writer that pastors."* In the Mark 9 sermon he told about receiving the Holy Ghost ("standing about right here" in a Church of God, praying "God, fill me") and going home "to a daddy that wasn't saved… got a drinking problem. We got money problems," landing it on the sermon's point: the authority from the cloud is for the valley.
- **Research and psychology language.** [4/8] "Collective trauma," "trauma has a way of making us forget what was promised," studies on group conformity, algorithms and dopamine. Always in plain words, always pointed back to faith.

### Imagining the scene [8/8]
He puts the listener inside the story with sensory detail and invented dialogue.
- "I could just hear the guards showing up. Your Majesty, that preacher's at it again."
- The inner prison at Philippi: "No windows… full of rats… Can you imagine the stench?" and how the stocks could be spread apart.
- The angel "whack, hits Peter in the side." "Get up. Hurry. Put your sandals on."
- Jesus in Abraham's bosom: "Come on, Isaiah. Come on, Daniel. Come on, Moses."
- He voices the enemy ("I can't let this happen on my watch") and Jesus ("Make no mistake, my child, I'm on my way to the other side"), and is careful about it: "When you preach, you speak for God. So I'll take the risk and say…"

### Application: concrete, tender, all the way through
- **Applies mid-story, not only at the end.** "Have you ever been there? Maybe you're there right now." [8/8]
- **Lists of specific, ordinary burdens.** A defining habit. [8/8]
  - "There's a bill sitting on your kitchen table. You've already done the math on it three times."
  - "An empty seat at the dinner table… that same tombstone… that biopsy report that you haven't even opened the envelope yet."
  - "The phone call from your kid that ended with a click."
  - "You stand up at work… and you get passed over for the promotion. At Thanksgiving, you're the one that's shunned."
  - "You prayed and it ended in a jail sentence. You prayed and it ended in a divorce."
- **Then a reversal litany:** "That hospital room is within His reach…" / "When the doctor reads a scan, we pray again. When the layoff hits, we pray again. When the addiction whispers, we pray again." / "I believe, but the boy still got the devil. I believe, but I still gotta watch the fire." / "Listen to him when he tells you what to give. Listen to him when he tells you what to lay down." [6/8]
- **Honest about unanswered prayer and lingering pain.** [6/8] "We prayed for James and James still died, but that don't mean we're going to quit praying." "We've seen healing happen on both sides of eternity." "Things are getting better… but that don't mean it doesn't hurt." "I don't know how those moments will turn out."
- **Obedience over outcomes.** [3/8] "It's not about the end result. It's about being obedient."
- **Practical holiness, named plainly.** [3/8] "That's why I quit going to the club…" "This fire costs something… Without holiness no man shall see God."

### Culture and current events [6/8; none in the Mark 9 sermon]
He regularly names what is happening in the culture: the "spirit of empire," COVID-era restrictions on churches, social media and algorithms, AI-generated video, a cash-free economy and surveillance, abortion, gender and sexuality, a recent New York City election, persecution in Nigeria. He usually adds a guardrail [4/8]: "I'm not blaming a politician or even a single party. I'm blaming the spirit behind it." / "There was a legitimate pandemic… we were being responsible." / "I'm not going to call anybody by name." / "I'm not saying the cell phone's wrong." / "Both extremes feed the machine." A stable conviction line appears nearly word for word in two sermons [2/8]: *"We don't offend for the sake of offense, but we stand with the truth."*

**For drafts:** keep his guardrails and his "spirit behind it, not the people" framing. Don't invent new specific political claims in his voice; mark a spot as *[current example here]* and let Kyle choose.

## 7. Prayers, altar calls and closings

### Opening pulpit prayer [8/8]
Short, personal, asks for anointing, ends in Jesus' name.
> "Would you pray for me, Lord? I thank you, God, for your word… I ask, God, that you'll anoint me, that you'll help me… It's in Jesus' name we pray. And the church said amen."

Recurring pieces: "Would you pray for me" [8/8] · "anoint me" [8/8] · "I thank you for your word / your spirit that I feel in this place" · "Let me simply be the mouthpiece" / "Don't let this be my words, but your words" · "preach under the unction of your Holy Ghost" · "we'll give you the praise, the honor and the glory" · "And the church said amen" [4/8]. He sometimes builds the sermon's theme into the prayer ("when things look quiet… there are things going on behind the scenes").

### Altar call [7/8 seen]
- **"This altar is open. I want to pray for you."** [7/8]
- **More than one group is invited,** usually in this order [6/8]:
  1. Those who need to be saved or come back. Model prayer [3/8]: *"Lord, I have sinned. I need forgiveness. I'm asking you to save me, and I put my trust in you."* Or Paul's answer to the jailer: *"Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household."* Reassurance: *"It don't have to sound impressive. It just has to come from the heart."*
  2. Believers who are worn out, grieving or carrying something.
  3. Sometimes a third group tied to the sermon: those called to use their influence, or those praying for a son or daughter "at the gate."
- **The response is shaped by the sermon's image.** [5/8] "Lay down the silver robe." "I'm in my midnight, but I'm going to sing." "Take off the brand and be sealed by the Lamb that bled for you." "Bring whatever's been sealed up… Come and see." "Maybe you're that father in the red light."
- **"Come now."** repeated as urgency builds. [2/8]

### Closing prayer [5/8 seen]
Weaves the sermon's own images back into prayer:
> "Lord, I thank you that you set a table in the wilderness… your table is not for the powerful, but it's for the hungry."
> "Open doors this morning that no man can shut… we believe your word is going to increase and multiply."
> "Write your name on us, seal us, Lord, not with a brand that enslaves, but with a brand that sets us free."
> "Help our unbelief… everything that we do, everything that we are comes from the connection that we have with you… This church is here because of people of prayer."

Dismissal: "God bless you this morning. It's been good to be in God's house. I hope to see you Wednesday night… Shake some hands. Hug some necks."

## 8. Theological fingerprints

What he keeps coming back to:
- **Pentecostal identity, owned openly.** [8/8] Anointing, the Holy Ghost, laying hands on the sick, Pentecost undoing Babel, "the fire" that "heals cancer… raises the dead" but "costs something." Balanced by patience: "Transformation rarely happens that way. It happens in the crucible."
- **God's kingdom vs. the spirit of empire.** [6/8] Herod, Saul's house, Rome's seal, the beast, the "loud kingdom." Power by force, fear and applause against power that is received, prayed for and given. This is the thread tying the Mark, Samuel, Acts and Revelation series together.
- **The gospel reaches everyone, especially outsiders.** [6/8] Gentiles, the demoniac, the "dogs under the table," a slave girl, a jailer, a Roman centurion, women as the first witnesses. "There is nobody that this gospel can't reach."
- **Christ's victory over the enemy.** [8/8] Spiritual warfare is real and personal, and there is "a spirit behind" evil systems; Christ "walked straight into death itself and said, this is mine."
- **Prayer and faith that don't depend on outcomes.** [6/8] "My faith isn't based on the outcome; my faith is based in Jesus." "Yet will I trust Him."
- **Grace, not earning.** [6/8] ("not because they want to earn anything") "If it was about what we deserve, we'd all be in hell today."
- **Testimony and witness.** [5/8] ("the person at work that's been watching to see if there's anything to this Jesus of yours") "You have a story to tell." "The watching world don't need more arguments… they need people who follow Jesus."
- **The church as a praying family.** [4/8] "I'm so thankful I'm part of a praying church."

## 9. Tendencies to watch (for coaching, not for copying)

Honest observations, offered to help Kyle grow and so drafts don't amplify them:
- **Length is creeping up.** The first three ran 32 to 38 minutes; the four newer ones ran 35 to 62, and he jokes about it in three of them ("I don't want to keep you here all day"). The Revelation 13 sermon carries about ten illustrations. **For drafts:** one opening story that returns at the end, plus one or two supporting examples. Cut a second illustration rather than add one.
- **The ending pays for the overrun.** In the Mark 9 sermon the front half grew (the mountain section ran about 11 minutes against about 5 planned), and the last ten minutes got compressed. The closing refrain was said once, a pastoral guardrail and the final line were dropped, and one altar group moved into the closing prayer. **For drafts:** mark which ending lines are protected, and keep the early sections lean so the landing gets its full time.
- **Long setups before the text.** About 6 minutes of Mark 1–4 recap in "The Stronger Man"; about 6 minutes of personal story in "Singing at Midnight" (powerful, but long). Drafts should get to the passage within the first quarter.
- **References slip in the moment** (or the captions garble them): Acts 5 for Acts 12:5, Luke 24 for Gethsemane, Genesis 5 for Babel, Isaiah for Elijah, "Peter writes this in Ephesians 4." He usually self-corrects live. **Drafts must give exact, checked references.**
