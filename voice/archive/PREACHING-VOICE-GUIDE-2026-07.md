# PREACHING VOICE GUIDE

**Purpose:** This is the drafting standard for Kyle Morrow's sermon manuscripts. It is built from a verified side-by-side study of seven manuscript/transcript pairs (1 Samuel 1, 2, 3 · Acts 27 · Luke 19 · Matthew 27 · Revelation 12) — see [[Preaching Diagnostic - Manuscript vs Transcript]]. Anyone drafting for Kyle (Kyle, Priscilla, Claude, Codex) writes to this guide.

**The core insight:** What survives from manuscript to pulpit is determined almost entirely by where Kyle is standing when the line is due. Don't write against the geography. Write for it.

---

## 1. The Governing Fact: Geography

Kyle's delivery pattern is predictable and consistent across all seven sermons:

| Zone | Where | What happens to the manuscript |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulpit zone** | Cold open, scripture reading, prayer, first movement, final move / altar call | Survives nearly verbatim. His best-tracked material. |
| **Floor zone** | Middle movements — pacing, off the stage, away from notes | Paraphrase territory. Crown-jewel insights placed here die. All seven sermons lost their apex insight here. |

**Drafting rules that follow:**

1. **Crown-jewel insights go at the START of a movement**, delivered from the pulpit before the walk begins. Never bury the payoff mid-movement.
2. **Or script the walk-back as a beat.** "Come here, look at this — I want to get this exactly right" is a credibility device, not a weakness. Write it into the manuscript.
3. **Mark the zones in the draft.** Tag sections `[PULPIT]` and `[FLOOR]`. Floor sections are written as paraphrase runways (see §6), not precision prose. If a line must land word-for-word, it belongs in a pulpit zone or on the spine card.
4. **Every must-say-verbatim line goes on the spine card** ([[Sermon Spine Card]] template). If it's not on the card, losing it is an accepted cost.

**Manuscript density matters.** The sparser the notes, the bigger the expansion: the 1 Samuel 2 outline tripled in delivery (2,110 → 6,370 words); full manuscripts (Luke 19, Matthew 27) held closest to plan at ~1.2–1.4x. Draft full prose for pulpit zones. Use structured runways, not bare bullets, for floor zones.

## 2. Sermon Architecture (the native shape)

Kyle's sermons follow a consistent six-part architecture. Draft to it:

1. **Cold open** — a story, confession, or vivid scene. The miners. The severed snake head. Leipzig candles. The Bunyan arrest. Often a self-deprecating personal confession ("I walk into a room and completely forget why I came... I'm just buffering"). *Rule: nothing between "You can be seated" and the hook. No series housekeeping, no announcements. A cold open only works cold.*
2. **Scripture reading (KJV) + title + invocational prayer** — fixed liturgical frame (see §4).
3. **Contemporary hook** — a direct question the congregation answers in their heart: "Have you ever prayed a prayer you were too embarrassed to say out loud?" "What's your storm right now?"
4. **Re-narration of the text** — present-tense human drama, camera language ("the camera zooms in on one barren woman," "the scene cuts back to a boy"), voiced characters, historical texture delivered conversationally.
5. **The typological reveal / Gospel turn** — the passage opens onto the whole-Bible story and lands on Jesus. Hannah's song becomes Mary's Magnificat. Samuel points to the faithful Priest. The struck rock is Christ. This is Kyle's theological signature: the Bible as one continuous story, the Protoevangelium (Gen 3:15) as the seed of every plot, the already/not-yet tension.
6. **Altar call** — direct, personal, repetitive, invitational. "This altar is open. Come." Segmented invitations ("If you're barren this morning... If you've been waiting on a hidden prayer... If you've never met the King..."). Often closes the story bookend — the cold-open story returns with its payoff (Bunyan's name, the Fénix, the shovel at Calvary).

**Story bookends are a signature device.** Open with a story missing its ending or its name; close by finishing it. Protect the withheld reveal: never leak it early (Acts 27 lost the Bunyan reveal by naming "12 years" in the intro).

## 3. Voice Constants (how the sentences sound)

**Register.** Southern Appalachian pastoral. Warm, direct, unhurried, playful, then suddenly dead serious. Contractions always. "Y'all," "ain't," "don't" for "doesn't" in emphatic runs ("He don't resurrect you halfway"), "bless his heart," "plum off," "squalling," "needing 'seen about,'" "get your clips out," "unloaded the clip," "narcs on them," "hug some necks."

**Direct address.** "Church," "hear me," "hear what I'm saying this morning," "Can anybody relate?", "Did you hear me?", "Don't miss this," "Watch this," "Look at verse 18," "I want you to feel this."

**Self-deprecation as trust-building.** The buffering confession, the communion-tray confession ("my heart had it filed under routine"), the glasses/contacts bit, "Tessa will tell you this is true." He tells on himself before he tells on the congregation. Draft at least one per sermon.

**Localization.** James Spann, Terrence Cody blocking the kick, Old Charlie's soup, "those of you that know where we live, there's creeks all over here," Fair Havens as "Minnesota vs. Alabama," Braves-game prayers. *Do not manuscript these — leave hooks for them.* Write the generic version and trust the room instinct (this is one of his genuine gifts).

**Repetition ladders (anaphora).** The engine of his climaxes and altar calls:
- "Keep pouring it out... Keep pouring till you can get up and eat again. Keep pouring till you can sing."
- "Come until the holy feels holy again. Come until the fear of the Lord comes back."
- "The lamp hasn't gone out in America... across this nation... around these altars."
Draft these deliberately — three to five rungs, escalating, ending on the anchor phrase.

**Anchor phrases.** Every sermon gets one to three short, repeatable lines that carry the whole message: "Pour it out." "The lamp hasn't gone out." "You can't kill a man that God has an appointment with." "The shovel fell at Calvary." "He is the mercy seat." These go on the spine card and recur at least three times.

**Contrast frames.** Two parades. Two crowds. Two mountains. Pulpit-and-pew. Manager-and-pilgrim. Kyle thinks in paired oppositions — structure movements around one when the text allows.

**Fill-in-the-heart questions.** Rhetorical question stacks aimed at the listener's private life: "Where did worship turn into routine? Where did the Bible become a textbook you skim? Where did prayer become a habit you do with your eyes half open?"

**Humor.** Dry, folksy, self-aware, usually mid-tension as a release valve: "He must have been on the Oregon Trail because the Bible says he had dysentery." "Before there was Dwayne Johnson, there was Simon Peter." Never at the congregation's expense; often at his own.

## 4. The Fixed Liturgical Frame (keep it verbatim)

These elements are stable across every sermon and should appear in every draft:

- **Reading:** KJV, congregation standing. Announce book, chapter, verses clearly (and verify them — see §8). The reading may be the preaching passage itself or a complementary text (a Psalm, an Epistle, OT prophecy) with a real canonical link to it; inline quotation of the preaching passage during delivery uses the NIV or ESV.
- **Title announcement:** "I want to preach to you for a few moments this morning on ___."
- **Invocational prayer**, with recurring language: "Would you pray for me? Lord, I thank you for your word... anoint me... let me get out of the way... season my words and let them be your words... and we'll be careful to give you the praise and the honor and the glory. It's in Jesus' name we pray, and the church said Amen."
- **"You can be seated"** — then straight into the cold open. Nothing in between.
- **"If you want to play something, I'm closing"** — the musician signal. *House rule: this now means three minutes.* Do not draft more than ~450 words of content after this line.
- **"This altar is open. Come."** — the altar call constant.
- **Closing prayer** over the people, returning to the sermon's controlling image (the lamp, the stones, the wilderness).

## 5. Theological Center of Gravity

Draft from these convictions; they're load-bearing, not decorative:

- **The Bible is one continuous story.** Every text connects backward to Genesis and forward to Christ. The Protoevangelium (Gen 3:15) is "the story every Bible story retells."
- **Already/not-yet.** The enemy is defeated but still dangerous — "dead snakes still bite." Victory language never erases present struggle; present struggle never erases settled victory.
- **God works through the hidden, small, and overlooked.** Hannah, Samuel in the linen ephod, the women at the tomb, Blandina, Susanna Wesley. "When one thing is dying under judgment, God is already growing the future somewhere small, hidden, weak, or overlooked."
- **Pentecostal texture.** The presence of God, the anointing, the altar as the destination of every sermon, testimony as weapon, healing and the moving of the Spirit affirmed plainly.
- **Pastoral realism about suffering.** "Getting help is not betraying faith. Medication is not a moral failure. You are not less Christian because your nervous system is on fire." Keep this note — it's one of his most distinctive registers.
- **The wilderness is prepared, not punitive.** Recurring motif across the corpus.

## 6. Planned-Extemporaneous Runways (the Easter model)

The single smartest device in the seven manuscripts, proven in delivery. Use it to give Kyle's improvisational gift a scheduled lane instead of letting it compete with the altar call for minutes.

**Format:**

```
[EXTEMPORANEOUS — <name of the beat> — <time budget>]
*This is your open runway. Step away from the pulpit if it feels right.
You're not teaching here — you're pastoring. The room will tell you how long to stay.*

*Cues (pick what the Spirit highlights — you don't need all of them):*
- <cue 1 — a concrete image>
- <cue 2>
- <cue 3>

*Anchor phrase back to the text: "<the sentence that lands the plane and returns to the manuscript>"*
```

**Rules:**
- Two or three runways per sermon, placed in floor zones.
- **One runway should be a testimony slot.** His unplanned personal testimony (Aunt Audrey, the COVID/wilderness testimony, the pastoral-numbness confession) is elite — among the strongest material in any transcript. Schedule it; don't suppress it.
- Every runway ends with a written anchor phrase that returns him to the manuscript.

## 7. Timing Math (the 70% rule)

Delivered length runs 1.2–3x the manuscript. Therefore:

- **Write to ~70% of the slot.** A 30-minute slot gets about 20 manuscript-minutes (~2,800–3,200 words of prose) plus 2–3 planned runways. He will fill the space; decide in the study where it gets filled.
- **Budget the ending FIRST.** Altar call gets its 3–5 minutes before the exposition gets anything. On communion Sundays, the communion liturgy is drafted and time-protected before Movement 1 is written. (The Easter communion meditation — Gilgal, Ebenezer, the scars, "Rome's seal was wax; this seal is blood" — was never delivered. That must not happen again.)
- **"I'm closing" = three minutes.** Hard rule, drafted accordingly.

---

## 8. Content Rules

1. **No unscripted hot-topic content. Period.** Zero of the seven manuscripts contained political or culture-war material; 100% of what was delivered in that lane was ad-libbed — and that's where the broken emotional arcs and the factual errors live. If a cultural point deserves the pulpit, it goes in the manuscript by Friday, weighed against the text, worded pastorally, and placed where it won't sever an emotional arc. What survives that filter gets preached with more authority, not less.
2. **One illustration per point.** If a story occurs mid-sermon and the point already has one, the new story goes in a note for next week. The ad-lib replaces or it waits. (Rev 12 ran four illustrations on one point and diluted the snake callback the sermon was named for.)
3. **Script every fact.** His scripted facts are consistently solid; his improvised facts drift (Maccabean dating, "John Lawrence," fall vs. spring air). Dates, names, numbers, and historical claims are written down or not said. Put the fragile ones on the spine card under "Facts."
4. **Script the Greek/Hebrew.** Scripted word-studies survive intact (the *dia*/basis point); unscripted ones garble (*epitimao* → "epiteo," *klaio* → "cleo"). Write the pronunciation-adjacent explanation in plain prose and keep the term count low — one or two per sermon, each earning its place.
5. **Protect emotional arcs.** Mark the sermon's most devastating stretch in the draft (`[DO NOT INTERRUPT — ARC]`). Nothing gets inserted between the buildup and the landing. (The Luke 19 lament was severed mid-weep by an inserted end-times run.)
6. **Guard the grammar of the point.** When a movement turns on a precise distinction (future indicative vs. subjunctive; certainty of *praise* vs. certainty of *judgment*), state the distinction twice in the manuscript — once forward, once as a guard-rail sentence ("not the judgment — the praise").

## 9. What Never Gets Sanded Off

The diagnosis was never that Kyle leaves the manuscript. Preserve, on purpose:

- The pacing, the volume swings, the all-caps peaks — but *aim* them: draft the engineered climaxes so the vocal intensity lands on the planned peak, not the tangent.
- "Y'all know me" intimacy and the willingness to bleed a little in front of his people.
- The localization instinct (§3). Nobody can manuscript it and nobody should try.
- The ad-lib upgrade instinct — some improvised lines beat the written ones ("Death, like a viper, latched itself to him… and on the third day he flipped his wrist and put that viper in the fire"). The goal is governance, not suppression: runways give it a lane.

## 10. Drafting Checklist (quick reference)

Before a manuscript is called done:

- [ ] Cold open is cold — no housekeeping between "be seated" and the hook
- [ ] Every crown-jewel insight sits at a movement start or a scripted walk-back beat
- [ ] `[PULPIT]` / `[FLOOR]` zones marked; floor zones are runways, not precision prose
- [ ] 2–3 extemporaneous runways with cues + anchor phrases; one is a testimony slot
- [ ] Manuscript ≈ 70% of slot; ending budgeted first; post-"I'm closing" content ≤ 3 minutes
- [ ] One illustration per point
- [ ] Zero unscripted hot-topic content; anything cultural is in the manuscript by Friday
- [ ] All facts, dates, names, Greek/Hebrew scripted
- [ ] Spine card drafted ([[Sermon Spine Card]]) — 5–7 verbatim jewels
- [ ] Emotional arc marked do-not-interrupt
- [ ] Anchor phrases recur ≥ 3 times and close the sermon
