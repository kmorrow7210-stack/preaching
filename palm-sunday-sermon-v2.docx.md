  
**THE KING WHO CAME TO PICK A FIGHT**

*Luke 19:28–44 | Palm Sunday — Part 1 of 2*

Kyle Morrow

Palm Sunday 2026

**I. TWO PARADES**

I want you to picture the western gate of Jerusalem. We’re talking first century here. It’s the week of Passover.

You’d most likely see Roman cavalry coming into the city. They’re on warhorses. You can imagine how blinding it would be when the armor on those things catches the afternoon sun. You’d hear the hooves cracking on the stone streets. And behind them, you’d have your infantrymen. With their leather. Their swords. And they’ve got a flag flying high with the eagle standard of the empire. In plain sight.

And at the very front, mounted on a horse, dressed ready for war — the Roman governor. Pontius Pilate.

This wasn’t just a state visit. This is SOP. Standard operating procedure. Every Passover, Pilate would leave Caesarea Maritima and take his garrison straight into Jerusalem. Because Passover was dangerous. This whole festival celebrated the last time God demolished a superpower to set His people free. You remember Egypt. The plagues. The sea is splitting open. And here they are again — occupied. But this time by Rome.

Every year the crowds would swell. Nationalism would surge. And every year, the Roman governor would show up with his war machine to remind the Jewish people of one thing:

*We’re in charge.* 

That’s the Pax Romana. The Peace of Rome. Peace at the point of a sword.

That was the parade from the west.

Now turn your head east.

Same week. Same city. On the other side.

You’ve got a crowd of Galilean peasants coming down the Mount of Olives. They’re dusty. They’re loud. They’ve been walking for days from the northern provinces. And they’ve been following this rabbi from Nazareth — the one who heals the sick, opens blind eyes, says things that make the authorities lose sleep at night. He’s riding right in the middle of the procession.

Not on a warhorse.

On a donkey.

And this crowd is shouting Psalm 118 — the coronation psalm — at the top of their lungs.

*\[Read Luke 19:35–38\]*

So you’ve got two parades. One from the west. One from the east. One backed by the most powerful military machine in the ancient world. The other backed by a group of fishermen and tax collectors and day laborers waving palm branches. And those palm branches mean something. They hadn’t been waved in public since the Maccabean revolt. That’s a nationalist symbol. Everybody in that city knew what palm branches meant.

One of these parades is still being talked about two thousand years later.

It’s not Pilate’s.

**II. THE DONKEY IS NOT WHAT YOU THINK**

Now we’ve all been taught that the donkey is about humility. Right? Jesus is meek. Jesus is gentle. He’s riding a donkey because He’s not a threat.

That’s not what’s happening here.

In the ancient Near East, a king rode a horse when he was going to war. But he rode a donkey when the war was already over. When he was entering territory that already belonged to him. That’s what the donkey is. It’s a victory ride.

Look at First Kings chapter 1\. David’s got a political crisis on his hands. Adonijah is trying to steal the throne. And David needs to publicly declare Solomon as the rightful heir — right now, before the coup succeeds. So what does he do? He doesn’t put Solomon on a warhorse. He puts him on the royal mule. Because the mule says: the battle is decided. The king has been chosen. And this city belongs to him.

That’s what the donkey means.

And the crowd knew exactly what they were looking at. The prophet Zechariah wrote this scene into the script five hundred years before it happened:

*“Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion\! Shout aloud, O daughter of Jerusalem\! Behold, your king is coming to you; righteous and having salvation is he, humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey.” — Zechariah 9:9*

But here’s the thing. Don’t stop at verse 9\. Read the next verse. Zechariah 9:10:

*“I will cut off the chariot from Ephraim and the war horse from Jerusalem; and the battle bow shall be cut off, and he shall speak peace to the nations.”*

You see what He’s doing? The donkey is a declaration. It says: I am the rightful King, and I have already defeated every power that claims to own this city. The chariot? Obsolete. The warhorse? Obsolete. The battle bow? Done. I didn’t come to fight Rome with Rome’s weapons. I came because the war is already won — on terms none of you have figured out yet.

And the location. Don’t miss this. He starts from the Mount of Olives. That’s the exact spot where Zechariah 14 says Yahweh’s feet will stand on the Day of the Lord when He shows up to judge the nations. Jesus isn’t just fulfilling one prophecy. He’s stacking them. He’s triggering every messianic alarm in the system at the same time.

This is not a humble entrance. This is an act of war. Just with different weapons.

**III. THE PANIC**

*\[Read Luke 19:39\]*

*“Teacher, rebuke your disciples.”*

The Pharisees in this crowd are terrified. And honestly? They should be.

They’ve done the math. If this peasant procession keeps shouting coronation psalms within earshot of the Roman garrison, Pilate is going to send cavalry. Real cavalry. The kind that doesn’t stop when it hits the crowd. The Pharisees are not stupid. They know exactly what a public messianic claim means under Roman law.

Treason.

And look at the word they use. “Rebuke.” In the Greek that’s epitimao. It means to sharply censure. To sternly command silence. And here’s where it gets interesting — this is the exact same verb Jesus uses to cast out demons. Same word He uses to silence a storm on Galilee. The Pharisees are treating this messianic fervor like a dangerous outbreak that needs to be shut down before it gets everyone killed.

And here’s the thing — from their perspective? *They were right.* This procession *was* dangerous. It *would* get people killed.

The man on the donkey knew that better than anyone. He just refused to stop.

— — —

**IV. THE STONES WILL CRY OUT**

*\[Read Luke 19:40\]*

*“I tell you, if these keep silent, the stones will cry out.”*

This verse gets domesticated every single Palm Sunday. We turn it into a worship song lyric. We make it sentimental. “Isn’t it beautiful — even the rocks would praise Him\!”

It’s not beautiful. It’s one of the most aggressive things Jesus says in the entire Gospel of Luke.

Let me show you why.

Start with the grammar. If you’re in Koine Greek and you want to say something hypothetical — like, “if they should happen to be silent” — you’d use the subjunctive mood. That’s the standard construction. It’s a maybe. A possibility.

But that’s not what Jesus does. He uses the future indicative. In both halves of the sentence. What that does grammatically is it eliminates the maybe. It takes contingency off the table. He’s not saying, “If they might be silent, the stones might cry out.” He’s saying: If they are silent — and let’s go ahead and assume that they are — the stones will cry out. Not might. Not could. Will.

He’s telling the Pharisees: this coronation cannot be cancelled. Your jurisdiction doesn’t apply here. You don’t have the authority to silence what the cosmos itself is compelled to announce.

Now look at the verb. The word for “cry out” is krazo. It’s visceral. It’s almost onomatopoeic — you can hear the sound in the word itself. It means to shriek. To scream. To let out a raw, uncontrolled sound. This is the same verb used when demons scream in the presence of Jesus. It’s the verb for the cry of the oppressed reaching the ears of God.

The stones wouldn’t hum a hymn. They would scream.

But here’s where this gets really sharp. Because Jesus is not making this up. He’s not inventing a metaphor. He’s reaching back into the Hebrew prophets and pulling out a loaded phrase.

Habakkuk 2:11. God is pronouncing judgment on a corrupt regime — one that built its wealth on the backs of the poor. That squeezed money out of the powerless and used it to build monuments to itself. And God says this: “The stone will cry out from the wall, and the beam from the woodwork will respond.”

Now in Habakkuk, the stones are not praising anybody. They’re testifying. They’re on the witness stand. The building materials have absorbed the violence of the builders. Every act of exploitation. Every drop of blood money. Every rigged transaction. The stones soaked it up. And in God’s courtroom, the building materials take the stand to condemn the people who laid them.

Now picture where Jesus is standing when He says this.

He’s coming down the Mount of Olives. And He’s looking right at it. The Temple Mount. Those massive, gleaming, white limestone blocks of Herod’s Temple. That structure was built by a violent puppet king using crushing taxation. It was maintained by a priestly aristocracy that had turned the house of prayer into a financial operation. One that Jesus is going to personally dismantle the second He walks through the gate.

When Jesus says “the stones will cry out,” He is not making a nice, general statement about nature worshipping God. He’s pointing at the Temple and invoking an indictment.

*If you silence the praises of these poor pilgrims,* He’s saying, *the stones of your own blood-bought Temple will take the stand against you. If the disciples stop shouting “Save us\!” the stones will start shouting “Guilty\!”*

And we know that first-century Jews already read Habakkuk this way. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls there’s a document called the Pesher Habakkuk. It’s a commentary written by the Essene community out at Qumran. And in their reading of this exact passage, they apply the crying stones directly to the corrupt priesthood of Jerusalem. This wasn’t a new idea. This critique was already circulating. Jesus is stepping into a prophetic tradition and pressing the trigger.

And Luke knows what he’s doing with this image. He doesn’t let the stone motif go. Watch this. Look four verses later.

Verse 40: The stones will cry out.

Verse 44: They will not leave one stone upon another.

Same word. Same stones. The stones that stand ready to testify in verse 40 are the same stones that are scheduled for demolition in verse 44\. The witness guarantees the verdict. The testimony of the stones seals the destruction of the stones.

And in 70 AD, that’s exactly what happened. The Roman general Titus laid siege to Jerusalem, starved its people, and tore the Temple down to its foundation. Stone from stone. Not one left standing. Exactly the way Jesus said it would go.

The stones cried out. And then the stones came down.

**V. THE TEARS**

*\[Read Luke 19:41–44\]*

This is the part that wrecks me every time I read it.

The King who just claimed cosmic authority. The one who told the Pharisees that the very stones of creation answer to Him, not to them. That King — right here, on this road, in front of everyone — breaks down.

And the Greek tells us this is not a quiet moment. The word Luke uses for Jesus’ weeping is klaio. That means to wail. To sob. To convulse with audible grief. This is not the word used in John 11:35, when Jesus shed tears at Lazarus’s tomb. That word is dakryo. That’s a tear running down the cheek. Composed. Quiet.

Klaio is the sound a mother makes at a funeral. It’s the weeping of somebody who sees the catastrophe coming and cannot stop it.

And it echoes an older prophet. Jeremiah used this same language before the Babylonians came. He said, “Oh that my head were waters, and my eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people.”

Jeremiah wept over Jerusalem before Babylon destroyed it. Now Jesus weeps over the same city before Rome destroys it. Same city. Same blindness. Same tears. Hundreds of years apart.

And He says: *“If you, even you, had only recognized on this day the things that make for peace — but now they are hidden from your eyes.”*

And then He uses a word that carries the weight of the entire Old Testament behind it.

*“Because you did not know the time of your visitation.”*

Visitation. In the Greek that’s episkope. And in the Greek Old Testament, this word translates the Hebrew paqad. It means a formal, official, divine inspection. It’s the moment when God Himself steps into human history to see what His people have done with what He gave them. And the result of that inspection goes one of two directions. Deliverance. Or destruction.

When God “visited” Israel in Egypt, He delivered them. Set them free. When God “visited” through the prophets and His people refused to listen, the visit became a verdict.

And Jesus is standing on that road saying: God came to this city. In person. He walked your streets. He healed your sick. He taught in your courts. The inspection happened. The visitation arrived. And you didn’t recognize it. You had the window open. And you let it close.

The tragedy locked inside that word is this: a missed visitation of grace becomes a guaranteed visitation of judgment. Not because God changed His mind. Because the people changed their answer. The same presence that would have saved them now becomes the standard by which they’re measured. Same God. Same arrival. Different response.

And so the King weeps. In public. On the road. With a crowd of worshippers behind Him and a blind city in front of Him.

**VI. TWO PARADES. ONE QUESTION.**

Two processions entered Jerusalem that Passover week.

Pilate came from the west. Horses. Swords. The full weight of empire. His message was simple: We’re in control. Resist and die.

Jesus came from the east. Borrowed donkey. Crowd of nobodies shouting Scripture. His message was different: The war is already over. Your swords are obsolete. The King is here. And He came to save, not to slaughter.

Rome offered peace through violence. Jesus offered peace through sacrifice. Rome said: bow to Caesar or be crushed. Jesus said: I am the one Zechariah wrote about. I am the one the psalms have been waiting for. I am the one whose arrival makes the stones scream and the foundations shake.

And then He wept. Because the city chose the wrong parade.

Jerusalem wanted the peace that comes at the point of a sword. And forty years later, that same sword turned on them.

Here’s what I want to leave you with this morning.

God does not do stealth visits. He announces Himself. He sends prophets. He stages processions. He fulfills promises publicly. Specifically. Loudly enough that creation itself responds. The question has never been whether God shows up. It’s whether we recognize Him when He does.

The Pharisees looked at Jesus and saw a political problem to manage. The Temple establishment saw a threat to their revenue. Pilate — if he noticed the other procession at all — saw a minor disturbance in a border province.

They were all looking at God in the flesh. And they missed it.

And I think every one of us has been there. We’ve all had seasons where God was moving and the form of His arrival didn’t match what we expected. He shows up on a donkey when we wanted a warhorse. He offers peace through a cross when we wanted peace through conquest. He comes through the people and places we’ve written off — and we’re too busy watching the western gate. Watching for power. For spectacle. For the version of God that looks like winning.

Don’t miss the visitation.

That word — *episkope* — comes with an expiration date. “You did not know the *time.”* Kairos. Not time in general. The appointed time. The window. The moment that was built for you.

Windows open. And windows close. And the mercy of God, once refused, does not sit frozen in place forever. It moves. What was offered as salvation becomes the measure by which we’re weighed.

If that feels heavy — good. Sit with it this week. Because this story is not over.

Jesus told the Pharisees: if the people are silent, the stones will cry out.

The people were silenced. By Thursday night the disciples had scattered. By Friday morning the loudest voice in Jerusalem was a mob screaming for execution. The Galilean pilgrims who sang the coronation psalm on Sunday? They were still camped outside the city walls. They had no idea their rabbi was standing trial before dawn. The voices that praised Him were nowhere near the courtroom.

Silence.

So the question going into next week is this:

***Did the stones cry out?***

I’ll see you Easter morning.
