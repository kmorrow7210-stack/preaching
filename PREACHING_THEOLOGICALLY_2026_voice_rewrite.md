# Preaching Theologically

## The Relationship Between Pentecostal Preaching and Spiritual Health in the Local Church

### 2026 Voice Rewrite

Editorial note: This is a non-destructive voice rewrite of the dissertation's main body. It preserves the argument, chapter movement, research design, and major evidence while recasting the prose in the tighter, more direct academic voice of the Revelation 12 paper. It does not replace the original dissertation, does not update the underlying research project, and does not rebuild the citation apparatus. A final academic version would need footnote cleanup, table verification, and source updates before public or institutional use.

## Abstract

This project asks whether theologically sound Pentecostal preaching can contribute to measurable spiritual health in a local congregation. The problem addressed is not merely weak pulpit technique, thin sermon structure, or a loss of religious vocabulary. The deeper concern is theological malnutrition. When the pulpit loses its theological center, the congregation does not simply receive less information. It becomes less formed in Scripture, less anchored in Pentecostal identity, and less equipped for discipleship and mission.

The intervention was conducted at Houston Church of God, a rural Pentecostal congregation in Winston County, Alabama. The congregation received a five-sermon series organized around the Fivefold Gospel: Jesus as Savior, Sanctifier, Spirit Baptizer, Healer, and Soon Coming King. These sermons were not treated as isolated lectures. They were embedded in Pentecostal worship practices, including prayer, testimony, altar response, sacramental action, and congregational participation. The project therefore tested preaching as a theological and ecclesial event rather than as speech alone.

The study used the Spiritual Assessment Inventory and the Spiritual Practices Frequency tool to measure changes in spiritual maturity and spiritual disciplines before and after the intervention. Forty-six valid paired responses were analyzed. The SAI results showed statistically significant positive change in four subscales: awareness, disappointment, impression management, and instability. The SPF results showed significant positive change in several spiritual practices, especially fasting, worship, prayer, solitude, silence, study, secrecy, and submission. The data do not prove that preaching alone produces spiritual maturity, but they do suggest that theologically focused Pentecostal preaching, carried within a Spirit-attentive worship environment, can contribute to congregational spiritual growth.

The central claim of this dissertation is that Pentecostal preaching forms a people. It does not merely deliver doctrinal content or produce emotional response. When preaching is rooted in Scripture, alive to the Spirit, faithful to Pentecostal distinctives, and directed toward the actual life of the congregation, it becomes one of the primary means by which a local church is called into spiritual health.

# Chapter 1: Story and Ministry Context

## Introduction

This project begins in a local church, but it does not begin only there. It begins in a life shaped by loss, calling, Pentecostal experience, pastoral failure, rural ministry, and the long discovery that theology is not an academic luxury for the pulpit. It is part of the pulpit's health.

The argument of this chapter is that the research question emerged from a concrete pastoral location. The question of theological preaching was not imposed on the congregation from outside. It grew out of the life of a pastor who had learned, sometimes painfully, that preaching carries more than inspiration. It carries identity, doctrine, memory, discernment, and the possibility of spiritual formation.

## Call and Formation

My call to ministry was formed in the tension between instability and grace. I was the youngest of six children. My mother died when I was eleven, and the years that followed were marked by grief, family addiction, economic struggle, and the search for a home large enough to hold the weight of that loss. The church became that place before I had language for it. It did not first come to me as an institution or an idea. It came through people, worship, altar moments, and the strange mercy of being found before I knew how to ask to be found.

The decisive Pentecostal moment in my early life came on Super Bowl Sunday in 1996. During a revival service I prayed to receive the baptism in the Holy Spirit. I prayed with a kind of Jacob-like refusal to leave the altar unchanged. As I worshiped, I experienced the Spirit's power and began to pray in tongues. That moment did not end in private religious intensity. It immediately became witness. I told teachers and classmates the next day that this experience was available to them as well.

That pattern would become important for the project. Pentecostal experience, at its best, does not terminate on the self. It becomes testimony, proclamation, and mission. It forms speech.

My first sermon was titled "Turn on the Light," based on Matthew 5:14. When I arrived at the church, the electricity was out. The irony relieved my fear. It also confirmed something that has marked my ministry since: preaching happens in the overlap between preparation and interruption. The preacher prepares, but the moment is never fully controlled by the preacher.

My formal call was confirmed through prayer, prophetic encouragement, and the inward sense that no other work could leave me whole. The language was simple, but the call was not simplistic: I was called before I was born, and there was nothing else in the world that could satisfy the deepest part of me besides ministering for Christ.

## Education, Pastoral Development, and the Recovery of Theology

My early formation combined Pentecostal experience with academic awakening. In Master's Commission I discovered the value of disciplined study. I did not want to become a preacher who operated only at the level of emotion. I wanted the fire of Pentecostal proclamation, but I also wanted the kind of mind that could handle Scripture and doctrine faithfully.

That desire did not mature evenly. My college years at Lee University were fruitful relationally and personally, but I did not fully take advantage of the academic opportunity before me. Ministry forced me to return to that unfinished formation. As a young pastor, I learned that charisma, energy, humor, and good intentions are not enough. Congregations need shepherds who can think theologically in public.

The pastoral path that followed included youth ministry, several lead pastorates, church planting, failure, conflict, growth, adoption, and eventual settlement in Houston, Alabama. Each assignment gave me a different view of the pulpit. At Buhl, I saw the power of humility and pastoral steadiness. In Uniontown, I learned the grace of a congregation willing to let a young pastor grow. At Pleasant Grove, I saw the cost of unresolved conflict. In church planting, I learned that momentum without structure can collapse under its own weight. At Crumley's Chapel, seminary training and maturity helped me pastor conflict with more care than I had known earlier. At Houston, I found a congregation where the questions of Pentecostal identity, theological preaching, and spiritual health could be studied in a real pastoral setting.

The D.Min. program did not create my love for preaching, but it clarified the question beneath it. What exactly does preaching do in the life of a congregation? Does theological preaching form healthier people, or does it merely satisfy the preacher's desire for doctrinal seriousness? This project was an attempt to answer that question in the life of a local church.

## Pastoral Identity and Leadership

My leadership style is relational, energetic, intuitive, and strongly people-oriented. Personality assessments name parts of this pattern: ENFJ, social Seven, extroverted, imaginative, empathetic, quick to connect, and often quick to move. Those labels are useful only insofar as they help name the pastoral strengths and dangers that shape ministry.

The strength is obvious. I love people. I am energized by the room. I notice emotional shifts in a congregation. I enjoy stories, laughter, ministry gatherings, denominational relationships, and the sense of belonging that comes when the church feels like family. In pastoral work, this has often helped me build trust quickly. People know they can approach me. They know I will listen. They know I want them to feel seen.

The danger is equally real. A pastor who is sensitive to people can become conflict-avoidant. A pastor who wants the room to be joyful can resist the hard truth the room needs. A pastor who moves quickly can leave structure behind. A pastor who values empathy can mistake being liked for leading well.

This matters for preaching. Theologically faithful preaching requires more than relational warmth. It requires the courage to speak the truth the congregation needs, not only the truth the congregation will affirm. The preacher must love people enough to be clear. In the Revelation paper, the argument is carried by contrast: dragon and Lamb, coercion and witness, accusation and verdict. This dissertation requires a similar contrast. Pentecostal preaching cannot be reduced to personality, energy, or atmosphere. It must carry truth.

## Houston Church of God

Houston Church of God is a rural congregation in Winston County, Alabama. The town is small, the setting is agricultural, and the church operates with a strong family culture. The congregation has no debt, a history of faithfulness, and a readiness to grow. Its greatest challenge is not hostility to the gospel but the realities that often press on rural churches: aging membership, limited population density, economic constraint, relational complexity, and the difficulty of reaching new people in a community where everyone already knows everyone.

The church's strength is also its challenge. It is a family church. That means belonging can be deep, loyalty can be strong, and care can be immediate. It also means that new people may struggle to find a place, family disagreements can become congregational issues, and the pastor must learn to mediate without becoming the judge of every relational dispute.

The surrounding community includes educators, farmers, factory workers, retirees, and families shaped by rural life. The county's history as the "Free State of Winston" gives the area a distinctive memory of independence, division, and local identity. Its economic and educational profile matters because sermons are never preached to abstractions. They are preached to people with jobs, families, losses, habits, memories, and inherited ways of seeing the world.

The pastoral task in such a setting is not only to pastor a church but to pastor a community. The schools, senior center, civic gatherings, funerals, hospital visits, and crisis moments all become extensions of ministry. This is part of the logic of the project. A pulpit that is detached from the actual life of the congregation cannot form the congregation well. Theological preaching must be local without becoming provincial. It must speak from Scripture into the life of a people in a particular place.

## Ministry Relationships

My ministry has been formed by relationships as much as by books. Ken Andrews modeled a humble, listening, stable form of pastoral leadership. James Passman has been a trusted friend, theological companion, and prayerful presence. Tristen Hartley represents the ministerial development impulse that continues to shape my calling: seeing gifts in younger leaders and helping them walk into their vocation.

These relationships clarify an important assumption beneath the project. Ministry is not an individual performance. It is a shared life. Even preaching, which can appear to be the most individual act of ministry, is formed by mentors, friendships, congregational memory, denominational identity, and the Spirit's work in the whole body.

## Passion and Focus

My central pastoral passion is preaching. The pulpit is not the only ministry of the pastor, but it is one of the most visible and formative. Through preaching, the pastor names reality for the congregation. The sermon can give hope, correct distortion, summon repentance, deepen doctrine, heal wounds, and call a people toward mission.

The concern that drives this study is the weakening of the theological pulpit. In too many places, homiletical energy has outrun hermeneutical depth. Pentecostal preaching has sometimes retained volume, charisma, and emotional force while losing doctrinal thickness. When that happens, the result is not merely shallow sermons. The result is thin discipleship.

This dissertation therefore asks whether theologically sound Pentecostal preaching can contribute to the spiritual health of a congregation. The question is academic, but it is not detached. It is the question of a pastor who loves the pulpit, loves the church, and wants Pentecostal preaching to be more than an inherited style. It must be a Spirit-empowered, Scripture-governed, theologically faithful means of forming the people of God.

# Chapter 2: Personal Theology of Ministry

## Introduction

The argument of this chapter is that preaching belongs within practical theology. The sermon is not merely an act of communication. It is one of the church's recurring practices of theological interpretation, spiritual formation, and communal action. If practical theology asks how Christian truth is embodied in lived practice, then preaching is one of its central sites.

Pentecostal preaching intensifies this claim. It joins Scripture, Spirit, congregation, experience, and response. It is not only a lecture and not only an encounter. It is a theological act performed before God and among the people.

## Practical Theology and the Life of the Church

Don Browning's account of practical theology begins with a religious community facing a crisis in its practices and meanings. Practical theology reflects critically on that situation in conversation with Christian sources and other communities of interpretation in order to guide faithful action. His model matters for this project because the dissertation begins with a pastoral problem: the loss of theological depth in the pulpit and the resulting weakness in discipleship and mission.

Browning's method of critical correlation moves from the life of the community to theological reflection and back into transformed practice. In this project, the movement is similar. The congregation's spiritual health is observed, the theology of preaching is retrieved, and a preaching intervention is designed to test whether doctrinally focused Pentecostal proclamation can contribute to change.

Ray Anderson sharpens the question by pressing practical theology toward Christopraxis. For Anderson, the center is not generic human experience but the continuing ministry of Christ through the Spirit. This is especially important for a Pentecostal project. The sermon is not merely a human attempt to improve religious behavior. It is participation in the ongoing ministry of Christ by the Spirit through the church.

Richard Osmer's four tasks also help frame the project. The descriptive task asks what is happening. The interpretive task asks why it is happening. The normative task asks what ought to be happening. The pragmatic task asks how the church should respond. This dissertation follows that movement: it describes a congregation, interprets the weakness of theological preaching, retrieves normative resources from Scripture and Pentecostal theology, and implements a concrete preaching intervention.

The practical-theological claim is therefore straightforward: preaching is one of the ways the church thinks, acts, and is formed. It belongs to the academy because it can be studied. It belongs to the church because it forms actual people. It belongs to the Spirit because the church cannot make itself alive.

## Contextual Theology and the Local Pulpit

Theology is always preached somewhere. It is spoken by someone, to someone, in a place with habits, wounds, memories, and assumptions. Contextual theology does not mean surrendering doctrine to culture. It means recognizing that faithful theology must be embodied in actual human life.

Stephen Bevans, Paul Matheny, Vladan Perisic, Craig Nessan, and Stephen Garner each press this point from different angles. The gospel is not cultureless because the church is not cultureless. Scripture itself comes to us through concrete histories, languages, communities, and crises. The preacher therefore has to discern both the Word and the world in which that Word is being heard.

This is not an argument for reducing doctrine to context. The danger is real: theology that takes culture seriously can become a theology controlled by culture. The preacher must therefore hold two truths together. First, all preaching is contextual. Second, faithful preaching is normed by Scripture, the character of God, the confession of the church, and the presence of the Spirit.

The rural context of Houston Church of God matters. So does Pentecostal identity. So does the cultural moment in which doctrine is often treated as optional and experience as self-authorizing. The preacher cannot answer those pressures with slogans. The pulpit must form a people who can live faithfully where they are.

## Scripture and the Pentecostal Pulpit

Ray H. Hughes grounds Pentecostal preaching in the Word of God. The church does not produce Scripture; Scripture forms the church. A congregation that ceases to live under the Word loses the center from which its doctrine, worship, witness, and discernment receive shape.

Jesus' own preaching and teaching were centered on Scripture. The early church in Acts was a Word-shaped community. Paul preached Christ crucified not with reliance on rhetorical display but in demonstration of the Spirit and power. The pattern is not anti-intellectual. It is anti-boasting. The preacher studies, prays, thinks, and speaks, but the authority of the sermon rests in God, not in the preacher's capacity to impress.

First Corinthians 2:1-5 becomes a controlling text for this project. Corinth was a city shaped by status, rhetoric, sexuality, wealth, religious diversity, and social fragmentation. The church mirrored many of those tensions. Paul's response was not to compete with Corinth on Corinth's terms. He centered the crucified Christ and trusted the Spirit's power.

The implication for Pentecostal preaching is direct. The sermon must not confuse emotional intensity with spiritual authority. It must not substitute technique for truth. It must not use the Spirit as an excuse for thin exegesis. The Spirit who empowers preaching is the Spirit who inspired Scripture, forms the church, and conforms believers to Christ. Spirit and Word belong together.

## Personal Theology of Preaching

My theology of ministry centers on the conviction that preaching is a primary means of congregational formation. It is not the only means. Pastoral care, discipleship, sacraments, worship, service, and relationships all matter. But preaching has a unique capacity to gather these practices into a theological vision of the Christian life.

The preacher stands at a dangerous intersection. He or she handles Scripture before people whose lives are fragile, complex, and contested. The sermon can heal, but it can also harm. It can deepen faith, but it can also reinforce shallow assumptions. It can summon a congregation into Pentecostal identity, or it can train them to expect only stimulation.

Pentecostal preaching therefore requires a double attentiveness: disciplined attention to Scripture and yielded attention to the Spirit. The preacher must be a student of theology and a person of prayer. The sermon must be intellectually honest and spiritually alive. The goal is not to make the congregation admire the preacher's learning. The goal is to help the congregation inhabit the truth of God.

# Chapter 3: Review of Literature

## Introduction

The literature surrounding preaching, practical theology, Pentecostal spirituality, and church formation reveals a recurring tension. Preaching is both theological and practical, both art and discipline, both proclamation and pastoral care, both text and event. The question is not which side should win. The question is how these elements are held together faithfully.

This chapter argues that Pentecostal preaching must recover the integration of hermeneutics, homiletics, doctrine, worship, and Spirit-empowered encounter. When any one element is isolated from the others, the pulpit becomes distorted.

## Homiletics and Hermeneutics

Clifton Guthrie notes that homiletics has often worked with grand theological claims about preaching while giving less attention to empirical and interdisciplinary analysis of the preaching event. This critique matters for a D.Min. project because the dissertation studies preaching not only as a theological claim but as a measurable congregational practice.

Thomas Long gives the preacher a more theological frame. Preaching is an event of speaking and hearing performed in Christ's name. The preacher joins what God is already doing. This keeps the sermon from becoming either a speech performance or a religious lecture. It is a creaturely act dependent on divine action.

Brad Braxton's three tensions are especially helpful: caught or taught, passion or precision, homiletics or hermeneutics. Pentecostalism has often valued the "caught" side of preaching: the anointing, the moment, the altar, the embodied force of proclamation. But Braxton's point is that the better answer is not either/or. Passion without precision can injure. Precision without passion can become sterile. Hermeneutics without homiletics may never reach the people. Homiletics without hermeneutics may move people without forming them truthfully.

Elizabeth Achtemeier presses the relationship between biblical studies and preaching. The pulpit has historically influenced biblical scholarship, and biblical scholarship has shaped the pulpit. The preacher who ignores historical and literary context risks turning Scripture into allegory, legalism, or denominational projection. That danger is not hypothetical. It is one of the reasons theological preaching matters.

Myles Macbean's Homiletical Window frames preaching through foundation, function, and form. The foundation is biblical exposition. The function may include proclamation, pastoral care, and transformation. The form may be inductive, narrative, philosophical, or otherwise. Abraham Kuruvilla adds that preaching must move from text to audience by discerning the divine thrust of the passage and inviting the congregation into the world projected by the text.

Together, these voices show that preaching cannot be reduced to delivery. It is a theological act requiring interpretation, craft, pastoral awareness, and spiritual dependence.

## Bridging the Pentecostal Gap

Adam White's critique lands close to the heart of this dissertation: Pentecostal preaching can be charismatic in content while anemic in theology. The point is not that Pentecostal sermons lack energy. The problem is that energy can conceal doctrinal weakness. A congregation may be stirred without being formed.

Cheryl Bridges Johns offers a way beyond that weakness. Pentecostal knowing is transrational rather than irrational. It does not reject reason; it refuses to treat reason as the only mode of knowing. Pentecostal education and preaching should therefore embrace the "suddenly" of the Spirit without abandoning the integrity of Scripture. This is precisely the bridge this dissertation seeks: a pulpit that is theologically faithful and spiritually alive.

The old Pentecostal saying that the preacher should pray full and preach empty contains a truth, but it needs theological deepening. Prayer is not a substitute for exegesis. Exegesis is not a substitute for prayer. The Pentecostal preacher must do both. The sermon becomes a God-moment not because the preacher bypassed preparation, but because preparation was offered to the Spirit.

## Preaching in a Postmodern Culture

The dissertation's cultural argument rests on a concern that American congregations are being catechized by forces other than Scripture. Mark Chaves observes changes in belief, affiliation, congregational life, and religious participation. The point for this project is not nostalgia. It is formation. If cultural pressures are already shaping the congregation's imagination, then preaching must give the church a thicker theological world to inhabit.

James K. A. Smith's reading of postmodernism helps refine the concern. Derrida, Foucault, and Lyotard are not merely threats to Christian proclamation. They expose the modern myth that human beings interpret from nowhere. Smith's point that interpretation is part of being human can serve Pentecostal preaching, because Pentecostals already know that people do not encounter Scripture as disembodied rational machines. They encounter it through story, experience, worship, community, and Spirit.

This does not mean that truth dissolves into interpretation. It means that the preacher must help the congregation see how Scripture interprets them. The text is not a static object placed under the preacher's control. It is living and active. In the Pentecostal imagination, the sermon is one site where Spirit, Word, preacher, and people meet.

The postmodern moment therefore presents both danger and opportunity. The danger is doctrinal relativism. The opportunity is a renewed account of Scripture as the living narrative of God's redemptive action in which the church now participates.

## Preaching, Leadership, and Pastoral Care

Preaching also leads. Carson Reed and Truls Akerlund both connect preaching to the narrative identity of a congregation. Leaders form people through the stories they tell and embody. The sermon gives the congregation language for its past, interpretation for its present, and imagination for its future.

This is why preaching cannot be separated from pastoral care. Joseph Jeter and John Neufeld show that preaching can equip people for crisis, identity, vocation, discernment, and biblical understanding. The sermon does not replace pastoral visitation, counseling, discipleship, or prayer. It gives those ministries theological coherence.

In a rural congregation, this leadership function is intensified. The pastor is not merely a Sunday speaker. He is present at funerals, ball games, hospital rooms, community meetings, and family crises. The pulpit gathers all of that pastoral knowledge and places it under Scripture. The preacher speaks to a people he knows, but he must speak a Word that is larger than what he knows.

## Preaching in the History of the Church

The history of preaching gives Pentecostals a broader inheritance. Augustine held together teaching, delight, and persuasion. Ambrose and Cyril connected preaching with worship, baptism, and participation in salvation. Chrysostom preached to form people for a life of rebirth and endurance. Wesley moved beyond inherited structures when necessity required open-air proclamation. Whitefield embodied a preaching marked by urgency, emotion, print, and itinerant reach.

These examples show that preaching has always been more than explanation. It has been formation, exhortation, sacramental accompaniment, public witness, and renewal. Pentecostal preaching stands within this broader stream while also bringing its own emphasis: the immediacy of the Spirit, the present activity of God, the testimony of the saints, and the expectation that the biblical story continues to shape present experience.

Kenneth Archer's account of early Pentecostal Bible reading is crucial here. Pentecostals read Scripture as a presently inspired story. They do not merely recount what God did. They proclaim what God is doing. The danger is that this can become ahistorical and undisciplined. The gift is that it keeps the church from treating Scripture as a closed archive.

## Pentecostal Distinctives

Pentecostal preaching does not occur in isolation from Pentecostal worship. Testimony, hymns, sacraments, footwashing, altar response, healing prayer, Spirit baptism, and communal participation all belong to the world in which the sermon lives.

Testimony is central because Pentecostal truth is often narrated as witness. It is not merely the recounting of personal experience. It is interpretation of God's action in the life of the believer and the community. Testimony says, "God has acted, and this is what that action means."

Hymnody also forms doctrine. Pentecostal worship has often carried theology through song before it was formalized in textbooks. The people learn what they sing. They also learn what they are invited to expect.

Sacramental practices matter because they locate Pentecostal spirituality in embodied action. Communion, baptism, and footwashing remind the congregation that grace is not an abstraction. Water, bread, cup, towel, and altar all become places where the church's theology takes physical shape.

The altar is especially important. For Pentecostals, the altar is not merely the end of a service. It is a space of response, encounter, repentance, healing, calling, and transformation. The sermon moves toward that space because preaching seeks more than comprehension. It seeks faithful response.

Pentecostal preaching itself emerges from the Full Gospel. The movement proclaimed Jesus as Savior, Sanctifier, Healer, Spirit Baptizer, and Coming King. This message was restorationist and eschatological. It announced that God was restoring apostolic faith and power for the sake of witness in the last days. The result was a preaching marked by urgency, holiness, mission, and expectation.

The dissertation's concern is that Pentecostal churches may retain the style of this inheritance while losing its theological content. If that happens, Pentecostal preaching becomes an echo of itself. The remedy is not less Pentecostalism. It is deeper Pentecostal theology.

## Synthesis

The literature points toward a single claim: preaching forms a people when it holds together Scripture, theology, Spirit, congregation, and response. The preacher is not merely a communicator. The preacher is a practical theologian, a pastoral leader, an interpreter of Scripture, and a witness to the Spirit's ongoing work.

The Pentecostal pulpit is therefore healthiest when it refuses false choices. It does not choose passion over precision, Spirit over Scripture, experience over doctrine, or altar response over theological formation. It holds these together because Pentecostal preaching, at its best, is a Spirit-empowered theological event through which the church is summoned into the life of God.

# Chapter 4: Project Design

## Introduction

The project was designed to test a pastoral question in a real congregation: Will theologically sound Pentecostal preaching result in increased spiritual health in a local church?

The design assumes that preaching is one important factor within a larger worship ecology. The intervention therefore did not isolate sermons from the practices that normally surround Pentecostal proclamation. The sermons were preached in worship services where prayer, testimony, altar response, sacraments, and attentiveness to the Spirit were also present.

## Narrative of the Problem

Abraham Kuruvilla defines biblical preaching as the communication of a scriptural pericope's thrust, discerned through theological exegesis, applied to a concrete body of believers, so that they may be conformed to Christ for God's glory in the power of the Spirit. That definition gives this project its homiletical center.

The problem is that many congregations are not being formed by preaching that carries this weight. Christian language may remain while Christian understanding thins. Churchgoers may attend worship while lacking basic theological clarity. Preachers may respond to cultural pressure by replacing theological proclamation with self-help, moral advice, pragmatic church growth strategy, or inspirational speech.

The concern is not that marriage, finances, emotional health, or practical life are unimportant. They matter deeply. The concern is that when these become detached from theological formation, the pulpit trains the congregation to seek improvement without discipleship. The goal of Christian preaching is not self-improvement but participation in the life of God.

The variables identified in the project are:

1. Weak hermeneutic: preaching becomes less theological because the preacher does not handle Scripture with sufficient depth.
2. Cultural influence: the church absorbs cultural assumptions that reshape its expectations of preaching and discipleship.
3. Loss of Pentecostal distinctive: Pentecostal churches drift from the doctrines and practices that formed their identity.

These variables are not isolated. A weak hermeneutic makes the church more vulnerable to cultural influence. Cultural influence makes Pentecostal distinctives appear optional or embarrassing. The loss of those distinctives further weakens the pulpit. The cycle becomes self-reinforcing.

## Intervention Concept

The intervention used a five-sermon series organized around the Fivefold Gospel: Jesus as Savior, Sanctifier, Spirit Baptizer, Healer, and Soon Coming King. This structure was chosen because it offers a concise and historically recognizable entry point into Pentecostal theology.

The project did not claim that the Fivefold Gospel exhausts Pentecostal theology. It does not. But for a local congregational intervention, it provided a clear theological spine. Each sermon presented a major Pentecostal claim about Christ and the Spirit and invited the congregation to respond not only intellectually but spiritually and communally.

The worship services also made room for Pentecostal practices that embody doctrine: altar prayer, testimony, sacramental action, Spirit-led response, and discipleship conversation. This was important because Pentecostal preaching is not fully intelligible apart from Pentecostal worship. The sermon is central, but it is not alone.

## Research Question and Hypothesis

The refined research question was:

Will implementing theologically sound Pentecostal preaching result in increased spiritual health in a local congregation?

The hypothesis was:

Theologically sound Pentecostal preaching will result in an increase of spiritual health in a local congregation.

The null hypothesis was that the data might be unreliable because participants may not be fully honest about their biblical, spiritual, or devotional deficiencies.

The original direction of the project leaned more heavily toward the development of the Pentecostal pastor. As the research matured, the focus shifted toward congregational formation. That shift strengthened the project. The pulpit does not exist to display the preacher's development. It exists to form the people of God.

## Instruments and Materials

The project used the Spiritual Assessment Inventory, developed by Todd W. Hall and Keith J. Edwards, to measure dimensions of spiritual maturity. The SAI includes subscales such as awareness, realistic acceptance, disappointment, grandiosity, instability, and impression management. These categories are useful because they connect spiritual maturity with relational and emotional dynamics in a person's relationship with God.

The project also used the Spiritual Practices Frequency measure, drawing on Dallas Willard's account of spiritual disciplines. The SPF assessed practices such as solitude, silence, fasting, frugality, secrecy, sacrifice, study, worship, celebration, service, fellowship, confession, submission, and prayer.

Participants completed a pre-test before the sermon series and a post-test after the series. The same questions were used so that change could be measured. Codes were used to match individual responses while protecting participant anonymity.

The intervention group was Houston Church of God. A control group was considered in the original design, but the final reporting centers on the Houston congregation's pre- and post-intervention results.

## Research Design Summary

The project design moved in four stages:

1. Establish a theological and pastoral problem: the relationship between theological preaching and spiritual health.
2. Pre-test the congregation using SAI and SPF instruments.
3. Preach a five-week Pentecostal theological series within a Spirit-attentive worship environment.
4. Post-test the congregation and compare the results.

The design is modest. It does not claim universal proof. It does not isolate preaching from all other factors in congregational life. It does, however, provide a concrete case study for observing how a congregation may be affected by a focused season of theological Pentecostal proclamation.

# Chapter 5: Implementation and Assessment

## Introduction

The project was implemented as a congregational case study at Houston Church of God. The study began with pastoral observation and ended with theological reflection on the data. The goal was not to reduce spiritual health to numbers. The goal was to ask whether measurable change accompanied a focused season of theological Pentecostal preaching.

## Implementation

The congregation averaged between sixty and seventy-five adults in the main worship gathering. The intervention took place over five weeks in March and April. The original dissertation records the year as 2001; that date should be verified because the surrounding project timeline indicates a later implementation date.

The sermons focused on major tenets of Pentecostal theology. Each worship service made space for more than sermon delivery. The services included prayer, worship, testimony, sacramental action, altar response, and openness to the Spirit's ministry. This matters because Pentecostal preaching is not merely the transfer of theological content. It is a Spirit-attentive event within the gathered church.

Sixty pre-tests and fifty-four post-tests were distributed. Forty-six paired responses were deemed valid. Two were invalidated because the participants did not attend all services. Three did not have corresponding codes. Three were illegible. The valid participants included twenty-nine females and seventeen males. Thirty-three were over age fifty-five, and thirteen were between eighteen and fifty-four. All valid participants were Caucasian.

These demographics limit the scope of the findings. The project does not speak for Pentecostal congregations broadly. It speaks from one rural, largely older, racially homogeneous congregation. That limitation should not be hidden. It is part of the pastoral truth of the study.

The strongest result was awareness. This is pastorally significant. If theological Pentecostal preaching helps congregants become more aware of God's presence and work, then the sermons did more than inform. They heightened spiritual perception.

The changes in disappointment and instability are also important. Theologically rich preaching may help worshipers name their relationship with God more truthfully. It may not remove struggle, but it can reframe struggle inside a more stable theological imagination. The change in impression management may also indicate that participants became more honest about their spiritual life or less invested in appearing spiritually whole.

The strongest changes clustered around practices directly tied to attention to God: fasting, worship, prayer, solitude, silence, and study. This pattern fits the theological logic of the intervention. The sermons were not only about Pentecostal identity as a set of labels. They invited the congregation into practices that make spiritual attentiveness possible.

Age-related analysis suggested that participants between eighteen and fifty-four showed broader significant change across disciplines than those over fifty-five, though older participants did show significant change in prayer and worship. This may indicate that older congregants began the study with more established spiritual practices, leaving less room for measurable movement. It may also suggest that younger and middle-aged adults are more responsive to structured short-term interventions. Further research would be needed before drawing a firm conclusion.

## Assessment

The data suggests that the worship experiences accomplished the intended purpose. The SAI showed meaningful movement in spiritual awareness and relational stability before God. The SPF showed increased engagement in several spiritual disciplines. Together, these results support the hypothesis that theologically sound Pentecostal preaching can contribute to congregational spiritual health.

The results should be interpreted carefully. A five-week sermon series cannot establish a comprehensive model of spiritual formation. Self-reporting has limits. The sample size was small. The congregation was demographically narrow. The project did not isolate preaching from other elements of worship or pastoral life.

Even so, that limitation may be a theological strength. Pentecostal preaching is never isolated in actual ministry. It is heard inside worship, relationship, memory, prayer, expectation, and response. The project measured preaching as the congregation actually experiences it: not as a transcript, but as a Spirit-attentive ecclesial event.

# Chapter 6: Conclusions

## Summary

This project found that a focused season of theologically sound Pentecostal preaching corresponded with measurable positive change in the spiritual health and practices of a local congregation. The SAI showed significant improvement in awareness, disappointment, impression management, and instability. The SPF showed significant improvement in several disciplines, especially fasting, worship, prayer, solitude, silence, and study.

The findings do not prove that preaching alone causes spiritual growth. They do suggest that preaching matters. More precisely, they suggest that theological Pentecostal preaching, practiced within a worship environment open to the Spirit, can help form a congregation toward deeper awareness of God and more faithful spiritual practice.

The central claim of the dissertation is therefore sustained: the Pentecostal pulpit is not peripheral to congregational health. It is one of the places where a church receives its theological imagination.

## Implications for Wesleyan-Pentecostal Ministry

Wesleyan-Pentecostal preaching must hold together hermeneutical faithfulness and homiletical fire. Lee Roy Martin's account of prophetic preaching, Ray H. Hughes's insistence on the dynamic of the Spirit, John Gordy's call to tarrying, and Vincent Leoh's account of Pentecostal proclamation all press toward the same conclusion: Pentecostal preaching is more than the delivery of religious information.

But that "more" must be named carefully. Pentecostal preaching is not more because it is louder, more emotional, or more theatrical. It is more because it seeks to be a Spirit-empowered encounter with the living God through the faithful proclamation of Scripture. The danger of Pentecostal preaching is emotional manipulation. The gift of Pentecostal preaching is Spirit-formed witness.

This study suggests that Pentecostal churches need a renewed commitment to preaching the doctrines and practices that make Pentecostal identity intelligible: salvation, sanctification, Spirit baptism, healing, eschatological hope, testimony, sacrament, altar response, mission, and holiness. These are not museum pieces. They are formative practices and claims that shape a people.

## Suggestions for Further Research

Future research should use a larger sample size and include congregations from multiple settings. A multi-church study could compare rural, suburban, urban, racially diverse, and multiethnic Pentecostal congregations. The project would also benefit from a longer intervention period and a delayed post-test to assess whether spiritual growth continued after the sermon series ended.

Future work should also refine the instruments used to measure spiritual health. The SAI and SPF were useful, but they do not fully capture Pentecostal categories such as testimony, altar response, Spirit baptism, discernment, healing prayer, communal worship, and mission. A Pentecostal spiritual health instrument could be developed with attention to both spiritual practices and Pentecostal distinctives.

The relationship between preaching and other practices should also be studied more closely. The sermon may be central, but spiritual formation is reinforced through small groups, pastoral care, worship, sacraments, testimony, and household discipleship. A fuller project would study how these practices work together.

## Personal Impact

This study changed the way I understand my preaching. I entered the project already convinced that preaching mattered. I leave it more convinced that theological preaching matters. The difference is important.

I am less interested now in using energy to stir the congregation and more interested in communicating truth so clearly that conviction, worship, and response have somewhere to land. Style does not have to disappear. Pentecostal preaching can remain passionate, embodied, and responsive. But the passion must be governed by truth.

The project also exposed my need to be a more faithful student of Scripture and Pentecostal doctrine. If the congregation is formed by the pulpit, then the preacher's shallowness becomes a congregational liability. The preacher cannot lead people into a theological world he does not inhabit.

## Final Claim

The Pentecostal church does not need a choice between Spirit and theology. It needs preaching where the two are held together. The Spirit does not weaken doctrine; the Spirit makes doctrine live. Theology does not quench Pentecostal fire; theology gives the fire a hearth.

Theologically sound Pentecostal preaching can form spiritually healthier congregations because it gives the church more than inspiration. It gives the church a world: Scripture as living witness, Christ as center, the Spirit as active presence, the congregation as a people being formed, and the pulpit as a place where the church learns again who it is.
