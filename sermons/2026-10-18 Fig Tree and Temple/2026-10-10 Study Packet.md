## 1. The text

### What happens (plain summary)
- **11:11** Jesus enters Jerusalem, goes into the temple, looks around at everything, and leaves for Bethany because it is late.
- **11:12-14** Next morning he is hungry, sees a fig tree in leaf, finds nothing but leaves, and speaks over it: "May no one ever eat fruit from you again." The disciples hear.

- **11:20-21** Next morning the tree is withered from the roots. Peter points it out.
- **11:22-25** Jesus answers with faith, a mountain thrown into the sea, prayer, and forgiveness.

## 2. How Mark built it: the sandwich

Mark often starts one story, cuts to a second, then comes back to finish the first, so each half explains the other. Scholars call it intercalation, or a "Markan sandwich" [C]. The standard study is James R. Edwards, "Markan Sandwiches: The Significance of Interpolations in Markan Narratives," *Novum Testamentum* 31 (1989): 193-216.

Kyle has preached one already: in "A Tale of Three Tables," Mark wraps John's death (6:14-29) inside the sending of the Twelve (6:7-13, 30). This one is laid out the same way:

| Layer | Text | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Top bread | 11:12-14 | A tree in leaf, no fruit. A word spoken over it. "His disciples heard him." |
| Filling | 11:15-19 | A temple full of activity, no prayer. A word spoken over it. The leaders "heard this." |
| Bottom bread | 11:20-25 | The tree withered from the roots. Jesus teaches faith, prayer and forgiveness. |

Note the matching verb [T]: the disciples "heard" (*ēkouon*, 11:14) and the leaders "heard" (*ēkousan*, 11:18). Both groups hear a word of judgment. One group asks about it the next morning. The other plans a murder.

**What the sandwich means [C]:** the fig tree is an acted parable about the temple. Jesus is not losing his temper at a tree. He is showing the disciples, in a picture they can't forget, what he is about to say in the temple courts. That is why Mark adds "it was not the season for figs" (11:13): the line tells the reader this is a sign, not a gardening problem.

**A note on the word "cleansing" [C].** Mark never calls it a cleansing. A cleansing fixes a building so it can keep going. Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7, where God tells the people to go look at Shiloh, the earlier sanctuary he let fall (Jer 7:12, 14). In Mark the action points toward destruction: "Not one stone here will be left on another" (13:2), and the veil torn in two (15:38). E. P. Sanders made this case at length in *Jesus and Judaism* (Fortress, 1985), chapter 1, and N. T. Wright follows a similar line in *Jesus and the Victory of God* (Fortress, 1996) [V: page numbers for both].

## 3. Key Greek terms, in plain English

1. ***periblepsamenos panta*, "he looked around at everything" (11:11).** *Periblepō* means to look all the way around, to take stock. In Mark it is almost always Jesus' searching look: at the Pharisees "in anger" (3:5), at the crowd he calls his family (3:34), for the woman who touched him (5:32), and at the disciples before "how hard it is for the rich" (10:23) [T]. Here he does the same to the temple, then leaves without a word. The inspection comes before the verdict.

2. ***ekballō*, "to drive out, throw out" (11:15).** This is Mark's exorcism verb (1:34, 39; 3:15, 22-23; 6:13; 7:26; 9:18, 28, 38) and the verb for the Spirit driving Jesus into the wilderness (1:12) [T]. Kyle used it in "The Stronger Man." Here Jesus "began driving out" (*ērxato ekballein*) the sellers. Mark uses his deliverance word inside God's own house [T on vocabulary; I on reading the temple as occupied ground]. One chapter later the same verb turns around: the tenants "took him and killed him, and threw him out (*exebalon*) of the vineyard" (12:8) [T]. Jesus drives out the market. The keepers of the house drive out the Son.

3. ***oikos proseuchēs*, "a house of prayer" (11:17).** From Isaiah 56:7. Mark alone keeps the words "for all nations" (*pasin tois ethnesin*); Matthew 21:13 and Luke 19:46 stop at "a house of prayer" [T]. Isaiah 56 is about foreigners and eunuchs, people kept outside, whom God promises to bring to his holy mountain. Mark wants that line heard.

4. ***spēlaion lēstōn*, "a den of robbers" (11:17).** From Jeremiah 7:11. A den is not where robbers rob. It is the hideout they run back to after the robbing, where they feel safe [C]. Jeremiah's charge was that people sinned all week, then came to the temple saying "The temple of the LORD" three times (Jer 7:4) as if the building made them safe. *Lēstēs* shows up only three times in Mark, and it builds [T]: the temple as a den of *lēstai* (11:17); "Am I leading a rebellion (*lēstēn*), that you have come out with swords and clubs?" (14:48); and two *lēstai* crucified on his right and left (15:27). The man who called out the den is executed between two of the men the word describes. (Barabbas is called a rebel in 15:7, *stasiastēs*, not *lēstēs*, so don't put that word on him from Mark.)

5. ***exērammenēn ek rhizōn*, "withered from the roots" (11:20).** Not a few dead leaves. Dried up from the bottom. See the Hosea link in section 5.

6. ***echete pistin theou*, "Have faith in God" (11:22).** Literally "have faith of God." Grammarians debate whether it means faith *in* God or faith that comes *from* God; most translations, the NIV included, take it as faith in God [C]. Either way, the object is God, not the faith itself, and not the mountain.

7. ***hotan stēkete proseuchomenoi*, "when you stand praying" (11:25).** Standing was the normal posture of Jewish prayer (1 Sam 1:26; Luke 18:11, 13) [C]. Jesus has just shut down business in the house of prayer. Then he tells a band of Galileans on a roadside what to do "when you stand praying." The house of prayer is being handed to people.

## 4. Historical background

**The temple courts.** Mark says *hieron* (the whole temple complex), not *naos* (the sanctuary building) [T]. The trading most likely happened in the large outer court, the one area where non-Jews were allowed [C]. Josephus describes a stone barrier with warning signs that kept foreigners out of the inner courts (*Jewish War* 5.193-194; *Antiquities* 15.417) [T in Josephus]. One of those signs was found in 1871 by Charles Clermont-Ganneau and is in the Istanbul Archaeology Museum; a second, broken copy was found in 1935 [C]. In Greek it says, roughly: no foreigner may enter inside the barrier around the temple; whoever is caught will have himself to blame for the death that follows [V: use a published translation for exact wording].

So the one place "all nations" could come to pray was the place filled with tables [I, resting on C]. Jesus quotes "for all nations" standing in that courtyard.

**Money changers.** Every Jewish man paid a half-shekel temple tax (Exod 30:13). The Mishnah says the tables for collecting it were set up in the temple on the 25th of Adar, a few weeks before Passover (m. Sheqalim 1:3) [V: confirm wording]. Temple payments were made in Tyrian silver, which held a reliable silver content [C; V source before citing, often given as t. Ketubbot 13:3].

**Doves.** The offering the law allows for people who cannot afford a lamb (Lev 5:7; 12:8). Mary and Joseph brought doves (Luke 2:24) [T]. Jesus overturns the benches of the dove sellers, the market that served the poor. Whatever was wrong with the system, it was wrong at the point where the poorest people met it [I].

**Carrying merchandise through (11:16).** Only Mark has this detail [T]. The Mishnah forbids using the Temple Mount as a shortcut: no entering "with his staff, his sandal, his money belt... nor may he make it a short cut" (m. Berakhot 9:5) [V: exact wording]. Some people treated God's house as a cut-through. Jesus stands in the path and says no.

**Complaints from inside Israel.** Jesus is not an outsider attacking Judaism. He stands in line with Jeremiah, and other Jews of his day made similar complaints about the high-priestly families [C]:
- The Dead Sea Scrolls' Habakkuk commentary speaks of "the last priests of Jerusalem, who amass wealth and loot" (1QpHab 9:4-5) [V: line numbers and wording].
- The Talmud preserves a lament against the high-priestly houses ("Woe is me because of the house of Boethus...") for their violence and greed (b. Pesahim 57a) [V: wording; late source, so use it as a memory, not as proof for AD 30].
- Mark aims at the leadership: "the chief priests and the teachers of the law" want him dead, while "the whole crowd was amazed" (11:18) [T].

**Fig trees around Passover.** Passover falls in spring. Fig trees leaf in spring, and ripe figs come in early summer [C]. Some writers say a fig tree in leaf should carry small early knobs that people would eat [V: botanically debated, don't build on it]. Mark's own explanation is enough: "it was not the season for figs." The tree promises with its leaves what it can't give.

**"This mountain."** Jesus says this on the road from Bethany, which runs over the Mount of Olives with the Temple Mount in full view across the Kidron Valley [C]. Two readings:
- **The Temple Mount** [I, held by a number of commentators; V before naming any]. In context, the mountain is the one Jesus has just judged. Faith in God will move what the system says can't be moved.
- **The Mount of Olives** [I]. Zechariah 14:4 says that on the day of the Lord the Mount of Olives will split in two.

Either way, it is a particular mountain, "*this* mountain," not mountains in general.

## 5. Whole-Bible patterns and connections

**A. Malachi 3 comes true in Mark 11 [C on Mark 1:2 using Malachi 3:1; I on reading 11:11 as its fulfillment].** Mark opens his book with "I will send my messenger ahead of you" (1:2), wording drawn from Exodus 23:20 and Malachi 3:1 [C]. Malachi goes on: "Then suddenly the Lord you are seeking will come to his temple... But who can endure the day of his coming?" (Mal 3:1-2). Mark spends ten chapters on the road. In 11:11 the Lord finally comes to his temple, and he looks around at everything. The question Malachi left hanging, "who can endure the day of his coming?", is answered the next morning. (Kyle's Wednesday plan for Sept 2 already worked through Mark 1:2, so this ties the Wednesday study to Sunday.)

**B. Hosea 9: fig, house, roots, fruit [T on the shared words; I on whether Mark intends the echo].** Checked in the Greek Old Testament (LXX, Rahlfs). Hosea 9:10-16 and Mark 11 share four things:

| Hosea 9 (LXX) | Mark 11 |
|---|---|
| "like early fruit on the fig tree (*sykē*)" (9:10) | the fig tree (*sykē*) (11:13) |
| "I will drive them out (*ekbalō*) of my house (*oikou mou*)" (9:15) | "began driving out (*ekballein*)"; "My house (*oikos mou*)" (11:15, 17) |
| "its roots (*rhizas*) are dried up (*exēranthē*)" (9:16) | "withered (*exērammenēn*) from the roots (*rhizōn*)" (11:20) |
| "it will no longer bear fruit (*karpon ouketi*)" (9:16) | "May no one ever (*mēketi*) eat fruit (*karpon*)" (11:14) |

KJV of Hosea 9:15-16: "for the wickedness of their doings I will drive them out of mine house... their root is dried up, they shall bear no fruit." Writers on the fig tree background list Hosea 9 among the fig-judgment texts (William R. Telford, *The Barren Temple and the Withered Tree*, JSNTSup 1, Sheffield: JSOT Press, 1980, is the standard study) [V: confirm Telford's treatment of Hosea 9]. Laying the four words side by side in the Greek is the part worth showing the church. This is the strongest "recovered knowledge" link in the passage.

**C. Jeremiah 7 and 8.** Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11. The sermon around it is about trusting the building ("The temple of the LORD," 7:4) and about Shiloh (7:12, 14). A chapter later, Jeremiah 8:13: "there shall be no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree, and the leaf shall fade" (KJV) [T]. Leaves and no figs: Jeremiah has already put the fig tree and the temple in the same breath.

**D. Mark's only two tables [T on the word; I on the link].** The Greek word for table, *trapeza*, appears just twice in Mark: the Gentile woman in Tyre, "even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs" (7:28), and the money changers' tables in the temple (11:15). In "A Tale of Three Tables," a Gentile woman begged for crumbs under a table. In Mark 11, the court where Gentiles were allowed to pray is full of tables, and Jesus turns them over and says "for all nations." That is a callback the congregation can hear because Kyle has already preached the first half.

**E. Made with hands, not made with hands [T].** At the trial, false witnesses say: "I will destroy this temple made with hands (*cheiropoiēton*) and in three days will build another, not made with hands (*acheiropoiēton*)" (14:58). Stephen, in the sermon Kyle preached from Acts 6-7, says: "the Most High does not live in houses made by human hands (*cheiropoiētois*)" (Acts 7:48). Same word [T]. The early church understood Jesus' temple action as the start of a new house.

**F. The Pentecostal turn [T on the texts; I on drawing the line].** The Spirit falls in Acts 2 not in the sanctuary, but on disciples gathered in one place (Acts 2:1) who had been devoting themselves to prayer (Acts 1:14), and "it filled all the house where they were sitting" (Acts 2:2, KJV). Paul: "Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you?" (1 Cor 3:16, KJV). Guardrail: the first believers still went to the temple "at the hour of prayer" (Acts 3:1). This is not contempt for the building; it is God moving his presence into a people.

**G. Divine Council and the cosmic mountain [C on Heiser's framework; I on applying it here].** Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (Lexham, 2015), treats Eden, Sinai and Zion as the "cosmic mountain," the place where God's council meets and heaven touches earth [V: chapter numbers]. The temple on Mount Zion was that meeting place. When Jesus stands on one mountain, looks across at the other, and says "this mountain" can be thrown into the sea, the sea being the old chaos-water that only God rules (Mark 4:39; Kyle's "Genesis 1 chaos-sea" thread), he is saying where God meets people is about to move [I]. Preach this as a reading.

**H. Series callbacks [T].**
- Mark 9:23: "Everything is possible for one who believes." Mark 11:23: "it will be done for them."
- Mark 9:29: "This kind can come out only by prayer." Mark 11:17: "a house of prayer." The disciples failed because they did not pray; the temple failed because it did not pray. Then Jesus teaches the disciples to pray (11:24-25).
- Bartimaeus (10:51-52) asked for one thing, by faith, and got it. Eleven verses later Jesus teaches the disciples to ask in faith.

## 6. Main idea

**One sentence (working):** Jesus came to God's house looking for fruit and found leaves, so he moved the house of prayer into a people who trust God and forgive.

**Three options for the pulpit line (Kyle chooses):**
- **A.** "Jesus is not impressed by leaves. He is looking for fruit."
- **B.** "When God's house quits praying, Jesus doesn't renovate it. He moves into people who will."
- **C.** "He looked around at everything. He's still looking."

**The contrast that frames the sermon:** a den or a house. A den is where people hide after they sin. A house of prayer is where people come to meet God, all nations included.

**Refrain:** "My house will be called a house of prayer." (Say it after each movement; it lands at the altar.)

**Title options:**
1. Nothing but Leaves
2. A House of Prayer
3. He Looked Around at Everything
4. Say to This Mountain

**Movement 4. Withered from the Roots (24:00-28:00, about 450 words). 11:20-21.**
- Peter: "Rabbi, look!"
- Hosea 9 laid out: fig, drive out of my house, roots dried up, no more fruit. Four for four. "I love finding patterns in Scripture."
- Leaves die from the roots up. You can keep the leaves on for a while.
- Forward look: "Not one stone" (13:2); the veil torn (15:38). Gently, no fear-based framing.

**Movement 5. Say to This Mountain (28:00-33:00, about 550 words). 11:22-24.**
- "Have faith in God." The object is God.

- Divine Council: the cosmic mountain, the sea only God rules. The meeting place between heaven and earth is moving.
- Series link: Mark 9:23 and 9:29. The disciples who couldn't drive out a spirit without prayer are being taught to pray mountains down.

**Movement 6. When You Stand Praying (33:00-37:00, about 450 words). 11:25 with Acts 2:2; 7:48; 1 Cor 3:16.**

- "Not made with hands" (14:58; Acts 7:48). At Pentecost the Spirit filled "all the house where they were sitting."
- The turn: "What I'm saying this morning is..." (the big idea, plainly).

**Close and altar (37:00-42:00, about 450 words).** "If you'll stand with me, I'm closing."
- Bookend the hook (see section 8).
- Altar groups: (1) the one whose life is all leaves, busy and dry at the root; (2) the one holding something against someone (11:25); (3) the one facing a mountain that won't move.
- Response shaped by the text: the whole church stands praying, a house of prayer, and forgives by name.
- Closing prayer reusing the images: leaves, roots, the house, the mountain.

**Protected lines for the ending** (from the voice profile's warning that endings get squeezed): the refrain, the bookend twist, and the three altar groups.

## 8. Illustration options

All options. One opener plus one or two supporting pieces at most.

1. **The warning stone (opener, true story; recommended).** In 1871, the French archaeologist Charles Clermont-Ganneau found a limestone block in Jerusalem with a Greek inscription from Herod's temple. It warned foreigners not to go past the barrier into the inner courts on pain of death. Josephus mentions these signs (*War* 5.193-194). It sits today in the Istanbul Archaeology Museum [C; V: confirm the find details and a published translation]. **Bookend:** the sign said whoever crosses the line dies. At the cross, Jesus did die, and the veil tore (15:38), and the first human in Mark to confess "Son of God" was a Roman, a foreigner (15:39). The stone that said "Keep out" lost the argument. Ephesians 2:14 ("the middle wall of partition," KJV) is often linked to this barrier [I; V before saying Paul had it in mind].
2. **The shortcut (supporting, local).** People cutting through a church parking lot to beat the light, or through a store to get to the other side. The Mishnah had to forbid people using the Temple Mount as a cut-through (m. Berakhot 9:5) [V].
3. **A tree in the yard (supporting, personal; Kyle supplies).** A fruit tree on a family place that leafed out every spring and never gave anything. Only use it if it's Kyle's own story.
4. **Lookout Mountain (supporting, local).** "If I stood in this pulpit and said 'this mountain,' every one of you would turn and look toward Lookout Mountain. That's what the disciples did."

## 9. Application

1. **He still looks around at everything.** Before Jesus judged, he looked. He looks at churches and at lives the same way. The question is not how much activity there is. It is whether there is fruit.
2. **Who can't get to God because of our tables?** The nations' court was full of business. Ask: who in Fort Payne would walk in and find no room to pray? Apply to ourselves first, as Straight Creek, before pointing at anyone else.
3. **Don't hide in the house.** A den is where you go to feel safe after you sin. Church attendance can become a hideout instead of a place to repent (Jer 7:4).
4. **Pray to the God who moves mountains, not to the mountain.** Faith in God, not faith in faith. Honest about prayers that didn't go the way we asked.
5. **Forgive while you stand.** Jesus ties the power of prayer to forgiveness in the same breath (11:25). Unforgiveness keeps the leaves and kills the root.
6. **Pentecostal identity.** A praying, Spirit-filled people is God's house now (1 Cor 3:16). "I'm so thankful I'm part of a praying church" is not a slogan here; it's the point of the text.

**Guardrails for the pulpit:**
- Don't make Jesus anti-Jewish or the temple the villain. He quotes Israel's own prophets, inside Israel's own house, and the crowd loves him.
- Don't turn 11:23-24 into name-it-and-claim-it. The verse sits between "Have faith in God" and "forgive."
- No fear-based eschatology around AD 70. Mark 13 can be mentioned as the direction the story goes, not dwelt on.
