# Singing at Midnight (Acts 16)

## Introduction

What do you do when obeying God's clear call leads you straight into suffering instead of success?

They thought they knew where they were going. The gospel needed to spread through Asia. That was the plan — clear, strategic, Spirit-led.

Until God blocked every road.

Acts 16 records one of the strangest seasons in Paul's missionary career. The Holy Spirit forbids Asia. The Spirit of Jesus blocks Bithynia. It's not that the doors were locked — God was actively closing them. Imagine the confusion. You're trying to obey, and heaven keeps saying "not that way."

Then comes the breakthrough: a vision of a Macedonian man begging for help. At last — clear guidance. They board ships for Philippi and within days, they have their first convert. Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth. Wealthy, connected, a natural hub for the new church. Her household believes in Jesus, and her home becomes headquarters for the church in Philippi. The pattern holds: follow God's leading. Just watch Him work.

This is how we want the Christian life to function. God guides, we obey, fruit appears.

Then a slave girl starts following them through the streets, and everything falls apart.

### The Uncomfortable Truth-Telling (Acts 16:16–17)

For days, she followed them. A slave girl, owned by businessmen, possessed by a spirit of divination — a python spirit, as the Greek text literally says. Her owners made good money off her fortune-telling.

And she wouldn't stop shouting: "These men are servants of the Most High God, who proclaim to you the way of salvation!"

Here's what makes this so disturbing: *she's telling the truth.* Every word is accurate. But it's invasive truth. Unwanted truth. Truth weaponized by darkness.

Paul puts up with it for days. Then **Acts 16:18 says something revealing: "Paul, having become greatly annoyed..."**

Not "moved with compassion." Not "led by the Spirit." Annoyed. He's had enough.

### The Right Action, Wrong Outcome (Acts 16:18–24)

Paul finally turns and commands the spirit, "I command you in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her."

And it does. Immediately. The girl is free.

But freedom has a price tag. She was valuable property. Now she's worthless to her owners. **Acts 16:19 is devastating in its simplicity: "When her owners saw that their hope of profit was gone..."**

Within hours, Paul and Silas are dragged before magistrates. Falsely accused. Stripped. Beaten with rods. Thrown into the inner prison, feet locked in stocks.

They followed God's call to Macedonia. They preached the gospel. They set a captive free. And now they're bleeding in a dungeon.

Where is God in this? Why does obedience look like this? They did everything right. The Macedonian man begged them to come. Lydia believed. The slave girl is delivered. And their reward is a prison cell.

## Historical Context: What Actually Happened

### The Beating (Acts 16:22–23)

**Greek detail:** The text uses *rhabdizō* (ῥαβδίζω) - "to beat with rods," a specific Roman punishment distinct from scourging.

The charge was vague - disturbing the peace, promoting illegal customs. But the punishment was precise. Roman magistrates ordered them stripped publicly in the marketplace. No trial. No defense. Just raw, imperial power on display.

The rods were bundles of birch or elm — like the ones Roman officers carried to show they had authority to punish. When **Acts 16:23** says they received **"many blows,"** the Greek points to a drawn-out, methodical beating — not just a few hits, but a brutal thrashing.

Paul would later write to the Corinthians: **"Three times I was beaten with rods" (2 Cor 11:25).** This was one of those times. He remembered it. The shame of public nakedness. The crack of wood on flesh. The crowd watching two Jewish troublemakers get what they deserved.

By the time the lictors finished, Paul and Silas weren't walking. They were carried.

### The Prison (Acts 16:23–24)

**Greek detail:** *Eis phylakēn* (εἰς φυλακήν) - "into prison," but v. 24 specifies *eis tēn esōteran phylakēn* - "into the inner prison."

**What "inner prison" meant:**

The jailer received clear orders: guard them securely. So he took them to the *eso desmoterion* - the innermost cell. Not the outer holding area where you could see daylight or hear the street. The deep hole.

Archaeological evidence from Roman prisons suggests what this looked like. No windows. No light except what filtered down through corridors. The air thick with the stench of unwashed bodies, human waste, and death. Some prisoners didn't survive long enough to see trial.

This is where they put the dangerous ones. The escape risks. The ones they wanted to forget.

Paul and Silas, beaten raw, dragged bleeding through the darkness to the deepest part of the dungeon.

**Historical notes:**

* Roman prisons weren't for punishment - they were for holding until trial or execution
* The inner prison (*ergastulum* in Latin sources) was often underground
* Prisoners were the jailer's personal liability - if they escaped, he'd be executed (see Acts 16:27)
* Light deprivation, isolation, and filth were standard, not exceptional

### The Stocks (Acts 16:24)

**Greek detail:** *Tous podas ēsphalisanto autōn eis to xulon* (τοὺς πόδας ἠσφαλίσαντο αὐτῶν εἰς τὸ ξύλον) - "they fastened their feet securely in the wood/stocks."

**What this device was:**

Then came the final degradation. The jailer locked their feet in the *xulon* - the stocks.

These weren't handcuffs. Picture two heavy wooden beams, the upper one hinged, with holes for ankles. But here's what made it torture: the holes could be set at different widths. Lock them close together and the prisoner sits uncomfortably. Spread them wide apart and you force the legs into an unnatural, excruciating position.

**Acts 16:24** says the jailer **"fastened their feet securely."** The Greek word is *asphalizō* (ἀσφαλίζω) - to make safe, to secure beyond doubt. He wasn't taking chances. He likely spread those ankle holes as wide as they'd go.

Now add the context: Paul and Silas have open wounds from the rods. Their backs are raw meat. And they're forced to sit - can't stand, can't lie down comfortably, can't shift position to relieve the pain - with their legs wrenched apart in the darkness.

Midnight is still hours away.

**Historical notes:**

* The *xulon* (ξύλον) is literally "wood" - could refer to stocks, pillory, or even a cross
* In this context, clearly foot restraints
* Roman and Greek sources describe stocks that could be adjusted for comfort or agony
* The "secure" detail suggests maximum restraint for maximum security
* Some commentators suggest this was standard procedure for high-risk prisoners

**The gap between expectation and reality:**

This is not how the story is supposed to go.

God sent the vision. They obeyed. They crossed the sea. Lydia believed. The church was planted. The demon was cast out. The slave girl was free.

And now God's missionaries are chained in the darkness, bodies broken, blood drying on their backs, legs locked in torture devices.

Where is the God who sent the vision? Where is Jesus whose name cast out the demon? Where is the Spirit who opened Lydia's heart?

**Application bullets (weave these in subtly):**

* The dark night when God feels absent
* The suffering that seems to have no purpose
* The isolation when you've done right and been punished for it
* The physical/emotional toll of following Jesus

**The UGH feeling (not question):** "This is what it costs. This is what obedience often feels like."

## The Clue to Resolution

**"They were already free before the chains fell off."**

The clue to resolution isn't the earthquake. It's what happens at midnight when Paul and Silas do something that makes no earthly sense: they worship.

### The Turning Point: Acts 16:25

**Greek detail:** *Kata de to mesonuktion Paulos kai Silas proseuchomenoi hymnoun ton Theon* (κατὰ δὲ τὸ μεσονύκτιον Παῦλος καὶ Σιλᾶς προσευχόμενοι ὕμνουν τὸν θεόν)

**Literal translation:** "But about midnight, Paul and Silas, praying, were singing hymns to God."

**What Luke is showing us:**

About midnight — hours into the darkness, bodies still screaming from the beating, legs still wrenched in the stocks — Paul and Silas began to pray.

*And then they sang.*

Not quietly. Not under their breath. **Acts 16:25** says **"the prisoners were listening to them."** In a Roman dungeon, sound carries. Their voices echoed off stone walls, traveled through corridors, reached other cells.

They sang loud enough to be heard.

### The Last Supper Echo: When Jesus Sang Into Suffering

This isn't the first time someone sang on the way to suffering.

The night Jesus was betrayed, He gathered His disciples in an upper room. He broke bread, knowing His body would be broken. He poured wine, knowing His blood would be poured out. He told them plainly: "One of you will betray me." He warned Peter: "Before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times."

Jesus knew what was coming. Gethsemane. Arrest. Beating. Cross.

And then **Mark 14:26 records this detail: "When they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives."**

They sang. And then they walked straight into the darkness.

**Historical note:** The hymn was almost certainly the Hallel (Psalms 113–118), traditionally sung at Passover. **Psalm 118 ends with "Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good; his steadfast love endures forever."** Jesus sang those words knowing the cross was hours away.

### The AHA: What the Singing Reveals

The question isn't "How could they sing?" It's "Why did they sing?"

In the ancient world, midnight was the hour of darkness. It was when demons were believed to be most active, when the powers of the air held sway.

And into that midnight hour, Paul and Silas unleashed worship.

This wasn't passive. This wasn't just emotional therapy or trying to feel better. This was spiritual warfare. Every note was defiance. Every word of praise was a declaration: "You haven't won. Our God reigns. These chains don't have the last word."

The enemy thought he'd silenced them. He'd beaten them, imprisoned them, isolated them in the dark. But instead of silence, he got a concert. Instead of despair, he got doxology.

Jesus did the same thing. Satan thought the upper room was a defeat — Jesus was hours from the cross, His disciples about to scatter. But Jesus sang. He praised His Father and walked into the darkness with worship on His lips.

The clue to resolution: Worship breaks the power of the enemy not by changing your circumstances, but by changing your allegiance. When you worship in chains, you're declaring that your captivity is temporary but your King is eternal.

## Experiencing the Gospel

### The Earthquake: Heaven's Response to Worship (Acts 16:26)

**Greek detail:** *Aphno de seismos egeneto megas* (ἄφνω δὲ σεισμὸς ἐγένετο μέγας) - "Suddenly there came a great earthquake"

**The timing matters:** *Aphno* (ἄφνω) means "suddenly, unexpectedly." Not gradually. Not eventually. Suddenly.

They're still singing when the ground begins to move.

At first, it's a rumble — deep, subterranean, the kind you feel in your chest before you hear it. Then the whole foundation of the prison shakes. Stone grinding against stone. Dust falling from ceiling beams. The sound of metal shrieking as iron doors twist in their frames.

**Acts 16:26** records it with almost clinical precision: **"Immediately all the doors were opened, and everyone's bonds were unfastened."**

Every. Single. One.

Not just Paul and Silas. Every prisoner. Every chain. Every lock. Every door.

The powers of darkness tried to silence worship with a beating and chains. Heaven answered with an earthquake that undid every restraint in the building.

This is what happens when worship becomes warfare. The enemy chains you in the midnight hour, and God Himself shakes the foundations.

### The First Salvation: A Man Who Knows Roman Law (Acts 16:27)

**Historical context:** Under Roman law, if a prisoner escaped, the guard responsible was executed — often in the same manner the prisoner would have been executed.

The jailer wakes to chaos. Doors open. Chains on the ground. An earthquake has just demolished his security system, and Roman law has one response to escaped prisoners: the guard dies in their place.

**Acts 16:27** doesn't waste words: **"He drew his sword and was about to kill himself, supposing that the prisoners had escaped."**

This isn't panic. This is cold calculation. Better a quick death by his own hand than execution, disgrace, and his family's ruin. He's already decided. The sword is drawn. The blade is at his throat.

He's one second from death when a voice cuts through the darkness:

**"Do not harm yourself, for we are all here!"**

Paul. Still in the prison. Still in the inner cell. Free to run, but he stayed.

The man who was supposed to guard Paul just got saved by him.

### The Question That Changes Everything (Acts 16:29–30)

**Greek detail:** *Ti me dei poiein hina sōthō?* (τί με δεῖ ποιεῖν ἵνα σωθῶ?) - "What must I do that I might be saved?"

**The word *sōthō* (σωθῶ) carries both physical and spiritual salvation — and the jailer means BOTH.**

The jailer calls for lights. Torches blaze in the darkness. And he sees it: every door open, every chain broken, every prisoner still sitting where they were chained.

No one ran. Not one.

He rushes in trembling and falls down before Paul and Silas. This Roman officer, this representative of imperial power, collapses before two beaten Jewish prisoners. He brings them out of the inner cell — out of the place of death — and asks the question that will define his eternity:

**"Sirs, what must I do to be saved?"**

Hours ago, he locked them in the stocks. Now he's on his knees calling them "sirs" and begging for salvation.

Hours ago, he was the one with power. Now he knows: the men in chains had something he didn't. Freedom. Peace. A God who answers worship with earthquakes.

He wants what they have. Not just survival — salvation. Not just to avoid death — to find life.

### The Second Salvation: The Gospel Simply Stated (Acts 16:31–33)

**Greek detail:** *Pisteuon epi ton Kyrion Iēsoun kai sōthēsē* (πίστευσον ἐπὶ τὸν κύριον Ἰησοῦν καὶ σωθήσῃ) - "Believe on the Lord Jesus and you will be saved"

Paul's answer is stunning in its simplicity: **"Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved — you and your household."**

That's it. No penance for locking them in the stocks. No payment for the beating. No ritual to perform or ladder to climb. Just: Believe.

And right there, in the ruins of the prison, Paul and Silas speak the word of the Lord to him and to all who are in his house.

What happens next is pure grace:

**Acts 16:33 — "And he took them that same hour of the night and washed their wounds; and he was baptized at once, he and all his family."**

The same hour. Still midnight. Still dark. But the jailer can't wait.

The man who hours before had locked their feet in stocks now washes their wounds. The man who threw them in the inner prison now opens his home. The man who served Rome now serves Christ.

And he's baptized — him, his wife, his children, his whole household — in the middle of the night because salvation can't wait until morning.

**The double washing:**

* The jailer washes their wounds (mercy, service, repentance made visible)
* They baptize him (grace, new birth, identity transformation)

**Theological depth:** The "you and your household" echoes the covenant promise to Abraham (Gen 17:7). Paul brings the Philippian jailer into the covenant family the same night he heard the gospel. This is the fulfillment of the Macedonian call — not just Lydia's household, but now a Roman jailer's household.

### The Feast: Experiencing Joy (Acts 16:34)

**Greek detail:** *Ēgalliasato panoikei pepisteukōs tō Theō* (ἠγαλλιάσατο πανοικεὶ πεπιστευκὼς τῷ θεῷ) - "He rejoiced with his whole household, having believed in God"

**The verb *agalliaō* (ἀγαλλιάω) means to exult, to rejoice exceedingly, to be overwhelmingly glad.**

Then he brought them up into his house and set food before them.

Think about this: hours ago, Paul and Silas were starving, bleeding, chained in darkness. Now they're at a table. Breaking bread. Being served by the man who imprisoned them.

And Acts 16:34 records what happens in that moment: "He rejoiced along with his entire household that he had believed in God."

Not quiet gratitude. Not somber thanksgiving. *Rejoicing.* The Greek word is *agalliaō* — it's exuberant, overflowing, can't-contain-it joy.

A Roman jailer, a man trained in violence and control, sitting at his own table in the middle of the night with his whole family, laughing and weeping and celebrating because he just met Jesus.

This is the gospel. This is what worship-as-warfare wins.

The enemy meant to destroy Paul and Silas with suffering. God used their suffering to save an entire household. The chains that were supposed to silence them became the doorway to salvation. The prison that was supposed to break them became a sanctuary.

And somewhere in that midnight feast, I imagine Paul and Silas looking at each other across the table, wounds still fresh, and thinking: *This is why we came to Macedonia. This is what the vision meant. Not just Lydia. Not just a church plant. This man. This family. This moment.*

The beating was worth it. The chains were worth it. The darkness was worth it.

Because the jailer and his whole house now belong to Jesus.

## Stage 5: Anticipating the Consequences

### The Mosaic Church: Gospel for All People (Acts 16:11–40)

Before the sun rises on that prison, let's zoom out and see what God was building in Philippi.

When Paul and Silas arrived in that city, there wasn't even a synagogue — just a small prayer gathering by the river. But look at the church that emerged from one week of ministry:

* **Lydia** - A wealthy businesswoman from Thyatira, a dealer in expensive purple cloth. A God-fearer who worshiped Israel's God but wasn't Jewish. She and her entire household believed and were baptized. Her home became the church's headquarters.
* **The slave girl** - Nameless in Luke's account, powerless, owned by men who exploited her. Possessed by a demon, used for profit. Jesus set her free. Her deliverance cost Paul and Silas everything, but she walked away with her soul intact.
* **The Philippian jailer** - A Roman civil servant, trained in violence, the face of imperial power. He and his entire household — wife, children, servants — baptized at midnight, feasting with joy because they met Jesus.

Three households. Three completely different worlds. One gospel.

**Theological connection:**

Do you remember the promise God made to Abraham in Genesis 17? "I will make you exceedingly fruitful, and I will make you into nations, and kings shall come from you... and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed."

All families. All nations. All people.

That's what's happening in a Philippian prison at midnight. The covenant God made with Abraham thousands of years ago is being fulfilled in real time. Asian merchant. Greek slave. Roman soldier. Jew and Gentile. Slave and free. Male and female. All one in Christ Jesus.

This is what worship-as-warfare wins. Not just individual salvation, but the breaking down of every wall that divides humanity. The gospel Paul preached — and sang in chains — doesn't recognize economic class, ethnic barrier, or social hierarchy.

The kingdom of God looks like Lydia's living room the next morning: purple cloth merchants sitting next to former slaves sitting next to Roman officials, breaking bread together because they all belong to Jesus now.

### The Morning After: Paul's Strategic Protection (Acts 16:35–40)

Morning comes. The magistrates, perhaps embarrassed by their hasty judgment, send officers to the jail with a simple message: "Let those men go."

Problem solved, right? Paul and Silas walk away quietly, everyone saves face, the incident is forgotten.

But Paul doesn't accept the quiet exit.

**The citizenship reveal:**

**Acts 16:37** records Paul's response, and you can almost hear the steel in his voice:

**"They beat us publicly, uncondemned, men who are Roman citizens, and threw us into prison. And do they now throw us out secretly? No! Let them come themselves and take us out."**

Suddenly, everything changes.

Under Roman law — specifically the *Lex Valeria* and *Lex Porcia* — it was a criminal offense to beat or imprison a Roman citizen without trial. Not a minor infraction. A career-ending, potentially life-threatening violation.

The magistrates who hours ago wielded unchecked power are now terrified. **Acts 16:38** is almost comical: **"The police reported these words to the magistrates, and they were afraid when they heard that they were Roman citizens."**

**Why this matters strategically:**

Paul could have left quietly. He'd been vindicated by the earthquake. The jailer was saved. The mission was accomplished. Why push back?

Because Paul wasn't just thinking about himself. He was thinking about the church he was leaving behind.

That church was meeting in Lydia's home — a house church made up of a foreign businesswoman, possibly a former slave girl with a dark past, and a Roman jailer who just betrayed his own system. Every single one of them was vulnerable. Every single one could be targeted the moment Paul left town.

So Paul forced the magistrates to do something they desperately didn't want to do: publicly apologize. Come to the prison personally. Escort Paul and Silas out with honor.

It was a master stroke. By securing a public vindication, Paul established something crucial: the Christians in Philippi were under his legal protection. Touch them, and you risk Paul returning with his Roman citizenship to prosecute you. The magistrates, now terrified of the legal mess they'd created, would think twice before harassing Lydia's household or investigating the jailer's conversion.

**Historical weight:** Paul "astutely uses his earthly citizenship to protect the community of his heavenly citizenship." The legal structures of Rome became a shield for the kingdom of God.

## The Implications: Your Midnight Has Purpose

### 1. God Uses Your Suffering for Kingdom Purposes You Can't See Yet

Paul and Silas didn't know, when the rods were cracking against their backs, that their beating would become the legal foundation for a church plant.

They didn't know, when their feet were locked in the stocks, that a jailer's entire household would be saved because they stayed instead of running.

They didn't know, when they sang in the darkness, that their worship would create a legal shield for vulnerable believers.

They just worshiped. They just stayed faithful. And God was working a strategy they couldn't see.

Here's what that means for you: The midnight you're in right now — the suffering you can't make sense of, the injustice that feels pointless, the darkness that seems to serve no purpose — God is using it.

You may not see it for months. Maybe years. But your faithfulness in the midnight hour is securing something for the kingdom that you can't yet comprehend.

**Concrete applications:**

* The job loss that feels random might be positioning you for a kingdom assignment
* The relationship that ended in betrayal might be teaching you the worship that will save someone else
* The financial hardship that makes no sense might be stripping away the false securities that keep you from full surrender
* The illness that derailed your plans might be the platform from which God will display His power

### 2. Worship in Suffering Becomes Witness to the Watching World

The prisoners were listening. The jailer was listening. The whole city would eventually hear the story.

When you worship in your chains, people notice. Not just other Christians — *everyone* notices. Because it's supernatural. It makes no earthly sense to praise God when life is falling apart, which is exactly why it's such powerful evidence that God is real.

Your boss sees how you respond when you're passed over for promotion. Your kids see how you handle disappointment. Your neighbors see how you walk through crisis. The watching world doesn't need more arguments for Christianity — they need to see Christians who have something they don't. Peace in chaos. Joy in suffering. Freedom in chains.

That slave girl was set free because Paul wouldn't tolerate demons speaking truth. That jailer was saved because Paul and Silas sang instead of cursing their circumstances. That church in Philippi flourished because Paul used his suffering strategically instead of wasting it on bitterness.

What if your suffering is actually your greatest evangelistic opportunity?

### 3. The Gospel Creates Communities That Shouldn't Exist

In the Roman world, Lydia, the slave girl, and the jailer would never have sat at the same table. Never. Economic class, social status, ethnic identity — every wall separated them.

But the gospel doesn't recognize those walls. It tears them down.

The consequence of embracing midnight worship is that you become part of a community that makes no earthly sense. The church isn't a gathering of people who look alike, think alike, vote alike, or earn alike. It's a gathering of people who were all freed by the same King.

If you embrace the gospel Paul preached, your life will start looking like Lydia's living room — diverse, unexpected, uncomfortable, and absolutely beautiful. You'll find yourself in relationship with people you'd never choose on your own. And that's the point. The kingdom is bigger than your preferences.

### 4. Strategic Faithfulness Sometimes Requires Bold Action

Paul didn't leave Philippi quietly because he knew silence would endanger the church. Sometimes faithfulness means speaking up. Insisting on justice. Using whatever platform or protection God has given you to shield the vulnerable.

You have influence. You have a voice. You have citizenship — earthly and heavenly. The question is: Will you use it to protect your comfort, or will you use it strategically for the kingdom?

Maybe God is calling you to:

* Speak truth in a work environment where everyone else is silent
* Use your resources to create space for ministry that doesn't benefit you directly
* Stand publicly for someone who can't defend themselves
* Leverage your reputation to advocate for the marginalized

Paul used his midnight suffering and his morning citizenship to secure a future for the church. What has God given you that could be leveraged for kingdom purposes?

## The Invitation: Your Midnight, His Strategy

Paul and Silas left Philippi. Acts 16:40 says they "visited Lydia and when they had seen the brothers, they encouraged them and departed."

They left behind a church — meeting in Lydia's house, protected by Paul's bold stand, diverse beyond imagination, and built on the foundation of midnight worship.

Years later, Paul would write to that same church: "I thank my God every time I remember you, constantly praying with joy in every one of my prayers for all of you, because of your sharing in the gospel from the first day until now" (Philippians 1:3–5).

From the first day. From the day Paul and Silas sang in chains. From the day the earthquake shook the prison. From the day the jailer and his household were baptized at midnight.

That church became one of Paul's greatest joys, one of his most generous supporters, one of his most faithful partners in the gospel.

All because two men worshiped in the darkness.

So here's the question for you: What midnight are you in right now? What chains are testing your faith? What darkness is making you question whether obedience is worth it?

And here's the promise: If you'll worship in your midnight — if you'll let your suffering become warfare instead of waste — God will use it for purposes you can't yet see.

He'll save someone you haven't met yet. He'll establish something you won't be around to finish. He'll build a community that shouldn't exist but does because you were faithful in the dark.

The question isn't whether God is working. He is. The question is whether you'll worship while you wait to see it.

## Altar Call / Response Moment

* **For those in their midnight:** If you're in a season of suffering right now — if you've obeyed God and it's cost you everything — I want to pray for you. That He'll give you the grace to sing at midnight. Because your worship might be the doorway to someone else's salvation.
* **For those who heard the singing:** Maybe you're like the jailer. You've been listening to Christians worship through suffering and you don't understand it. You want what they have. Today, you can ask the same question he asked: 'What must I do to be saved?' And the answer is the same: Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved — you and your household.
* **For those who need to use their influence:** Maybe God is asking you to leverage your platform, your resources, your voice for kingdom purposes. Not for your comfort, but for the protection and flourishing of His church. If you sense God asking you to be strategic with what He's given you, I want to pray for courage and wisdom.
