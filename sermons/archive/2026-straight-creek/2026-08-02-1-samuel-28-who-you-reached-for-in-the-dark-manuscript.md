# Who You Reached For in the Dark

**Text:** 1 Samuel 28:3–20; 1 Chronicles 10:13–14  
**Date:** Sunday, August 2, 2026  
**Occasion:** Straight Creek Church of God, Sunday morning  
**Target:** 30–35 minutes delivered  
**Status:** Full working manuscript

## Controlling Question

Who do you reach for when fear is loud and God will not answer on your terms?

## Big Idea

Saul did not lose his way because God had never spoken. He lost his way because he kept refusing the word God had already given, until fear drove him to seek from the dead what he had rejected from the living God.

## Anchor Phrase

> The problem was not that God had never spoken. The problem was that Saul had never listened.

## Cold Open: A King at the Edge of the World

[PULPIT]

Right now Christopher Nolan has put *The Odyssey* back on a screen about six stories tall, because apparently regular-sized Greeks were no longer enough for us.

And if you have seen it, you know there is a scene where Odysseus sails to the edge of the world because he is out of options and needs a word.

He reaches a land where the sun does not shine. He takes a sword and digs a trench in the ground. He pours out an offering. He sacrifices over that trench until dark blood runs into it.

Then the dead begin to gather.

Soldiers. Old men. Young women. People Odysseus loved. People he failed. All of them pressing toward the blood because the blood lets them speak.

But Odysseus is not there for all of them.

He is waiting for one dead prophet.

Tiresias.

He has crossed the sea and walked to the border of death because he cannot find the way home, and he believes a dead man can tell him what to do next.

Nolan puts James Remar in the role. The scene is strange. Mud. Blood. Dead faces rising in the dark. A living king leaning over a pit, asking a dead prophet for directions.

That picture is ancient Greek poetry. Homer's *Odyssey* took shape in the eighth century before Christ, out of an older oral tradition.

But several centuries before that poem took its familiar form, across the sea in Israel, the same haunted shape appears in real royal history.

A king.

Out of options.

Walking through the dark.

Looking for a dead prophet to tell him what to do.

Only this king had the prophet standing in front of him for years.

And would not listen.

The problem was not that God had never spoken.

The problem was that Saul had never listened.

## Movement One: He Wants a New Word Without Obeying the Old One

[PULPIT]

You are seated now. Let me walk you back through what we just read.

First Samuel 28 opens with four words that should stop us before we get anywhere near Endor.

**Now Samuel was dead.**

That matters because Samuel has been the voice Saul kept pushing away.

Samuel told him to wait at Gilgal. Saul would not wait.

Samuel told him what God required concerning Amalek. Saul spared what God told him to destroy, then built himself a monument and called partial obedience success.

Samuel looked him in the face and said, “To obey is better than sacrifice.”

Saul heard it.

He just did not bow to it.

Now the Philistines gather at Shunem. Saul gathers Israel at Gilboa. Across the valley he sees an army large enough to finish him, and verse 5 says his heart greatly trembles.

He is afraid.

Not nervous. Not concerned. Shaking.

So Saul inquires of the Lord.

By dreams.

By Urim, the priestly means of seeking an answer from God.

By prophets.

And the Lord does not answer.

Church, be careful with this scene. The silence here is not the silence every hurting believer feels in a hard season. Job suffers without an explanation. David cries in the Psalms and asks how long. Jesus prays Psalm 22 from the cross.

Saul’s silence sits inside Saul’s own history.

God spoke to him plainly for years.

Saul kept asking for a word he could use while refusing the word that could rule him.

[PULPIT, CROWN JEWEL]

Come here. I want to get this exactly right.

**Saul does not want God. Saul wants information from God.**

He does not ask, “How do I come home?”

He asks, “How do I survive tomorrow?”

He does not ask, “What must change in me?”

He asks, “What will happen to me?”

He is not grieving the loss of God’s presence. He is panicking over the loss of God’s protection.

And there is a difference.

The road from Gilboa to Endor takes him dangerously close to the Philistine position at Shunem. He risks enemy patrols to reach a practice God has forbidden.

Fear will make a man travel a long way for an answer that repentance could have met him with at home.

## Movement Two: The King Who Went Down

[PULPIT]

There is a direction built into this whole story.

Down.

Saul leaves his camp at night.

He goes to a woman who calls the dead up from beneath the earth.

When Samuel appears, Saul stoops with his face to the ground.

When Samuel speaks judgment, Saul falls full length on the earth.

The king Israel could see spends his final night going lower and lower.

He takes off the clothes of a king.

He walks in the dark.

He bows to the ground.

He falls with no strength left in him.

This is not one bad decision that ruins an otherwise faithful life. This is the visible end of a direction Saul has traveled for years.

Every time God said obey, Saul negotiated.

Every time the truth cost him something, Saul protected himself.

Every time the kingdom slipped, Saul gripped harder.

Until finally the man who would not bow to the living God bows in a forbidden room waiting for a dead prophet.

[DO NOT INTERRUPT: ARC BEGINS]

The woman is afraid at first.

She knows Saul’s law. She thinks this may be a trap. “You know what Saul has done. Why are you laying a snare for my life?”

Then Saul swears to protect her.

And he swears by the Lord.

“As the Lord liveth, there shall no punishment happen to thee.”

He invokes the name of God to guarantee protection while breaking the command of God.

That is how far he has come apart.

The woman asks, “Whom shall I bring up?”

Saul says, “Bring me up Samuel.”

Then the text says she sees Samuel and cries out with a loud voice.

Now people have argued for centuries about exactly what happened in that room. Was this truly Samuel? Was it a demonic deception? Was the woman expecting a familiar trick and terrified when God overruled the whole thing?

The narrator keeps calling him Samuel. He speaks the same word Samuel spoke while he was alive. His prophecy comes true. I believe God sovereignly allowed the real Samuel to appear, not because the medium controlled a prophet of God, but because God would not let Saul control the message.

Saul breaks every boundary to get a different word.

God sends the same prophet with the same word.

Samuel asks, “Why have you disturbed me?”

Saul pours it out. “I am sore distressed. The Philistines are making war against me. God has departed from me. He will not answer me. So I called you to tell me what to do.”

And Samuel says, in effect, “Why are you asking me? The Lord has done what he told you he would do.”

The kingdom is torn from your hand.

It is given to David.

Tomorrow you and your sons will be dead.

Saul wanted directions.

He received a verdict.

He wanted a way around God’s word.

He met God’s word waiting for him in the dark.

Then Saul falls.

All of him.

The man who once stood head and shoulders above everybody in Israel is stretched out helpless on the dirt floor.

No crown.

No strength.

No answer that will save him.

The woman kills the fat calf. Kneads flour. Bakes bread. Saul eats his last meal in the house of the medium.

Then verse 25 ends with a sentence that sounds like a funeral procession.

**“Then they rose up, and went away that night.”**

He walks back into the dark.

By sundown the next day, Saul and three of his sons are dead on Mount Gilboa.

The Philistines cut off his head. Strip his armor. Put that armor in the house of Ashtaroth. Fasten his body to the wall of Beth-shan.

The king who spent his life trying to keep his throne ends as a trophy in the house of the enemy.

Scripture will not let that scene close without naming what actually happened.

> So Saul died for his transgression which he committed against the LORD, even against the word of the LORD, which he kept not, and also for asking counsel of one that had a familiar spirit, to enquire of it; And enquired not of the LORD: therefore he slew him, and turned the kingdom unto David the son of Jesse.
> — 1 Chronicles 10:13–14

Notice what the Chronicler names as the cause of death.

Not merely that the Philistines were stronger.

Not merely that fear got loud.

Saul died because he kept the word of the Lord unkept, and because he asked counsel of a familiar spirit instead of asking the Lord.

First Samuel told you Saul went through motions that night at Endor. First Chronicles tells you what those motions were worth. Dreams he ignored the meaning of, a priestly Urim he never intended to obey, prophets he ran past — that is not enquiring of the Lord. That is auditioning God for a verdict Saul had already decided to reject if he did not like it.

## Movement Three: Two Men in the Dark

[PULPIT]

Last week we left David standing in the ashes of Ziklag.

His city is burned.

His family is gone.

His men are talking about stoning him.

David is in the dark too.

Same region of the story.

Same pressure from the Philistines.

Same feeling that everything is falling apart.

But David reaches in the opposite direction.

David strengthens himself in the Lord his God.

He calls Abiathar the priest.

“Bring me the ephod.”

David is not the priest in that scene. Abiathar is. David does not invent a new door to heaven. He seeks the Lord through the means the Lord has given.

And God says, “Pursue. You will overtake. You will recover all.”

David reaches up for the ephod.

Saul reaches down for the dead.

One man receives a road toward recovery.

One man hears that the road has ended.

One house is about to rise.

One house falls before the next sunset.

[PULPIT, CROWN JEWEL]

But this was never merely David against Saul.

And years earlier it was never merely David against Goliath.

Goliath came out cursing David by his gods.

The giant represented the Philistine god-world standing against the living God of Israel.

And God answered all that armor, all that blasphemy, and all that spiritual arrogance with a shepherd boy and a stone.

Saul watched it happen.

He watched David run into the valley in the name of the Lord.

He watched the giant fall.

He watched the Philistines run.

But on Saul’s final night, he walks toward the forbidden spiritual world God had warned Israel to reject.

The battle on Gilboa has not started, but Saul has already decided where he will seek his help.

Before the Philistine archers wound his body, fear has already taken his allegiance hostage.

Fear reveals who we trust to tell us what comes next.

Saul's fear did not create a new loyalty. It exposed the one he had been building for years.

## Typological Reveal: The King We Could See

[PULPIT]

Israel had asked for a king they could see.

God was their King, but they wanted a man who could stand at the front of an army. A king with shoulders. A king with a sword. A king who looked like every other nation’s answer to danger.

So they chose Saul.

Saul looked like a king.

David looked like a shepherd.

Saul stood taller than everybody else.

David was the youngest son left with the sheep.

But the king who looked right could not carry the weight of the kingdom.

God chose David. A man after God’s own heart. A man who ran toward the giant. A man who reached for the Lord in the ashes.

And yet David is not the end of the story either.

Because even the best earthly king fails.

The man after God’s own heart will one day take another man’s wife and send her husband to the front line to die.

Earthly kings send men to the front to save themselves.

The true King goes to the front to save his men.

David sends Uriah out so David can live with what he has done.

Jesus goes out so sinners like David and Uriah can live.

David is a real king. A chosen king. A better king than Saul.

But David is still a shadow.

The house of David exists to carry us to Jesus Christ.

Israel said, “Give us a king we can see.”

And God said, “One day you will.”

Not a king who merely looks the part.

God with us.

The Word made flesh.

A King who can be touched.

A King who steps into the valley himself.

A King who does not send somebody else to face our giant.

A King who lays down his own life.

Saul reaches into the dark for a dead man.

We reach up for a King who walked out of the grave alive.

That is the gospel.

Jesus does not shout directions to us from the other side of death.

He enters death.

He takes our sin into the grave.

He breaks its claim.

And on the third day, the Son of David walks out with the keys of death and hell in his hand.

Saul asks, “What will happen to me tomorrow?”

Jesus says, “Because I live, you shall live also.”

Saul wants information about the future.

Jesus gives himself as our future.

## Altar Call: You Had the Word the Whole Time

[PULPIT]

If you want to play something, I’m closing.

The hardest line in this whole story is not that Saul could not hear God.

It is that Saul had heard him so many times before.

Samuel had stood in front of him.

The word had been clear.

Mercy had given Saul warning after warning.

The Chronicler did not write "Saul died because God abandoned him." He wrote that Saul died because he kept not the word of the Lord.

That is a hard sentence to sit under. It means the emergency most of us are praying our way out of right now may not be God's silence. It may be an old instruction we have been managing around instead of obeying.

But he kept putting off obedience until crisis came, and then he wanted in one desperate night what he had rejected over a lifetime.

Do not wait for the funeral to wish you had answered the voice that kept calling from the front door.

Do not wait until the army gathers to open the Bible that has been sitting in the house the whole time.

Do not wait until fear has you walking through the dark to ask whether God has already spoken.

Maybe that is you this morning.

You have had access to the word of God your whole life.

You know the songs. You know the language. You know when to stand. You know when to say Amen.

But knowing where the voice comes from is not the same as obeying what the voice says.

This altar is open. Come.

If fear has you reaching for anything that promises control, come.

If you have been hunting for a new word because you do not like the old one, come.

If God has already told you to forgive, come.

If God has already told you to leave it, come.

If God has already told you to confess it, come.

If God has already told you to come home, come.

If you have opened doors to the occult, divination, mediums, horoscopes, or any spiritual power that is not the Holy Spirit, do not hide in shame. Come renounce it in the name of Jesus. The living King is stronger than the thing you invited into the room.

And if you have never surrendered your life to Jesus, come.

You do not need a dead prophet to tell you what comes next.

The risen King is calling you now.

Come while mercy is speaking.

Come while the door is open.

Come while the King is near.

Saul reached into the dark for a dead man.

Reach up this morning for the King who walked out of the grave alive.

This altar is open.

Come.

## Research and Honesty Notes for Kyle

- Homer’s *Odyssey* is commonly dated to the late eighth century BC, with older oral material behind it. The events narrated in 1 Samuel belong roughly to the eleventh century BC. Say “same ancient Mediterranean world” or “centuries apart in the same ancient world,” not “same century.”
- In *Odyssey* 11, Odysseus digs a cubit-sized pit, pours libations, sacrifices a ram and ewe, keeps the dead away from the blood until Tiresias drinks, then receives the prophecy.
- Nolan’s 2026 film includes the underworld consultation with Tiresias, played by James Remar. Reviews confirm the scene. Do not claim a reviewer used the exact word “disturbing” unless quoting a verified review.
- The *Odyssey* scene and 1 Samuel 28 are a strong ritual and narrative parallel. There is no evidence of literary borrowing in either direction.
- The Hebrew **’ob** is difficult. “Medium,” “ghost/spirit,” ritual specialist, and “pit” have all been argued. Do not tell the room that 1 Samuel explicitly says the woman used a dug ghost pit.
- The claim that she learned the rite from Philistines, or that Saul used Philistine/Aegean “technology,” is plausible reconstruction in some popular archaeological writing, not established by 1 Samuel. It has been left out of the manuscript.
- The Philistines do show Aegean-linked material culture, and a 2019 ancient-DNA study from Ashkelon found a European-related genetic component in the early Iron Age population. That does not prove the Endor rite was imported from them.
- The narrative calls the figure “Samuel,” gives him Samuel’s earlier message, and records fulfillment. The manuscript states Kyle’s likely reading while acknowledging the historic interpretive dispute.
- Saul did inquire of the Lord in 1 Samuel 28:6. First Chronicles 10:14 says he “inquired not of the Lord.” The manuscript now quotes 1 Chronicles 10:13–14 directly (Movement Two) and resolves the tension explicitly: 1 Samuel records the outward motions, Chronicles renders the verdict on what those motions were worth. The sermon still avoids claiming Saul made no inquiry at all — the canonical point is that his approach was not faithful seeking marked by repentance and obedience.
- Do not generalize Saul’s judicial silence to every believer who feels God is silent. The manuscript explicitly guards this.
- The Ashtaroth detail is textually secure in 1 Samuel 31:10. First Chronicles 10:10 names the temple of Saul’s armor as the house of their gods and his head as placed in the temple of Dagon. If you add Dagon aloud, distinguish the Chronicles detail accurately.
- The Uriah inversion is a seed for the 2 Samuel series, not a developed detour. Keep it brief.
