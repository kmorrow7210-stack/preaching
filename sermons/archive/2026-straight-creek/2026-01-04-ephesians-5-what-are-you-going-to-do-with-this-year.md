What Are You Going To Do With This Year?
Ephesians 5:14-17

I. Upsetting the Equilibrium
9:42 PM.
Costa Concordia is gliding like a floating city off the coast of Italy. Lights. Music. Dinner plates
clinking. Vacation brains turned off.
Then the ship hits rocks.
Not a bump. Not a hiccup. A 174-foot gash rips open the hull. Water starts pouring in like
somebody pulled a plug. People hear this grinding, scraping sound that doesn’t belong on a
cruise. The whole thing shudders.
And right there, in that first moment when truth matters most, the captain does what a lot of
people do when consequences show up uninvited.
He lies.
He calls the Coast Guard and says it’s “just an electrical problem.”
Basically: Relax. We’re fine.
But they are not fine.
An hour passes. An hour of denial, delay, pretending the shaking will stop if you don’t look at it.
Then, finally, he orders evacuation. But now the clock has already eaten the options.
Because by the time evacuation starts, the ship is tilting so badly lifeboats can’t launch right.
Gravity is in charge now. Procedures don’t work cleanly anymore. People are trying to climb.
People are sliding. People are panicking. Some are jumping into freezing water because the
ship is becoming a trap.

I wondered off stage here
And where’s the captain?
He takes off his uniform.
He climbs into a lifeboat.
He heads to shore.
Not to rescue. Not to lead. To save himself while people are still trapped.

Then comes the phone call that makes your stomach turn. The Coast Guard captain gets him
on the line.
“Where are you?”
“I’m on shore.”
He later claims that as the ship listed, he “tripped and fell into a lifeboat.” Right. The kind of
accident that only happens when your survival instinct has a calendar reminder.
The Coast Guard captain loses it and yells, “Get back on board, you coward!”
But he never does. He says he’s “coordinating from the shore.”
Safe. While people die.
Later, he’s convicted of manslaughter and abandoning ship, sentenced to 16 years. But even
that isn’t the worst part of the story.
Here’s the part you don’t hear first.
He wasn’t just careless. He wasn’t just overconfident.
He had his mistress in the control room. She isn’t supposed to be there. But he wants to
impress her. So he orders a “sail-by salute,” blowing the foghorn like a show-off parade. “Look
at us. Look how great this ship is.”
So instead of staying five miles offshore where it’s safe, he steers toward the rocks.
Three hundred meters from shore. Three football fields. Close enough to wave at people on
the island. Close enough to play captain-of-the-year for an audience.
And when he finally orders the turn to avoid the rocks, the helmsman hesitates and the
correction comes 13 seconds too late.
Thirteen seconds.
That’s all it took for “We’re fine” to turn into catastrophe.
And that’s the danger. Not the rocks you can see. The danger is the slow slide into sloppy,
casual confidence where you keep telling yourself, I’m fine, right up until the moment you’re not.
That’s why Paul writes what he writes next:
“Look carefully then how you walk… making the best use of the time…
because the days are evil.” (Ephesians 5:15–16)

Because a lot of disasters don’t start with rebellion.
They start with showing off, drifting off course, ignoring warnings, and wasting time until
you’re tilted past the point where the lifeboats work.

Some of you 90's youth group kids will remember this. We’d bring our “worldly” CDs to church
like they were cursed objects. We’d pray over them, lay hands on them, and then toss them into
a trash can fire behind the building while somebody tried to sing “Lord, I Lift Your Name on High”
in the wrong key. I remember watching my Aerosmith Get a Grip CD warp and bubble, thinking,
“This is it. I’m fully sold out.” And in that moment, I meant it. But then life kept happening.
Temptation updated. Napster showed up like a free buffet. And suddenly Outkast was back in
the playlist. Not because I had a villain arc. I didn’t wake up and choose darkness. I just got…
casual. Now flip to Acts 19. Ephesus wasn’t youth camp. It was a city with a goddess on every
corner and magic in the air. It was famous for spells and scrolls and power you could buy. And
then Paul shows up and Jesus starts bulldozing the place. People get saved, and they don’t just
feel sorry. They get specific. They start dragging out their old lives into the street. Real occult
books. Expensive stuff. Not symbolic. Not a $12 CD from Walmart. These were tools.
Livelihood. Identity. And they torch them publicly. No secret stash. No “I’ll keep one just in case.”
No backup hard drive. Luke says it was worth fifty thousand pieces of silver. Translation: this is
not a cute testimony night. This is repentance with receipts.
And you know what happens when repentance gets that public? It collides with the economy.
Because in Ephesus, business was built on the goddess. Shrines. Tourism. Fear. Ritual. An
entire system that fed off darkness. So the city snaps. A mob packs the theater. For hours they
scream, “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!” Not because they’re deep thinkers. Because their
profits are threatened. Their identity is threatened. Their whole world is getting shaken.
Paul wants to walk into it, because of course he does. The disciples physically restrain him. And
then, later, Paul gathers the Ephesian elders and he gives them the tearful warning on the
shore. Wolves are coming. Not cute wolves. Not outsiders with pitchforks. Wolves that talk like
Christians. Wolves that know the sheep. Then Paul leaves for good. So when you open the
book of Ephesians, you’re not reading an abstract theology essay. You’re reading a letter sent
back into a city like that, to a church like that, from a man in chains who knows the kind of
pressure that’s coming. And when he says, “Look carefully how you walk,” he’s not giving you a
planner tip. He’s teaching a church how to stay awake before the hit comes. We'd pray over
them like they were cursed artifacts, then toss 'em into the flames while singing 'Lord, I Lift Your
Name on High' off-key. I watched my Aerosmith "Get a Grip" cd warp and bubble, thinking, 'This
is it! I'm fully sold out!'
But flip to Acts 19 in Ephesus: Real-deal revival explodes under Paul. New believers drag out
their occult scrolls; expensive magic books worth a fortune, and publicly torch them. No regrets,

no backups. Just real repentance. Us 90s warriors? We meant it in that fiery moment... but then
Napster rolled up like a digital temptation, and suddenly Outkast was back in the playlist.

Transition
Now here’s the uncomfortable part.
The people who burned the books are the same people Paul has to tell, ‘Wake up.’
He’s writing to believers and he says, ‘Look carefully how you walk.’
Which means you can have a real conversion and still start walking sloppy.
Not because you had some villain arc.
Because you got tired. You got distracted. You got casual.
And in a city like Ephesus, casual is how you get devoured.
That’s why Paul says, ‘Redeem the time.’
Because if you don’t buy it back on purpose, something else will buy it from you.

II. Analyzing the Discrepancy
Some of you 90’s youth group kids will remember this.
We’d bring our “worldly” CDs to church like they were cursed objects. We’d pray over them, lay
hands on them, and then toss them into a trash can fire behind the building while somebody
tried to sing “Lord, I Lift Your Name on High” in the wrong key.
I remember watching my Aerosmith Get a Grip CD warp and bubble, thinking, “This is it. I’m fully
sold out.”
And in that moment, I meant it.
But then life kept happening.
Temptation updated. Napster showed up like a free buffet. And suddenly Outkast was back in
the playlist. I didn’t wake up and choose darkness. I just got… casual.
And if you’ve ever wondered, “How did I drift?”
That’s not just a 90’s youth group story.
That’s an Ephesus story.
Flip to Acts 19.
Ephesus wasn’t church camp. It was a city with a goddess on every corner, and magic was part
of the economy. Spells, scrolls, power you could buy.

Then Paul shows up, and Jesus starts bulldozing the place.
People get saved, and they don’t just feel sorry. They get specific. They start dragging out their
old lives into the street. Real occult books. Expensive stuff. Not symbolic. Tools. Livelihood.
Identity. And they torch them publicly. Luke says the value was fifty thousand pieces of silver.
Paul stays there about three years. Then he’s leaving, and he gathers the Ephesian elders.
Tearful scene, shoreline scene. And he tells them the part nobody wants to hear.
“Wolves are coming.”
Not outsiders with pitchforks. Wolves who talk like Christians. Wolves who know the sheep. He
says some will even rise up from among you and twist things and pull people away. And then
Paul says, “You may not see my face again.”
But by the time Paul was under house arrest in Rome…
Those same people who burned the books are the same people Paul later has to coach with,
“Look carefully how you walk… making the best use of the time… because the days are
evil… understand what the will of the Lord is.”

Kissing Your Faith Goodbye
Joshua Harris wrote I Kissed Dating Goodbye when he was 21. That book didn’t just sell well, it
shaped a generation. It passed a million copies and became a purity playbook for youth
groups everywhere.
And for a long time, it looked like a clean success story.
Then, over time, the story shifts.
He starts publicly rethinking what he taught. He apologizes. He asks his publisher to stop
printing the book.
Not long after, in 2019, he announces he no longer considers himself a Christian.
And here’s what I want us to recognize: it wasn’t a headline-grabbing leap. It was a long series
of steps. Re-evaluations. Disappointments. Reframing. Distance.
Now, he just says he’s a seeker, still drawn toward Jesus, still praying, but embracing “unsure”
as a place to live.
So I’m not telling you that story so we can pile on a guy.
I’m telling you to remind you that,
You can be sincere.

You can be influential.
You can have chapter-and-verse answers.
And still drift if you don’t “look carefully how you walk.”
Because the rocks you hit are usually the rocks you stopped watching for.

The Nature of the Wolf
When Paul is leaving for Jersusalem, knowing he’s most likely not ever coming back, he gives
these parting words:
Acts 20:29–31.
“I know that after I leave, savage wolves will come in among you and will not spare
the flock. Even from your own number men will arise and distort the truth in order to
draw away disciples after them. So be on your guard! Remember that for three
years I never stopped warning each of you night and day with tears.”
And remember what Paul said in Acts 20? He didn’t say the danger was a lion roaring in the
street. He said the danger was a wolf.
And if we learned anything from fairytales, we know this about wolves: The wolf is notorious
for wearing grandma's clothes.
They don’t come at you yelling heresy. They don't walk in with a pitchfork and horns. No, they
wrap the poison in stuff you already love.
They start with the agreement. "Yeah, protect the babies!" "Yeah, cut the taxes!" "Yeah, let's get
prayer back in schools!"
Boom. You’re nodding. Your guard is down. You think, "Finally, someone who gets it."
And then—psst—while the door is open, they slide it in. "By the way, the Jews run the media."
"You know Israel is a fake state, right?" "Maybe we should repeal the 19th Amendment."
And you freeze. You think, "Wait... that sounds wrong. But... they were right on the family values
part. They were right about the taxes." So you swallow the poison because you liked the sugar.

The 360-Degree Danger
And if we learned anything from fairytales, we know this about wolves: The wolf is notorious for
wearing grandma's clothes.
They don’t come at you yelling, "I'm evil!" They wrap the poison in stuff you already love.

And right now, in 2026, the clearest example of this is the exploding sentiment against
the Jewish people.
It used to be that if you saw antisemitism, you knew exactly where it was coming from—it was
the college campus protests, framed as "social justice."
And if you were a conservative Christian, it was easy to spot that wolf. You saw the rainbow or
Palestinian flags (two things that would normally never go together,) you heard the slogans, and
you said, "That’s not us. That’s the Left."
But the wolf is crafty. He knows if he comes at you from the Left, you’ll lock the door.
So now, he’s coming from the Right.
We are seeing the rise of the "Groyper" movement—the extremely online, "America First" fringe.
And here is the trap: They don't talk like college professors. They talk like our playlist and social
media feeds.
They say, "Christ is King."
They say, "Protect the family."
They say, "Defend tradition."
And you’re nodding. You’re thinking, "Finally, someone fighting for truth!"
But then, while the door is open, they slide it in.
"By the way, have you noticed who runs the banks?"
"By the way, we don't need to support Israel; they're the enemy of the faith."
And suddenly, you have people who claim to love the Jewish Messiah (Jesus), spewing hatred
toward the Jewish people.
The Trap of the "Allowed" Hate
Think about it. In our culture right now, hatred of the Jewish people is the one form of
discrimination that seems to get a pass from both sides.
The Wolf on the Left dresses up as "Compassion" and "Anti-Colonialism." It appeals to your
desire for justice.
The Wolf on the Right dresses up as "Christian Nationalism" and "Heritage." It appeals to your
desire for order.
But look at the result.

Different costumes. Same teeth.
Both roads lead to the same pit of hatred.
And if you are "unwise"—if you are walking casually—you will swallow the poison because you
liked the packaging.
You’ll tolerate the antisemitism because you liked the meme. You'll ignore the hate because you
liked the political stance.
This is why Paul is screaming in Ephesians 5.
He isn’t just saying, "Be nice."
He is saying, "Look carefully."
Because the days are evil. And the wolf doesn't care which side of the aisle you sit on, as long
as he can get you to drift away from the heart of God.

Stage 3: AHA (Disclosing the Clue to Resolution)
Paul gives us the anecdote in Ephesians 6.

Ephesians 5:15-17

The Cure — Eph 5:15–17 in three moves (33:00–35:00)
1. carefully how you walk.
On a summer evening in July of 2009, a teenager named Alexa is walking along Victory
Boulevard in Staten Island. It is a normal night. Sidewalk. Traffic. No drama. She is walking with
a friend, and her friend gets a text message.
Alexa leans in to read it. They keep walking. She is looking at the screen, laughing, completely
at ease. And then... the ground disappears.
There is no barricade. No orange cones. No "Warning" tape. Just an open manhole in the
middle of the sidewalk.
Alexa drops six feet straight down into the sewer and lands in raw sewage. When people finally
get to her, she is cut up, covered in filth, and shaken. She is trying to process how you can be
walking on a city sidewalk one second, and literally standing in a sewer the next.
She lived. She wasn’t paralyzed. It could’ve been worse. But the point is brutal.

She didn’t fall because she chose danger. She didn't wake up that morning and say, "I think I'll
jump in a sewer today." She fell because she stopped watching where she was walking.
And here is the hard truth we have to swallow: Gravity does not care about your motives.
It does not care that you didn't mean to fall. It only cares that you weren't looking.
Stop grading your life by intentions. Grade it by direction.
2. Redeem the time.
Naturally, our time doesn't belong to us, but is a slave to the Evil Age. We tend to view
time as an empty container we fill with activities. Paul views time as a prisoner we must
rescue. If you do not actively "buy it out," it remains the property of the age, serving the
purposes of decay, distraction, and darkness Like water flowing downhill, time serves the
path of least resistance: busyness, pleasure, fear, cultural currents. No effort needed to
waste it—it just happens. If a swimmer stops swimming in a fast river, they do not stay
still; they are swept downstream. Similarly, if a believer does not actively "buy back" the
time, the "evil days" automatically reclaim it for waste and si. Left unchecked, time
"belongs" to the evil age's agenda—numbing us, fragmenting us, pulling toward
death/futility. There are two types of time. Chronos, which is the time on a clock. We
can't control that without a flux capacitor. Then there's karios, which is timing,
opportunity. Chronos asks: “What time is it?” Kairos asks: “Is it time?”
● When Jesus said "My time has not yet come" (John 2:4) — He meant kairos (the
appointed moment)
● When Mark records "The time is fulfilled" (Mark 1:15) — He used kairos (the right
moment for God's kingdom)
● When demons asked "Have you come to torment us before the time?" (Matthew
8:29) — They meant kairos (the appointed judgment day)
Paul’s phrase “redeem the time” is a slave-market word. It means “buy out of the
marketplace.” Here’s the picture: as long as the slave is standing in the market,
somebody else can own them, use them, and spend them. Redemption is when a price
is paid to take them out, so they’re no longer available to that old ownership.
Paul says your kairos moments are like that. Left alone, they don’t stay neutral. They get
bought by the Evil Age and spent on distraction, numbness, and compromise. So to
redeem the time means you walk into that moment and you pay the cost to take it back.
You buy it back and give it to Christ.
3. Because the days are evil.
Paul doesn’t mean “hard days.” He means the atmosphere is hostile. The current runs
downhill.

The days are not neutral containers.
They’re more like pressure.
If you don’t notice it, you start breathing in what the age is pumping out.
That’s why drift is so normal.
Evil days don’t usually drag you into sin. They wear you into it.
Busy enough to stop praying. Entertained enough to stop thinking. Angry enough to stop
loving. Tired enough to settle.
So if you stop swimming, you don’t stay still.
You get carried.

Experiencing the Gospel
Now, you might hear Paul and think, “He’s being dramatic. I’m not about to backslide. I’m not
joining a cult. I’m just tired.”
Okay. But Paul’s not writing from a vacuum.
Later on that journey, he ends up in a city called Troas. Acts 20. It’s Sunday night. They’re
gathered. Paul is preaching late. The room is packed. Oil lamps burning. Hot air. Long day.
And there’s a young man named Eutychus sitting in an open window.
The Bible doesn’t say he was rebellious.
It doesn’t say he was plotting sin.
It says he was “sinking into a deep sleep.”
He wasn’t evil. He was exhausted.
And when you’re sitting in a window, tired is not harmless.
Because there’s one thing that does not care about your motives: gravity.
Gravity doesn’t care that you meant well.
Gravity doesn’t care that you love Jesus.
Gravity only cares that you nodded off in a dangerous place.
So Eutychus falls from the third story.
People rush down.
The moment is chaos.
The text says they took him up dead. At the very least, they believe he’s gone. The impact is
real.
So when Paul writes earlier in Ephesians 5:14, “Wake up, sleeper, rise from the dead,” that isn’t
just a clever line to him.

He has watched what happens when a person closes their eyes for “just a moment” in the
wrong spot.
And then comes the moment that turns this into gospel.
Paul runs downstairs.
And he doesn’t stand over the boy and lecture him.
He doesn’t say, “See? You should’ve tried harder.”
He throws his body on him.
He embraces him.
He identifies with him.
And life comes back.
And here is the turn.
We are Eutychus.
We sit in the window of the Evil Age.
We get comfortable.
We get casual.
We get sleepy.
Not because we hate God, but because we’re tired and the room is warm and the warnings start
sounding dramatic.
And we fall.
Into rubble we didn’t plan.
Into consequences we didn’t want.
Into sin that feels like, “How did I even get here?”
But a Greater Paul didn’t just run downstairs.
Jesus stepped down from Heaven.
He didn’t coordinate from shore.
He entered the wreck.
He threw Himself onto the cross.
He absorbed the impact of the fall that should’ve killed us.
So hear me: the reason you can redeem the time is not because you’re a disciplined person.
It’s because you’ve been raised.
You don’t have to stay dead in the driveway of your mistakes.
You don’t have to rot in the sewer you fell into.
The embrace of the Savior put life back in your lungs.

So wake up.
Not to earn love.
Because you’ve been loved.
Not to earn life.
Because life has already been spoken over you.
And that’s why Paul doesn’t stop at “be careful.”
Right after this, he tells us the power source.
“Be filled with the Spirit.”
Because you cannot out-swim evil days on willpower.
You need a new filling.

Anticipating the Consequences
There’s a moment from that night that became famous.
The Coast Guard commander, Gregorio De Falco, finally gets Schettino on the radio and
realizes the captain has abandoned his post. And he unleashes a sentence that basically
translates to:
“You are the captain. Get back where you belong. Do your job. Take responsibility.”
I’m not repeating it verbatim in church, but you can hear the fury in his voice because this is not
about protocol. This is about lives.
And I think that is exactly what the Holy Spirit is saying to the church in 2026.
Not “try harder.”
Not “feel guiltier.”
Not “make a few New Year’s resolutions.”
He’s saying: Get back on board.
Get back to your post.
Stop letting the Attention Economy steer the ship.
Stop letting tiredness make the decisions.
Stop letting the current pick your direction.
Paul’s been saying it the whole time:
“Look carefully how you walk.”
“Redeem the time.”
“Because the days are evil.”

And here’s the part that should haunt us:
The investigation showed the helmsman hesitated.
The correction came thirteen seconds too late.
Thirteen seconds.
That’s the difference between a close call and a catastrophe.
And I’m telling you right now, you’re going to have your thirteen seconds.
Maybe today. Probably tonight.
It’s that moment the notification pops up and you feel your hand reach automatically.
It’s that moment anxiety starts rising and you’re about to medicate it with noise.
It’s that moment you’re about to click the clip because you already agree with the first half and
your guard is down.
It’s that moment temptation whispers, “You deserve this.”
It’s that moment you’re about to say, “I’m fine,” while you’re clearly leaking.
That’s your thirteen seconds.
That’s kairos.
Not “what time is it?”
Is it time?
And in that moment you have a choice.
You can keep drifting.
You can keep doing sail-by salutes with your life, flirting with the rocks for applause.
Or you can grab the wheel.
You can redeem the time.
You can buy that moment back.
You can say, “No. Not this one.”
“This minute doesn’t belong to the age.”
“This attention doesn’t belong to the algorithm.”
“This moment belongs to the King.”
And here’s the gospel under the command:
Jesus stayed on the ship for you.
He didn’t abandon you.
He didn’t coordinate from shore.
He stepped into the wreck and took the fall.
So if you’re tired, the answer isn’t shame.
The answer is Spirit-filled wakefulness.

Because Paul doesn’t just say “be careful.”
He tells you the power source:
“Be filled with the Spirit.”
That’s how you get your thirteen seconds back.
Not by white-knuckling your way into holiness.
But by being filled with a better influence than the age.
So here’s the call:
Wake up, sleeper.
Get back on board.
Watch where you walk.
And when the thirteen seconds come, don’t hesitate.
What are you going to do with this year
Turn the wheel.
Buy it back.
