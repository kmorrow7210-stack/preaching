THE KING WHO CAME TO PICK A FIGHT

Luke 19:28–44 | Palm Sunday — Part 1 of 2

Kyle Morrow

Palm Sunday 2026

I. TWO PARADES

  

I want you to picture the western gate of Jerusalem. We’re talking first century here. It’s the week of Passover.

You’d most likely see Roman cavalry coming into the city. They’re on warhorses. You can imagine how blinding it would be when the armor on those things catches the afternoon sun. You’d hear the hooves cracking on the stone streets. And behind them, you’d have your infantrymen. With their leather. Their swords. And they’ve got a flag flying high with the eagle standard of the empire. In plain sight.

And at the very front, mounted on a horse, dressed ready for war — the Roman governor. Pontius Pilate.

  

This wasn’t just a state visit. This is SOP. Standard operating procedure. Every Passover, Pilate would leave Caesarea Maritima and take his garrison straight into Jerusalem. Because Passover was dangerous. This whole festival celebrated the last time God demolished a superpower to set His people free. You remember Egypt. The plagues. The sea splitting open. And here they are again — occupied. But this time by Rome.

Every year the crowds would swell. The nationalism would surge. And every year, the Roman governor would show up with his war machine to remind the Jewish people of one thing:

We’re in charge.

That’s the Pax Romana. The Peace of Rome. Peace at the point of a sword.

  

That was the parade from the west.

  

Now turn your head east.

  

Same week. Same city. Other side.

You’ve got a crowd of Galilean peasants coming down the Mount of Olives. They’re dusty. They’re loud. They’ve been walking for days from the northern provinces. And they’ve been following this rabbi from Nazareth — the one who heals the sick, opens blind eyes, says things that make the authorities lose sleep at night. He’s riding right in the middle of the procession.

Not on a warhorse.

On a donkey.

  

And this crowd is shouting Psalm 118 — the coronation psalm — at the top of their lungs.

  

[Read Luke 19:35–38]

  

So you’ve got two parades. One from the west. One from the east. One backed by the most powerful military machine in the ancient world. The other backed by a group of fishermen and tax collectors and day laborers waving palm branches. And those palm branches mean something. They hadn’t been waved in public since the Maccabean revolt. That’s a nationalist symbol. Everybody in that city knew what palm branches meant.

One of these parades is still being talked about two thousand years later.

It’s not Pilate’s.

II. THE DONKEY IS NOT WHAT YOU THINK

  

Now we’ve all been taught that the donkey is about humility. Right? Jesus is meek. Jesus is gentle. He’s riding a donkey because He’s not a threat.

That’s not what’s happening here. And to see why, I need to take you to a different story for a minute.

  

First Kings chapter 1.

  

David is old. The text says he can’t even keep himself warm anymore. He’s dying. And everybody knows it. Which means the throne is about to be empty. And in the ancient world, an empty throne is a loaded weapon.

David’s oldest surviving son — Adonijah — decides he’s not going to wait. He launches a coup. He goes and gets Joab, the commander of the army. He gets Abiathar, one of the two high priests. He’s got military power. He’s got religious authority. And he throws himself a private coronation party down at the Serpent Stone, near En-Rogel, in the Kidron Valley. Invitation only. Very deliberate about who gets in and who doesn’t.

You know who didn’t get an invitation? Solomon. Nathan the prophet. Zadok the priest. Benaiah and the royal bodyguard. Every single person loyal to David’s actual choice was deliberately excluded.

This wasn’t a celebration. This was a political hit.

  

Now Nathan finds out. And Nathan goes to Bathsheba and says — and I’m paraphrasing — “If we don’t move right now, you and Solomon are both dead by morning.” Because that’s how regime change worked. You take the throne, you eliminate the rivals. Standard procedure in the ancient Near East.

So Bathsheba goes to David. Nathan goes to David. And David — old, frail, can barely keep himself warm — suddenly snaps back into command mode. He doesn’t hesitate. He gives the order:

“Take my son Solomon. Put him on my mule. Take him down to the Gihon Spring. Have Zadok anoint him. Blow the trumpet. And declare him king.”

  

Now catch this. David doesn’t say “get Solomon a mule.” He says “put him on my mule.” The king’s personal mule. That animal was like Air Force One. It was the seat of the king. When Solomon sits on that mule, he’s sitting in David’s saddle. He’s occupying David’s seat before he ever sits on David’s throne.

And the mule matters. In the ancient world, a king rode a horse to war. But a king rode a mule or a donkey in peacetime — when the territory was already his. The mule says: this isn’t a battle. This is a coronation. The fight is over. The king has been chosen.

  

So they take Solomon down to the Gihon Spring — which is the only reliable water source in the city. It’s public. It’s visible. It’s right there at the base of the City of David in the Kidron Valley. And Zadok takes the horn of oil from the tabernacle and anoints Solomon. They blow the trumpet. And the text says the people celebrated with such noise that “the earth seemed to split with their sound.”

You hear that? The ground shook. The coronation of the true king was so loud that creation itself responded.

  

And here’s my favorite part. Adonijah is half a mile away, finishing his little private dinner party, and he hears the noise. Joab hears the trumpet and says, “What’s going on over there?” And when they find out? The text says every guest at Adonijah’s table got up and left. The pretender’s party emptied in minutes. Because when the real king shows up, the fake coronation falls apart on its own.

  

Now hold that story in your head and come back to Palm Sunday.

  

Same city. Same valley. Same road. A thousand years later, another Son of David is riding into Jerusalem. And He’s on a donkey.

Not a horse. A donkey.

And now you know what that means. It’s not humility. It’s not weakness. It’s the coronation animal. It’s the animal that says: the war is decided. The king has been chosen. This city belongs to Him.

  

Solomon rode David’s mule to the Gihon Spring and the ground shook. Jesus rides a donkey down the Mount of Olives and the whole crowd erupts with Psalm 118 — the coronation psalm. Same pattern. Same imagery. Same claim. But bigger. Because Solomon was a son of David. Jesus is the Son of David. Solomon was anointed at the spring. Jesus is the spring. Solomon built a temple. Jesus is the temple.

  

And the prophet Zechariah had written this scene into the script five hundred years before it happened:

“Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout aloud, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your king is coming to you; righteous and having salvation is he, humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey.” — Zechariah 9:9

But don’t stop at verse 9. Read the next verse. Zechariah 9:10:

“I will cut off the chariot from Ephraim and the war horse from Jerusalem; and the battle bow shall be cut off, and he shall speak peace to the nations.”

The donkey is a declaration. It says: the chariot is obsolete. The warhorse is obsolete. The battle bow is done. I didn’t come to fight Rome with Rome’s weapons. I came because the war is already won — on terms none of you have figured out yet.

And the location. Don’t miss this. He starts from the Mount of Olives. That’s the exact spot where Zechariah 14 says Yahweh’s feet will stand on the Day of the Lord when He shows up to judge the nations. Jesus isn’t just fulfilling one prophecy. He’s stacking them. He’s triggering every messianic alarm in the system at the same time.

This is not a humble entrance. This is an act of war. Just with different weapons.

  

Adonijah threw a parade and claimed a throne that wasn’t his. And the second the real king was anointed, Adonijah’s party vanished. Now flash forward a thousand years. Remember who was at Adonijah’s table. Joab — the military muscle. Abiathar — the religious authority. The sword and the priesthood, both backing a pretender.

Now look at Jerusalem during Passover. Rome is Joab. The military machine. The muscle enforcing a false claim. And the Sadducees, the high priest, the Sanhedrin, the whole Temple establishment? They’re Abiathar. The religious authority that backed the wrong king because the wrong king protected their position.

And behind all of it — behind Rome, behind the religious system, behind every power grab dressed in political and priestly robes — the accuser himself. The original Adonijah. The one who has wanted the throne of God since before the foundations of the world. And when Jesus rides down the Mount of Olives on that donkey, He’s letting all of them know — Pilate, the priesthood, and every dark power standing behind them: your party is over. The true King is here.

III. THE PANIC

  

Now I need you to understand what this procession actually looked and sounded like. Because we read “the whole multitude of disciples began to rejoice and praise God with a loud voice” and we picture a church service. People clapping politely. Maybe somebody raises a hand.

That is not what’s happening here. You have to strip away the sanitized Sunday school imagery and stand on that road.

  

You’ve got hundreds — maybe thousands — of Galilean pilgrims crammed onto the narrow dirt road that descends the steep western slope of the Mount of Olives. They’ve been walking for days. They are sweating. They are kicking up choking clouds of white limestone dust in the hot spring air.

They watched Jesus heal a blind man screaming “Son of David” outside Jericho. They watched Him walk into the house of a tax collector the whole town hated and walk out with the man’s repentance. And some of them — John’s Gospel tells us this — some of them were there in Bethany when Jesus called a man named Lazarus out of a tomb. Four days dead. Wrapped in grave clothes. And he walked out.

These people have seen things that don’t have natural explanations. And now their rabbi is on a donkey, and they know exactly what that means.

  

So they start pulling the cloaks off their own backs and throwing them on the dirt road in front of Him.

Now don’t move past that too fast. Because we read “they spread their cloaks on the road” and we think they tossed a light jacket on the ground. That’s not what this is. In the first century, your outer cloak was the most valuable practical thing you owned. The Torah says if you take a man’s cloak as collateral on a debt, you have to give it back by sundown — because it’s his blanket. It’s his shelter. It’s what stands between him and the freezing desert night. When these pilgrims throw their cloaks on the ground in front of a donkey, they are laying down the most essential thing they have to survive.

And there’s a precedent for this. Second Kings chapter 9 — when Jehu is anointed king of Israel during a military coup, his officers immediately strip their garments and spread them under him. Cloaks on the ground. Trumpet blast. Declaration: “Jehu is king!” It’s a coronation act. It’s a pledge of total allegiance that costs you something.

These pilgrims aren’t just cheering. They are crowning Him. With the only thing they have.

  

And they’re ripping branches off the trees — palm branches, the symbol of Jewish revolt that hasn’t been waved in public since the Maccabees threw out the last foreign occupiers.

And they’re shouting. Not singing — shouting. Psalm 118. The coronation psalm.

  

And you need to understand the geography of this shout. Jesus is descending into the Kidron Valley. Topographically, that valley acts like a massive natural acoustic amphitheater. Every sound made on that road is caught, amplified, and bounced directly off the sheer eastern limestone wall of the Temple Mount.

Luke says they praised God with a loud voice — and the Greek word for loud is megas. Great. Massive. The phrase is phonē megalē. It’s the root for our word megaphone. This is a deafening, uncontainable roar.

And in their native Hebrew, they aren’t politely singing “Hosanna.” They are screaming “Hoshiya na!” It is a desperate, guttural, militaristic demand. It literally translates to: “Save us NOW!” “Deliver us IMMEDIATELY!” And Luke tells us the crowd explicitly altered the ancient Greek text of the Septuagint as they shouted. They injected a new, unauthorized noun into the liturgy. They screamed: “Blessed is the King — ho basileus — who comes in the name of the Lord!”

  

It’s a scene this city has seen before.

  

When David brought the Ark of the Covenant into Jerusalem in Second Samuel 6, the text says he danced before the Lord with all his might. He stripped off his royal robes, put on a linen ephod — a common priestly garment — and he worshipped with total abandon. The people shouted. The trumpets blew. The presence of God was entering the city, and the king didn’t care who was watching.

That’s what’s happening on the Mount of Olives. The presence of God is entering Jerusalem again — not in a golden box carried by priests, but on a donkey, in the flesh. And the crowd responds the same way David did. They strip what they have. They shout with everything in them. They worship without calculating the cost.

  

And just like before, somebody is watching from a position of privilege and hating every second of it.

When David danced before the Ark, Michal — Saul’s daughter, the old regime — looked down from a palace window and despised him. She thought it was beneath his dignity. She thought he was making a fool of himself. She looked at undignified worship and saw only disorder.

The Pharisees are Michal. Standing in the choking dust, watching peasants throw their survival gear into the dirt and scream altered coronation psalms at a rabbi on a donkey — and all they can see is a threat. All they can feel is the ground shifting under their feet.

  

And here’s what makes it terrifying for them. The Mount of Olives overlooks the Temple Mount. The Antonia Fortress — Rome’s military garrison — sits at the northwest corner of that Temple, staffed with extra soldiers because it’s Passover week. Those Roman sentries are standing on the parapets in full armor, looking east. And what they are seeing and hearing out of that megaphone valley isn’t a religious parade. It’s a massive mob of Jewish nationalists waving revolt symbols and screaming that their new king has arrived.

Declaring a king without the Roman Senate’s authorization is crimen maiestatis — high treason.

This is a lit fuse.

  

[Read Luke 19:39]

  

“Teacher, rebuke your disciples.”

  

The Pharisees are terrified. And honestly? They should be.

They’ve done the math. If this megaphonic roar keeps bouncing off the Temple walls within earshot of the garrison, Pilate is going to send cavalry. Real cavalry. The kind that doesn’t stop when it hits the crowd. They know exactly what a public messianic claim means under Roman law.

Treason.

  

And look at the word they use. “Rebuke.” In the Greek that’s epitimao. It means to sharply censure. To sternly command silence. And this is the exact same verb Jesus uses to cast out demons. Same word He uses to silence a deadly storm on Galilee. The Pharisees are treating this messianic fervor like a dangerous, demonic outbreak that needs to be shut down before it gets everyone killed.

And here’s the thing — from their earthly perspective? They were right. This procession was dangerous. It would get people killed.

The man on the donkey knew that better than anyone. He just refused to stop.

IV. THE STONES WILL CRY OUT

  

[Read Luke 19:40]

  

“I tell you, if these keep silent, the stones will cry out.”

  

This verse gets domesticated every single Palm Sunday. We turn it into a worship song lyric. We make it sentimental. “Isn’t it beautiful — even the rocks would praise Him!”

It’s not beautiful. It’s one of the most aggressive things Jesus says in the entire Gospel of Luke.

  

Let me show you why.

  

Start with the grammar. If you’re in Koine Greek and you want to say something hypothetical — like, “if they should happen to be silent” — you’d use the subjunctive mood. That’s the standard construction. It’s a maybe. A possibility.

But that’s not what Jesus does. He uses the future indicative. In both halves of the sentence. What that does grammatically is it eliminates the maybe. It takes contingency off the table. He’s not saying, “If they might be silent, the stones might cry out.” He’s saying: If they are silent — and let’s go ahead and assume that they are — the stones will cry out. Not might. Not could. Will.

He’s telling the Pharisees: this coronation cannot be cancelled. Your jurisdiction doesn’t apply here. You don’t have the authority to silence what the cosmos itself is compelled to announce.

  

Now look at the verb. The word for “cry out” is krazo. It’s visceral. It’s almost onomatopoeic — you can hear the sound in the word itself. It means to shriek. To scream. To let out a raw, uncontrolled sound. This is the same verb used when demons scream in the presence of Jesus. It’s the verb for the cry of the oppressed reaching the ears of God.

The stones wouldn’t hum a hymn. They would scream.

  

But here’s where this gets really sharp. Because Jesus is not making this up. He’s not inventing a metaphor. He’s reaching back into the Hebrew prophets and pulling out a loaded phrase.

Habakkuk 2:11. God is pronouncing judgment on a corrupt regime — one that built its wealth on the backs of the poor. That squeezed money out of the powerless and used it to build monuments to itself. And God says this: “The stone will cry out from the wall, and the beam from the woodwork will respond.”

Now in Habakkuk, the stones are not praising anybody. They’re testifying. They’re on the witness stand. The building materials have absorbed the violence of the builders. Every act of exploitation. Every drop of blood money. Every rigged transaction. The stones soaked it up. And in God’s courtroom, the building materials take the stand to condemn the people who laid them.

  

Now picture where Jesus is standing when He says this.

  

He’s coming down the Mount of Olives. And He’s looking right at it. The Temple Mount. Those massive, gleaming, white limestone blocks of Herod’s Temple. That structure was built by a violent puppet king using crushing taxation. It was maintained by a priestly aristocracy that had turned the house of prayer into a financial operation. One that Jesus is going to personally dismantle the second He walks through the gate.

When Jesus says “the stones will cry out,” He is not making a nice, general statement about nature worshipping God. He’s pointing at the Temple and invoking an indictment.

  

But He’s also tapping into something that runs through the entire biblical story. Because stones have been testifying since the beginning.

The stones of Abraham’s altar told the story of a God who interrupts the impossible to keep His covenant. The stones Joshua pulled from the Jordan riverbed and stacked on the bank were set there for one reason — so that when your children ask “what do these stones mean,” you can tell them God made a way where there was no way. When Joshua made his final covenant at Shechem, he set up a great stone under the oak and told the people: “This stone has heard all the words of the LORD that He spoke to us. It will be a witness against you.” And when a shepherd boy walked onto a battlefield with nothing but a sling, it was a stone — one small, humble stone — that brought down the armor-covered champion of an empire.

Stones witness. Stones remember. Stones testify. And Jesus is telling the Pharisees: you can silence every human voice on this hillside, and the testimony will not stop. It has never stopped. The stones have been preaching since Abraham, and they’re not about to go quiet now.

  

If you silence the praises of these poor pilgrims, He’s saying, the stones of your own blood-bought Temple will take the stand against you. If the disciples stop shouting “Save us!” the stones will start shouting “Guilty!”

  

And we know that first-century Jews already read Habakkuk this way. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls there’s a document called the Pesher Habakkuk. It’s a commentary written by the Essene community out at Qumran. And in their reading of this exact passage, they apply the crying stones directly to the corrupt priesthood of Jerusalem. This wasn’t a new idea. This critique was already circulating. Jesus is stepping into a prophetic tradition and pressing the trigger.

  

And Luke knows what he’s doing with this image. He doesn’t let the stone motif go. Watch this. Look four verses later.

  

Verse 40: The stones will cry out.

Verse 44: They will not leave one stone upon another.

  

Same word. Same stones. The stones that stand ready to testify in verse 40 are the same stones that are scheduled for demolition in verse 44. The witness guarantees the verdict. The testimony of the stones seals the destruction of the stones.

And in 70 AD, that’s exactly what happened. The Roman general Titus laid siege to Jerusalem, starved its people, and tore the Temple down to its foundation. Stone from stone. Not one left standing. Exactly the way Jesus said it would go.

The stones cried out. And then the stones came down.

V. THE TEARS

  

[Read Luke 19:41–44]

  

This is the part that wrecks me every time I read it.

  

Think about the whiplash of this moment. The King who just claimed cosmic authority. The one who told the Pharisees that the very stones of creation answer to Him, not to them. That aggressive, unyielding King — right here, on this dirt road, at the height of His own parade — breaks down.

And the Greek tells us this is not a quiet moment. The word Luke uses for Jesus’ weeping is klaio. That means to wail. To sob. To convulse with audible grief. This is not the word used in John 11 when Jesus shed a quiet tear at Lazarus’s tomb. That word is dakryo. Composed. Quiet.

Klaio is the sound a mother makes at a funeral. It’s the heaving, chest-cracking weeping of someone who sees a catastrophe coming and knows the people they love are refusing the only way out.

  

Listen to what He says through His tears: “If you, even you, had only recognized on this day the things that make for peace — but now they are hidden from your eyes.”

  

And then He uses a word that carries the weight of the entire Old Testament behind it.

  

“Because you did not know the time of your visitation.”

  

Visitation. In the Greek, that’s episkope. It means a formal, divine inspection. It’s the moment when God Himself steps into your reality. He doesn’t send a postcard. He doesn’t send a prophet. He shows up in person, offers you a window of grace, and hands you a way out.

Historically, we know exactly what Jesus was seeing. He knew that because the religious establishment rejected His peace, they would eventually try to fight Rome on their own terms. And forty years later, the Roman legions would march in and reduce their magnificent Temple to ash.

  

But if you leave this in the first century, it is just a tragic history lesson. It stays “out there.”

  

If you want to know what is making the Creator of the universe sob in the dirt, ask yourself this:

  

Have you ever had a front-row seat to somebody driving their life, their marriage, or their family right off a cliff? You offer them help. You beg them to turn around. But they are so addicted to their own control, so blinded by their own pride, that they look you in the eye and say, “No thanks, I’ve got this.”

What do you do in that moment? You don’t stand there with a clipboard and say, “I told you so.” You weep. You wail over the senseless waste of it all.

That is what God is doing on the Mount of Olives.

  

Who is He weeping over? Not the pilgrims behind Him. They got it.

He is weeping over the managers. He is sobbing over the people who had God standing right in front of their faces, and chose their system instead. To accept Jesus meant surrendering their leverage. It meant giving up their seat at Adonijah’s table. They would rather keep their exhausting, anxiety-ridden illusion of control than surrender to the King of Peace.

  

And we do the exact same thing.

  

God still does episkope. He still does visitations. How many times does the Holy Spirit interrupt your life, offering you a window of grace? A moment of conviction. A crisis that brings you to the end of yourself. The King of Peace rides right up to the gate of your carefully managed life, your career, your secret sin, and says, “Let me take the throne. Let me give you peace.”

But peace requires surrender. And we are terrified of what it will cost us to actually bow. So we lock the gate. We miss the visitation.

  

And here is the detail that makes Jesus break. Look at what He says in verse 44. He doesn’t just say the stones will come down. He says this pride is going to dash you to the ground — you and your children within you.

Your children.

  

Jesus isn’t crying because His feelings are hurt. He is weeping because He knows the collateral damage of human pride. When we act like the managers — when we stubbornly hold onto our control, when we refuse to surrender our pride, our addictions, our need to be right — we don’t just hurt ourselves. The walls eventually collapse on the people we love. Our pride always brings the roof down on the “children within the walls.” The pretender’s ambition always leaves collateral damage.

The tragedy locked inside that word — episkope — is this: a missed visitation of grace leaves you entirely exposed to the natural, destructive consequences of your own rebellion.

  

This moment exposes the terrifying vulnerability of God. He just proved He has the power to make the inanimate cosmos scream. But He will not force the human heart to surrender. He gives us the terrifying dignity of saying “no” to Him. He loves you enough to let you choose your own destruction.

But He will sob as you do it.

  

And so the King weeps. In public. On the road. Surrounded by praises, staring at a people who are too proud to be saved, weeping over the peace they could have had.

VI. TWO PARADES. ONE QUESTION.

  

Two processions entered Jerusalem that Passover week.

  

Pilate came from the west. Horses. Swords. The full weight of empire. His message was simple: We’re in control. Resist and die.

Jesus came from the east. Borrowed donkey. Crowd of nobodies shouting Scripture. His message was different: The war is already over. Your swords are obsolete. The King is here. And He came to save, not to slaughter.

  

Rome offered peace through violence. Jesus offered peace through sacrifice. Rome said: bow to Caesar or be crushed. Jesus said: I am the one Zechariah wrote about. I am the one the psalms have been waiting for. I am the one whose arrival makes the stones scream and the foundations shake.

  

And then He wept. Because the people running the city chose the wrong parade. And everyone inside the walls was going to pay for it.

  

Now we love to talk about how “fickle” that palm-waving crowd was. How they praised Him on Sunday and crucified Him on Friday. But that’s not what happened. And we need to stop saying it, because we’re blaming the wrong people.

The Hosanna crowd — the ones shouting Psalm 118 on that road — those were pilgrims. Galileans. Outcasts. People who had walked with Jesus for days. They’d seen the blind healed outside Jericho. They’d watched Him call Zacchaeus out of a tree. They recognized Him because they’d been close enough to see what He was doing.

The Crucify crowd was different people entirely. That was the home team. The power brokers. The Temple managers. Abiathar’s heirs — religious leaders who backed the wrong king because the wrong king kept their system running. They woke up before dawn on Friday morning to make sure this got handled before the pilgrims found out. They ran the trial at night. They assembled their own crowd at the praetorium. And by the time the Galileans woke up in their camps outside the city walls, it was already done.

It’s a haunting distinction. The people with nothing to lose saw God. The people with everything to manage stayed blind.

  

And that’s the question this text puts in front of every one of us.

  

Which crowd are you in?

  

Are you the pilgrim — close enough to Jesus to recognize Him when He shows up in a form you didn’t expect? Walking with Him even when the road is long and dusty and the destination isn’t clear? Shouting the psalm even when it’s politically inconvenient?

Or are you the manager? Protecting the system. Keeping the institution running. Too invested in the way things are to recognize God doing something new. So busy managing the Temple that you miss the God the Temple was built for.

  

We live in a world full of Adonijah’s dinner parties. Private coronations. Power grabs dressed up in religious language. People and systems and institutions that have crowned themselves king — over your family, over your identity, over your future — operating on the assumption that the real king isn’t coming.

But the real king always comes. And He doesn’t come on a warhorse. He comes on a donkey. Through a door you weren’t watching. At a time you didn’t plan for.

And when He shows up — the pretender’s table empties. Every time. The ground shakes. The trumpet sounds. And the thing that crowned itself king over your life suddenly realizes it’s been sitting at the wrong party.

  

Don’t miss the visitation.

  

That word — episkope — comes with an expiration date. “You did not know the time.” Kairos. Not time in general. The appointed time. The window. The moment that was built for you.

  

This is why Jesus spent His entire ministry warning us with parables about a thief in the night and a bridegroom arriving at midnight. What do a thief and a midnight bridegroom have in common? They don’t send a calendar invite. They don’t wait for you to be ready. They arrive, they disrupt your carefully managed schedule, and then the window closes.

Think about the parable of the bridegroom. Five bridesmaids missed the wedding because they were out conducting transactions — managing their oil. Just like the Temple leaders missed the King because they were too busy managing their religious economy. And the most terrifying sentence in that parable is just five words long: “And the door was shut.”

On Palm Sunday, the Bridegroom is riding down the Mount of Olives. But the managers are asleep at the wheel. And Jesus is weeping in the dust because He knows the door is shutting.

  

Windows open. And windows close. And the mercy of God, once refused, does not sit frozen in place forever. It moves. What was offered as salvation becomes the measure by which we’re weighed.

  

If that feels heavy — good. Sit with it this week. Because this story is not over.

  

Jesus told the Pharisees: if the people are silent, the stones will cry out.

  

The people were silenced. By Thursday night the disciples had scattered. By Friday morning the loudest voice in Jerusalem was a mob screaming for execution. The Galilean pilgrims who sang the coronation psalm on Sunday? They were still camped outside the city walls. They had no idea their rabbi was standing trial before dawn. The voices that praised Him were nowhere near the courtroom.

  

Silence.

  

So the question going into next week is this:

  

Did the stones cry out?

  

I’ll see you Easter morning.
