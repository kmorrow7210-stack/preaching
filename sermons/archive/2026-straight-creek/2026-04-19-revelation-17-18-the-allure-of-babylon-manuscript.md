## HOOK

Before he was ever a traitor, he was an apothecary in New Haven, Connecticut. Benedict Arnold. He sold medicines and books and a few imported goods out of a shop on a back street. He was good at it. He was also a ship captain, running cargo up and down the Atlantic seaboard. By the time the Revolutionary War broke out, he was a man of some means.

And he was a war hero before most people had even picked a side. He took Fort Ticonderoga in the first weeks of the war. He marched an army through the Maine wilderness in winter to attack Quebec. He held off a British fleet on Lake Champlain with a handful of boats built from green wood. He won the battle at Saratoga, the turning point of the entire war, and took two musket balls in the same leg for it. They called him the American Hannibal. George Washington said Arnold was the most enterprising man in the army.

And then he was overlooked. Congress promoted five junior officers ahead of him to major general. He paid for war expenses out of his own pocket and never got reimbursed. He got accused of profiteering. Most of the charges were dropped, but he received a public reprimand that burned him to the ground. By 1780 he was still limping. He was still bitter. Washington offered him command of West Point, the most strategic fort on the Hudson.

Something had already broken.

For more than a year, Arnold had been in secret correspondence with the British. He named his price. Twenty thousand pounds sterling and a general's commission in the British army. In exchange he would hand over West Point and three thousand American soldiers. A chance capture of a British courier and a mislaid letter kept it from working.

\[slow\]

I think some of us in this room understand something about what it feels like when the manna stops being enough.

## TRANSITION

Because the story John is about to show us is not the story of a villain.

It is the story of a hero who got tired.

Israel stood at the edge of the wilderness longing for the fish they used to eat in Egypt. The prophets watched kings and priests sell the nation to Babylon for a little more breathing room. The churches in Asia Minor watched families walk out every week because the incense at the pagan altar was easier than the cup of the Lord. And on and on.

Not just Egypt. Not just Babylon. The system that was supposed to serve you starts to own you. It is not your friend. It is hungry.

And if you read to the bottom of the manifest in Revelation 18, the cargo list of this city, past the gold and the silver and the silk and the spice and the cattle and the sheep and the chariots, the very last item on the ship's ledger is this. Bodies and souls of men.

That is what it wants.

And then the Lamb shows up, and his strategy looks insane from the outside. Because from the outside his fight looked like absolute defeat and slaughter.

Watch John tell it.

## OOPS — UPSETTING THE EQUILIBRIUM

Before he was ever called Peter, he was Simon bar Jonah. Simon son of Jonah. A fisherman out of Capernaum.

Not poor. Not some lonely figure with a net draped over one shoulder. He had a business. He had a boat. He had a partnership with James and John, the sons of Zebedee. Mark tells us they had hired servants. Hired servants. That is a small fleet. That is a charter operation.

Simon was a small businessman on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, working the best fishing waters in the region, paying his tax to the empire, selling his catch at the processing plants down in Magdala, watching the Roman road run past his town, and making a life. A real life. A good one.

And one day Jesus of Nazareth walks up to his boat after a failed night of fishing and says, "Put out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch."

Simon had been up all night. He had washed the nets. He was done for the day.

But he does what the rabbi says.

And the catch almost sinks two boats.

Simon falls to his knees right there in the water and says, "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord."

And Jesus says, "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men."

Simon leaves the boats. Leaves the partnership. Leaves the charter. Walks away from the business his father gave him. And for the next three years, that fisherman is closer to Jesus than almost anyone on earth. He walks on the water. He watches the transfiguration on the mountain. He stands up and says, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." And Jesus looks at him and changes his name. Not Simon anymore. Petros. Rock. "On this rock I will build my church."

Peter loved the Lord. Peter meant every word of it. Nobody in that room loved harder than Peter.

And at the last supper, when Jesus said all of them would fall away that night, Peter stood up and said, "Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away."

Jesus looked at him and said, "Truly, I tell you, this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times."

And Peter doubled down. "Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you."

And they all said the same thing. Every one of them.

Then they went to the garden.

Judas came with torches, and soldiers, and the captains of the temple. And Peter, the rock, pulls out a sword. A fisherman with a sword. He swings it at the high priest's servant and cuts off his right ear. John gives us the servant's name. Malchus.

\[slow down here\]

Y'all listen. That was Peter trying to save Jesus with the empire's tool. A sword. That is how Rome solves problems. That is how Herod solves problems. That is how the high priest's men solve problems. Peter had been told the kingdom was coming. And when the moment came, he reached for the kingdom's enemy's tactics to defend the kingdom's King.

And Jesus says, "Put your sword into its sheath." He touches the servant's ear. Heals it. And then he walks off with the soldiers. Voluntarily. Nobody took him. He went.

And Peter runs.

He runs for a minute. Then he comes back. He follows at a distance, the text says. He is trying to stay close. He loves his Lord. He wants to be there. But he does not know how to be there anymore, because the kingdom did not come the way he thought it would.

And he ends up in the courtyard of the high priest. Outside. In the cold.

And there is a fire.

\[pause\]

John calls it a specific kind of fire. The Greek word is ἀνθρακιά. Anthrakia. A charcoal fire. A fire of coals. A fire somebody else built, in somebody else's courtyard, and Peter is standing at it with his hands out in the dark.

And a servant girl looks at him. "You also were with Jesus the Galilean."

Peter says, "I do not know what you mean."

Another one sees him and says, "This man was with Jesus of Nazareth."

Peter says, "I do not know the man."

And then a group of them come at him together. "Certainly you too are one of them, for your accent betrays you." Your speech gives you away. You are a Galilean. You were with him.

And Peter began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear. "I do not know the man."

Immediately the rooster crowed.

And Luke tells us this one detail, and I need you to catch it. "And the Lord turned and looked at Peter."

Jesus was in the hearing. In the beating. In the mockery. And across that whole courtyard, he turned and found Peter's face. And Peter went out and wept bitterly.

\[pause, pastoral\]

Here is what I need you to hear this morning.

Peter did not deny Jesus because he was a villain. Peter denied Jesus because he had watched the kingdom lose. He had swung the sword, and it had not worked. He had followed at a distance because close was too dangerous. He had come to the courtyard to stay near, and the courtyard was the empire's courtyard, and the fire in that courtyard was the empire's fire.

And standing there in the cold, with his hands stretched out to a fire somebody else had built, Peter made a calculation. I can hear that calculation. Because it is the same calculation every tired, faithful person makes at some point in their life.

If I say I am his, I lose the warmth. I lose the place in this courtyard. I lose whatever safety this position gives me. And the kingdom I was betting on is already inside being beaten. I cannot save him. I already tried. My sword did not work. So what do I do?

I keep my seat. I keep my voice down. I keep my hands stretched out to the fire. I stay in the courtyard.

\[let that land\]

That is not a story about a coward. That is a story about a man who loved Jesus and could not figure out how the kingdom worked when the kingdom looked like it was losing. And in that confusion, in that cold, he took the warmth that was available, in the place that was available, from the fire that was burning, and he settled.

He settled for the empire's warmth because he could not see the King's fire yet.

And some of y'all know exactly what that feels like.

## TRANSITION — JOHN IN THE WILDERNESS

Now move with me.

Chapter 17, verse 1. The angel says to John, "Come, I will show you the judgment of the great prostitute who is seated on many waters." And the Spirit carries John away into a wilderness. And he sees her.

Gold. Purple. Scarlet. The cup gilded and full. The beast beneath her, scarlet, covered in blasphemous names. And John, the same John who watched the Lamb open the seven seals, John who heard the seven thunders, John who watched the martyrs cry out from under the altar, John is frozen.

The text says it in verse 6. "When I saw her, I marveled greatly."

The Greek word for marveled is θαυμάζω. Thaumazo. He was astonished. He was stopped cold.

Now think about who John is when this happens. This is not a new believer. This is not somebody who has not been warned. John has seen everything. He watched the horsemen ride. He saw the mountains fall when the sixth seal broke. He heard the trumpets blow and watched the star fall into the abyss. He has been through all of it. He knows her name. He read it on her forehead. Mystery, Babylon the Great, the Mother of Prostitutes and of Earth's Abominations. He knows what is in the cup. He has already seen the cargo list.

And he still marveled.

Because the offer is that good.

\[slow\]

She is not waving the cup in his face like a cartoon villain. She is not repulsive. She is beautiful. She is wealthy. She is confident. She is wearing the clothes of prosperity and sitting on something that looks like stability, and what she is offering looks like wisdom. It looks like the version of life where the manna does not run out. The version where you do not have to wonder if this commitment is going to cost you more than you have.

She looks reasonable.

And that is the problem. That is always the problem.

John was not writing to churches who were about to face a sword. He was writing to churches in Asia Minor who were facing something harder than a sword. He was writing to ordinary people living inside a prosperous, functional, beautiful empire where the meat at the marketplace had been offered to idols, where the trade guilds required a pinch of incense to join, where the invitations kept coming, and where the question every week was not, will you die for Jesus.

The question was, will you eat at his table when Babylon is serving a better meal next door?

\[pause\]

And that is still the question.

It is the same question Peter faced in the courtyard. Not, will you walk into the arena and die for him. The question was, will you say his name out loud, right now, at this fire, in this courtyard, with these people looking at you?

The warmth or the name. Pick one.

## AHA — DISCLOSING THE KEY TO THE RESOLUTION

And then the angel speaks.

Verse 7. "But the angel said to me, 'Why do you marvel?'"

\[pause\]

Four words. That is the gospel crux. Right there.

The angel is not yelling at John. He is not condemning him for being tempted. But he is stopping him cold and asking a real question. John, after everything you have seen, after every seal and every bowl, after every throne room vision, after all of it, why does she still have you? Let me show you what is underneath. Let me pull the curtain back. Because the allure only works if you do not know what is under the gold.

That is the key to the resolution. Not a stricter moral code. Not gritting your teeth and trying harder not to want what Babylon is selling. The key is seeing her for what she actually is. And you only see her for what she actually is when you already know what is actually true. Which means you have to know Christ. You have to know the one who told you what the cup is full of before the cup gets to your lips.

And this pattern is not new. It is everywhere in the Scripture.

\[build\]

Go back with me to Genesis 14. Same chapter we started this morning in. Abraham's nephew Lot has been kidnapped in a war, and Abraham gathers his men and goes and brings him back. And on the way home, two kings come out to meet him. One of them is the king of Sodom.

And the king of Sodom says, "Give me the persons, but take the goods for yourself."

Reasonable deal. You just won the war. Keep the spoils. Take what you earned.

But Abraham says, "I have lifted my hand to the Lord, God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth, that I would not take a thread or a sandal strap or anything that is yours, lest you should say, I have made Abram rich."

Why? Why would Abraham turn that down?

Because something happened before the king of Sodom showed up. A priest came out first. A king of Salem, whose name was Melchizedek, priest of God Most High. And Melchizedek brought out bread and wine. And he blessed Abraham. And Abraham gave him a tenth of everything.

Abraham already had bread and wine from the priest of God Most High in his hands. He had already received something better. And because of that, the king of Sodom's offer did not have anywhere to land.

\[pause\]

You want to know how you resist Babylon? You sit at the Lord's table until that table means more than anything Babylon is serving. You receive from Melchizedek before the king of Sodom shows up. You let the bread and the wine of the better covenant fill you first.

Or look at 1 Samuel 17. Goliath is standing in the valley. David is going to fight him. And Saul tries to put his own armor on the boy. His own coat of mail. His own helmet of bronze. His own sword.

And David tries it on. And then he takes it all off and lays it on the ground. And he says, "I cannot go with these, for I have not tested them."

And he goes to the brook and picks out five smooth stones.

David was not refusing because the armor was bad armor. It was the king's armor. It was the best equipment the empire had. He refused because it was not his. He had already been tested, alone in the fields with nobody watching, with a lion and a bear and the weight of a sling. He knew what he could fight with. He knew what had already been proved between him and God. And when the empire offered him its tools, offered him the strategy the empire uses to solve problems, he looked at it, set it down, and went to the brook.

\[slower\]

And there was a woman named Rahab.

She lived in Jericho. On the wall. She ran a business, and she survived the way you survive when you live on the wrong side of every wall in a city like that.

When the spies came, she hid them. And she tied a scarlet cord in her window. She bet everything on a God she had never met in person, whose armies were camped on the other side of the river, and she said, "I know that the Lord has given you the land." She chose the side that looked weaker. The side that made no economic sense. The side that required her to trust a promise she could not yet see.

And Jericho fell. And every wall on that city came down. And one house was still standing. The one with the scarlet cord.

\[build toward the close of this section\]

Abraham saw the cup and refused it because he already held something better. David saw the armor and set it down because he knew his own weapons. Rahab saw the walls coming down before they fell and tied her cord. None of them had a stricter moral code than the people around them. They had something they had already seen. Something already proved. Something already received. And because of that, the empire's offer could not get all the way in.

That is the key the angel is pointing to when he says, "Why do you marvel?"

Stop marveling at what she is. Start remembering what you already know is true.

\[pause\]

## WHEE — EXPERIENCING THE GOSPEL

So let's see what becomes of her.

Chapter 17, verse 15. The angel says, "The waters that you saw, where the prostitute is seated, are peoples and multitudes and nations and languages." She is sitting on the whole system. Every trade route. Every shipping lane. Every currency. All of it feeds her.

And then verse 16.

"And the ten horns that you saw, they and the beast will hate the prostitute. They will make her desolate and naked, and devour her flesh and burn her up with fire."

\[slow\]

The beast she rode. The kings who profited off her. The partners who toasted her wine and hauled her cargo. They turn on her. They strip the jewelry. They take the purple. They devour her flesh. And they burn the rest in a fire they built themselves.

That is not external judgment. That is the system feeding on itself.

Because here is what verse 17 says. "For God has put it into their hearts to carry out his purpose by being of one mind and handing over their royal power to the beast, until the words of God are fulfilled."

God did not have to kick the door down. He just let the beast be what the beast was. Every empire that worships itself eventually eats itself. Babel tried to reach the heavens, and God scattered their tongues. Nabonidus pulled the gods of his provinces into the walls of Babylon for safekeeping, and the Marduk priests opened the gates for Cyrus in a single night. Rome built its legions out of the very people it refused to make citizens, and one September afternoon in Ravenna those very people shipped the imperial regalia east, because there was nothing left to crown.

The head grows back. And the head eats itself again. That is what empires do.

\[build\]

Now come over with me to chapter 18.

John sees another angel. This one does not come out of the incense bowls. This one comes down from heaven. And the text says, "he had great authority, and the earth was made bright with his glory."

Pause on that. The earth was lit up.

This is not a candle. This is not the charcoal fire in the high priest's courtyard where Peter stretched his hands out in the cold. This is heaven's own light hitting the ground, and the ground lights up.

And he cries out with a mighty voice.

"Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great! She has become a dwelling place for demons, a haunt for every unclean spirit, a haunt for every unclean bird, a haunt for every unclean and detestable beast."

\[pause\]

Fallen. Fallen. Past tense. The Greek is aorist. It is a done thing. God does not say she will fall. He says she has fallen. From heaven's side, the matter is settled. The cup is already broken. The golden chain is already on the floor. The merchants have not caught up yet. The kings have not caught up yet. But heaven has already rung the bell.

And now. Verse 4.

"Then I heard another voice from heaven."

\[slow\]

After seventeen chapters of angels and trumpets and thunders and seals and bowls. After seventeen chapters of cosmic spectacle. Another voice from heaven. And this one is not an angel.

This one says, "my people."

That is the first time in the book of Revelation that God directly addresses his people in covenant language. Seventeen chapters of the cosmic drama. And the first tender word out of heaven's mouth to his own is right here. Two words in the Greek. Ho laós mou. My people.

Come out of her, my people.

\[pause, pastoral\]

Y'all hear what just happened? The angel asked John, why do you marvel. That was the Aha. That was heaven saying, here is what she really is, here is what is under the gold. But this. This is different. This is not a rebuke. This is a father's voice.

This is the voice Simon heard on the shore of Galilee after he denied the Lord three times in that courtyard. Jesus on a beach. Cooking fish on a charcoal fire. The same word. Anthrakia. A charcoal fire. The only other time that Greek word shows up in the whole New Testament. The courtyard fire where he denied, and the breakfast fire where Jesus is cooking for him. And Jesus does not bring up the denials. He just asks three times. "Simon, son of John, do you love me?" Three denials. Three questions. Three answers. "Feed my sheep."

The same voice that says come out of her my people is the voice that said come and have breakfast.

\[let that sit\]

Because that is what the whole book has been for. All that fire. All that smoke. All that judgment. Every bowl poured out on the empire's infrastructure. God did not write seventeen chapters of spectacle to impress you. He wrote them to get you out of the courtyard.

The warmth you have been standing at was never yours. The fire you have been stretching your hands over was built by people who will turn on you the same way the ten kings turned on her. That is what the system does. It consumes what it uses. It always has.

But the Lamb is on the beach. The Lamb has already been slain. The Lamb is already standing. And the Lamb is calling his people by name.

Come out.

\[pause\]

Not a thundered sentence. A covenant sentence. A father calling his kids home.

"Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins, lest you share in her plagues."

Because the plagues are coming. The bowls are already tilted. The cargo is already on a ship that is about to go down in a single hour. A single hour. John says it three times in chapter 18. "In a single hour your judgment has come." "In a single hour all this wealth has been laid waste." "In a single hour she has been laid waste."

The clock is not on you. The clock is on her.

## YEAH — ANTICIPATING THE CONSEQUENCES

So what do God's people do while the clock is running out on Babylon?

Come back with me to chapter 15. One page. One turn. Because before the bowls ever poured, before the angel ever came down, before the kings ever stripped the prostitute, John saw something else.

Verse 2. "And I saw what appeared to be a sea of glass mingled with fire, and also those who had conquered the beast and its image and the number of its name, standing beside the sea of glass with harps of God in their hands."

Standing.

At a sea of glass.

Mixed with fire.

\[slow\]

Y'all, a thousand years before John ever walked on Patmos, another group of people stood on the edge of another sea. They were former slaves. Their grandfathers had forgotten how free felt. They had built Pharaoh's storehouses with their backs. They had eaten onions and garlic because that was what the empire paid them in. They had watched their sons thrown into a river.

And then the plagues came. Ten of them. And on the other side of the last plague, they walked out of Egypt with borrowed gold and their children still breathing. And they got to the edge of the Red Sea.

And that sea was death. It was chaos. It was the deep. The Hebrew is tehom. The same water that hung over the face of the deep in Genesis 1. It was the end of the road. Pharaoh's army was behind them. The sea was in front of them. And they were stuck.

And God split it open.

And they walked across on dry ground. And when they got to the other side, and Pharaoh's chariots came in behind them, that same sea closed over Pharaoh and his horses. The very thing that was going to kill them became the thing that saved them.

And then Exodus 15. Moses and Miriam and the whole camp on the far bank. And they sang.

"I will sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea."

\[build\]

Now come back to Revelation 15.

Because John sees them standing at another sea. A sea of glass. A sea that looks like it is on fire. And it is the same picture. The sea of chaos, tamed. The sea of death, frozen under their feet like a floor. And the same people who came out of the last empire are standing at the sea that almost drowned them, and they are holding harps, and they are singing.

What song? Verse 3. "They sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb."

Two songs. One song. Moses at the first Red Sea. The Lamb at the last one.

And watch what verse 7 does. "And one of the four living creatures gave to the seven angels seven golden bowls full of the wrath of God."

The bowls do not come down until the song goes up.

\[pause\]

Let me say that again. The bowls that hit the empire's infrastructure in chapter 16 do not get poured until the singing happens in chapter 15. The worship of the saints at the sea of glass is what fills the bowls. The incense goes up. The judgment comes down. The order matters.

They did not fight Babylon with a sword. Peter already tried that. It did not work. They did not pull out the empire's tools. David already laid those down. They did not run the empire's calculations and look for a better deal. Abraham already refused the sandal strap.

They stood at the sea that tried to drown them. And they sang.

\[slow, pastoral\]

That is the Lamb's way.

Go all the way back to chapter 5. John is weeping because nobody could open the scroll. And one of the elders says, "Weep no more. Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered." And John turns to see a lion.

And there is no lion.

There is "a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain."

That is the pattern. That was the pattern the whole time. The Lamb did not coerce. The Lamb did not sit on the prostitute's throne. The Lamb did not run up the prostitute's numbers. The Lamb stood, and bled, and sang his way through a trial and a cross and a tomb, and on the other side of the deep, he walked out holding keys.

The strategy looked insane from the outside. I told you that at the top of this sermon. His fight looked like absolute defeat and slaughter. It looked like Peter was right to deny him. It looked like the sword was the only option and he had disarmed. It looked like the kingdom lost.

But the Lamb knew what Peter did not know yet. The sea that looks like your death is the floor heaven is about to put under your feet.

## CLOSE — THE SACRED STARE

Now watch what those bowls are.

Back in chapter 5, John sees the Lamb, slain and standing, and around him are twenty-four elders. And those elders are holding harps and golden bowls full of incense. And the text tells us exactly what that incense is. "The prayers of the saints."

Every cry of every martyr under the altar. Every "Our Father, hallowed be your name." Every whispered midnight "Lord, how long." Every saint in a prison cell. Every mother on her knees at a kitchen table. Every grandmother who prayed for a grandchild she might never live to see come home. All of it. Collected. Held in golden bowls at the feet of the Lamb.

And when the time came, heaven tilted the bowls.

\[pause\]

Your prayers are not decorative.

They are ordnance.

The bowls in chapter 16 are the prayers in chapter 5, finally poured back.

\[pause\]

Tomorrow morning you are going to wake up, and Babylon will still be there. She is going to pour you a cup before your feet hit the floor. She is going to whisper about your retirement account before you brush your teeth. She is going to slide the Amazon cart across the screen before your coffee is cold. She is going to sell you the illusion that if you just build your own little empire, if you just stack a little higher, if you just hedge a little better, then maybe you can be safe.

But y'all have stood at the glassy sea. Y'all have tasted Jesus's better fire.

And that is exactly why the angel cries out in Revelation 18, verse 4. "Come out of her, my people." This is not a call to take up a sword against the culture. Peter already tried that. Peter already pulled that sword and cut off a servant's ear, and Jesus healed it and told him to put it away. This is not a call to arm up. This is a call to simply withdraw from the cup the city serves.

Because the Lamb's victory is already finished. It was finished on a cross. It was finished in a tomb. It was finished when the stone moved and a fisherman ran to a garden to find his rabbi standing, still bleeding, and alive.

So now when Babylon leans in with her cup, you have a power John did not have yet in chapter 17. He had to ask an angel, "Why do you marvel?" But you already know the answer. You have been told what is in the cup. You have seen what happens to her. You have watched the kings devour her flesh and the merchants mourn at the smoke.

And so when she lures you tomorrow morning, you do not have to panic. You do not have to argue with her. You do not have to take up a sword.

You can look right at her allure. You can remember that you are already safe, on the shore, with Jesus. And you can simply walk away.

\[slow\]

The Sacred Stare.

That is what it looks like to come out of her.

Some of y'all came in this morning with a cup in your hand that you have been trying to stop drinking from for a long time. Not a dramatic cup. A beautiful one. A respectable one. A cup that looks like security. A cup that looks like provision. A cup that looks like you just being practical.

But you feel the weight of it. You feel the warmth of that courtyard fire. And you know it is not yours.

Heaven is calling you by name this morning. Not thundering. Not condemning. A father's voice. My people.

Come out of her.

Standing.

Singing.

With a Lamb who is bleeding, and standing, and calling.

Come out.

## CRAFT NOTES

**Runtime estimate.** Approximately 5,500 words. At a conversational 150 wpm that is about 37 minutes. At 140 wpm with pauses and pastoral moments it is closer to 40. Trimmable sections if needed: the Abraham/David/Rahab triad in the Aha can fold to two of the three. The John 21 beach beat in the Whee can compress to one paragraph. The Babylon/Rome specifics in the Whee ("Nabonidus...Ravenna...") can drop without losing the thesis.

**Thesis through-line.** Every move supports "the Lamb's answer is victory through sacrificial witness, not coercive force."
- Hook: Arnold tried to save himself with the empire's bargain.
- Oops: Peter tried to save Jesus with the empire's sword.
