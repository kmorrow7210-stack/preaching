#### The Scripture Reading — Mark 15:21-25

> And they compelled a passerby, Simon of Cyrene, who was coming in from the country, the father of Alexander and Rufus, to carry his cross. And they brought him to the place called Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull). And they offered him wine mixed with myrrh, but he did not take it. And they crucified him and divided his garments among them, casting lots for them, to decide what each should take. And it was the third hour when they crucified him.

---

#### The Hook: The Interrupted Plan

You ever have a moment, just one, that you replay in your head over and over again? What if I had just done it differently? Maybe a job you didn't take. That girl you didn't ask out. A word you said that you can't take back. Or maybe the time you swung when you should have held the bat.

There was a movie that came out with Jim Belushi called *Mr. Destiny*. It's about a guy named Larry. He's back in high school, in the state championship game. Bottom of the ninth, game on the line. Larry's at the plate, bat in his hand, everyone's watching, all eyes on Larry, and he strikes out. Just whiffs at it. For the next twenty years, Larry decides that strikeout is the reason his life didn't turn out the way he fantasized it would as a young man.

Now this is a classic Hollywood trick. We see it in *It's a Wonderful Life*. We see it in *Big*. Somebody's stuck, and they get handed the thing they always wanted: the chance to go back and do it differently. And Larry gets handed that chance. He wakes up one morning in a new reality. He hit that pitch. He's not the guy with the mortgage he can barely cover. He's not the guy with the job he hates. He's rich, he's powerful, he's driving luxury cars. All it took was one swing, and it gave him the life he thought he was always supposed to have.

But then the movie flips on us. Larry's perfect life — the money, the mansion, the corner office — it doesn't take long before he realizes it's not all it's cracked up to be.

His best friend Clip, the one who would crack jokes and make Larry laugh when things got hard — in this new timeline, Clip is broken. He's standing on the edge of a building, ready to jump, and Larry has to talk him down. And the woman Larry loved, Ellen — back in the old life she was his wife. They built a family. They had a life together. In this new reality, she's married to somebody else, and she cannot stand Larry.

Here's Larry. Rich, powerful, driving the car he always wanted, and he has never been more alone in his life. And Larry realizes he would rather have the mortgage, the stalled career, the ordinary, frustrating life with Ellen and Clip in it than the mansion with all the money and the cars. He begs to go back home. Back to the burden. Back to the unfinished house and the strikeout he never got over. Because he finally sees it. The life he thought was a curse was actually the one worth living.

#### The Transition: From the Bat to the Cross

Larry spent twenty years agonizing over a piece of wood. A baseball bat. He convinced himself that if he could change one swing from his past, he could erase everything wrong with his life. In our text this morning, we meet another man whose life and legacy were changed by a piece of wood. But he didn't swing it. He carried it.

Let's talk about Simon of Cyrene.

---

#### Movement 1: The Ruined Itinerary

Look at what the text says. "Coming in from the country." Mark is telling us something on purpose. He's telling us Simon is not local. He didn't roll out of bed that morning and show up in the middle of Jerusalem. Simon is a Diaspora Jew. "Diaspora" just means scattered. These were Jewish families who didn't live in Israel. They were scattered all across the Greco-Roman world.

Simon lived in Cyrene, modern-day Libya. For him, coming to Jerusalem at Passover wasn't a vacation. It was a pilgrimage. A once-in-a-lifetime trip. He had traveled over eight hundred miles to get there. And there he was. He had made it. He was clean, ceremonially pure, walking in, having followed every rule. He was ready to worship.

He got maybe a hundred yards from the temple gates. And then he felt a sharp tug at his collar.

He hears soldiers shouting. He can smell the metallic scent of blood mixed with sweat. This wasn't a parade. He was about to witness an execution. He sees a man beaten so severely he's unrecognizable.

He wasn't asked politely. The word Mark uses is *compelled*. In the ancient world that's the language of military conscription. It means you don't get to say no. It doesn't matter if you have plans to sacrifice, friends to meet, family waiting on you. In one moment, Simon's perfect plan was completely changed.

The soldiers slammed that jagged cross onto his shoulders. I imagine, as the wood bit into his skin, he was thinking about how his Passover was ruined. There goes his ritual purity — the very thing he had walked eight hundred miles to preserve. Gone the moment the blood touched his skin.

He won't be able to enter the temple gates now. He can't eat the holy meal with his boys. Instead, he's marching in public humiliation, following the footsteps of a condemned man.

I wonder if Simon, who traveled all that distance to worship, ever questioned God on those steps. Why me? Why my plans, God? Out of all the people in this city, why did you pick me?

Every dad in this room has most likely asked that question. Why me?

You had a picture in your head of how your career, your marriage, your kids were going to turn out. And then life handed you a splintered piece of wood you didn't ask for.

That precious child you love with all your heart, but you didn't see coming. Whether you're sixteen or forty, a surprise is a surprise, and you're seldom prepared. And you find yourself saying, "God, I didn't sign up for this. Why me? Why now?"

Or that Friday afternoon. You were looking forward to a stress-free weekend, and your boss wants to talk to you in the office. There are layoffs. You sit in your car wondering what you're going to tell everybody. Why me, God? I was just trying to do the right thing.

Or you show up for a routine check-up and walk out with a diagnosis that rearranges your entire life. The word the doctor used is now a word that defines your family's calendar, your finances, your prayers. You didn't ask for it, but it found you.

Or that call from the guidance counselor wanting to talk about your child. You didn't see that coming. And now you're carrying something nobody at work knows you're carrying. A weight that doesn't even have a name yet.

There's stuff nobody sees.

- The cold nights you go fill the tank and fix the car at ten o'clock so she can get to school safely the next day.
- The promotion you turned down because the travel was going to hurt your family.
- Every game you showed up to, even when you were running on fumes.
- The times you held it together when you were falling apart inside.

You don't get an award for that. You don't get recognition. Just a cross on your shoulders. But you keep walking.

#### Movement 2: The Sons

You keep walking. Simon kept walking. He didn't drop the wood.

But what else do we know about Simon? Let's go back to what Mark tells us.

"And they compelled a passerby, Simon of Cyrene, who was coming in from the country, the father of Alexander and Rufus, to carry his cross."

It's Father's Day. We cannot just look past the fact that Simon has two sons.

In the ancient world, you did not identify a father by his kids. That's backwards. You identified the sons by their father. But Mark flips the script. Why? Because when Mark wrote his Gospel to the church in Rome, the church already knew the sons. Mark is pointing to the front row of the congregation. He's saying, "You know the guy who carried the cross? That's Alexander and Rufus's dad."

The sons were more famous than the father.

Now here's what Mark doesn't tell us. He doesn't give us the road home. He doesn't give us a dinner table. He doesn't film the scene where Simon explains to his family what happened on that hill. But Mark tells us something louder. He tells us the sons were believers. Believers the whole Roman church knew by name. Somewhere between Golgotha and the front row of that church, the cross that ruined Simon's Passover became the faith that saved his family. We don't get the scene. We get the result.

---

#### Movement 3: The Historical Dominoes

Look at the dominoes that fall because one father carried a burden he never asked for.

Not long after, intense persecution breaks out in Jerusalem. Believers are scattered. Read Acts chapter 11. It says men from Cyprus and Cyrene traveled up to Antioch and started preaching the gospel to the Greeks. Antioch becomes the seedbed of the global church. It becomes the launchpad for the Apostle Paul.

Now I'm not going to tell you those men were Simon. The Bible doesn't say that, and I'm not going to put words in the text. But I'll tell you what I can see plainly. Simon's hometown — Cyrene — became one of the doors the gospel walked through to reach the nations.

And then it goes further. Decades later, Paul sits down and writes the book of Romans. In chapter 16 he lists the people who sustained his life and his ministry. Look at verse 13.

"Greet Rufus, chosen in the Lord; also his mother, who has been a mother to me as well."

Do you see what happened? Rufus. One of the sons named back in Mark 15. He's in Rome now, known in that church as "chosen in the Lord." And his mother — the wife of the man who carried the cross — is there too. When the great Apostle Paul was exhausted from planting churches across the Mediterranean, he went to her house. She fed him. She cared for him like a second mother.

The woman who watched her husband get dragged out of a crowd by Roman soldiers became a mother to the man who wrote half the New Testament.

---

#### Movement 4: The Treatment and the Altar

Fathers, I know you carry weights nobody sees. You go to work. You pay the mortgage. You carry stress and anxiety, and most days you do it with zero recognition. You look at your stalled career, your ruined plans, and you think you're living a cursed Option B.

But God's economy runs on unseen sacrifice.

Simon had no idea that carrying a piece of wood on that Passover afternoon would result in his wife feeding the Apostle Paul. He had no idea his forced burden would help push the gospel to the ends of the earth.

Just like Larry finally realized that his strikeout was the foundation of the life he was always meant to live, Simon's story proves your ruined plans are not accidents. The cross that hijacked Simon's Passover was the exact instrument God used to save his sons.

Your cross isn't a punishment for your failure. It's the foundation of your family's legacy.

Jesus said in Luke 9:23, "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me."

Being a disciple means carrying the cross. Being a father means carrying the cross.

Simon carried his. Will you carry yours?
