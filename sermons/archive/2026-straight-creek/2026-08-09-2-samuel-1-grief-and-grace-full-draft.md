# Grief and Grace in the Same Heart

## 2 Samuel 1

**Controlling question:** Why can deliverance feel like a funeral?

**Big idea:** Because our future is held by God, we can grieve what was lost without seizing, gloating, or refusing the life grace gives.

**Durable line:** My future is in God's hands, so mine can stay open.

---

## Opening — The Phone Call

There was a baseball player who could do almost anything with a bat.

His name was Rod Carew.

He won seven batting titles.

He finished with more than 3,000 hits.

And when his career was over, they put him in the Baseball Hall of Fame.

But there came a day when none of that mattered.

At seventy-one years old, Rod Carew was not waiting for another award.

He was waiting for a phone call.

He needed a new heart.

[Pause.]

Finally, the call came.

They had found him a donor.

Can you imagine the relief?

But there is a hard truth behind that kind of phone call.

The phone call you have been praying for almost always means another family has just received the phone call they have been dreading.

Rod Carew was going to live because a twenty-nine-year-old former NFL player named Konrad Reuland had died after a brain aneurysm.

Four days later, Rod received Konrad's heart and one of his kidneys.

[Let that breathe.]

What do you call a phone call like that?

Good news?

Bad news?

It was the answer to one family's prayer and the announcement of another family's grief.

One man lived.

One family lost a son.

Rod Carew's story is not David's story.

The circumstances are completely different.

But the feeling rhymes.

News that opens a future can also close another chapter forever.

Relief and grief can walk through the same door.

Have you ever received something you prayed for and still felt like crying?

Have you ever made it out of something and discovered that freedom did not feel the way you thought it would feel?

Why can some deliverances feel like a funeral?

The thing you wanted so desperately to end finally ends, and your first emotion is not celebration.

It is grief.

And you start asking yourself:

What is wrong with me?

What is wrong with us?

That is where we find David in 2 Samuel 1.

---

## The News Comes Up the Road

We know something David does not know yet.

Saul is dead.

The man who hunted David through the wilderness can never hunt him again.

The road toward the throne has opened.

But David does not know any of that yet.

You remember where we left him.

David has recovered everything the Amalekites took from Ziklag. He has been back for two days.

And on the third day, a lone figure appears in the distance.

His clothes are torn.

There is dust on his head.

Everybody looking down that road knows what that means.

This man is carrying news.

And look who walks into Ziklag.

An Amalekite.

Only this one is not carrying a sword.

He is carrying a story.

And a crown.

He thinks he is walking into a celebration.

But he has walked into a funeral carrying a crown and expecting a reward.

He falls down in front of David as if David is already king.

David asks him, "Where have you come from?"

He says, "I escaped from the camp of Israel."

David says, "What happened? Tell me."

And the young man gives him the news straight the first time.

The army ran.

Men died.

Saul is dead.

Jonathan is dead.

Then David asks one more question.

"How do you know?"

And that is when the Amalekite tells him a story.

He says, "I happened to be on Mount Gilboa. Saul was leaning on his spear. The chariots were closing in. He called me over and asked me to finish it."

"So I stood beside him, and I killed him."

Then watch his hands.

"I took the crown that was on his head and the band that was on his arm, and I brought them here to my lord."

He lays a dead king's crown at David's feet like an invoice.

Everything about this says the Amalekite has done the math.

Saul hated David.

Saul is dead.

David is next.

And I am the man who brought him the crown.

He is gambling that the crown equals a paycheck.

He thinks he knows what David wants.

He thinks David will look at Saul's death and see vindication.

Opportunity.

Advancement.

But the Amalekite has completely misread him.

The text does not show David reaching for the crown.

David reaches for his own clothes, and he tears them.

And all six hundred men with him do the same thing.

They mourn.

They weep.

They fast until evening.

Verse 12 tells us what they are grieving.

They grieve for Saul.

They grieve for Jonathan.

They grieve for the army of the Lord.

And they grieve for the nation of Israel.

Six hundred hard men sitting in the ashes of Ziklag, weeping over a king who spent years trying to kill their commander.

That is not a political performance.

That is grief.

And the Amalekite is standing in the middle of it with a crown the text never shows David taking.

---

## The Man Dies by His Own Story

After the mourning, David turns back to the messenger.

Verse 13:

"Where are you from?"

He has already asked where the man came from.

Now he is asking who the man is.

The messenger answers, "I am the son of a foreigner. An Amalekite."

David says, "Why weren't you afraid to lift your hand to destroy the Lord's anointed?"

Then David calls one of his men.

"Go. Strike him down."

And the Amalekite dies in the dirt at Ziklag.

Listen to the sentence David speaks over him:

"Your blood be on your own head. Your own mouth testified against you when you said, 'I killed the Lord's anointed.'"

Your own mouth.

The man walked into Ziklag believing his story would buy him a future.

His story killed him.

And before we move past that, we need to turn back one page.

First Samuel 31 has already told us how Saul died.

Saul was wounded by the Philistine archers. He asked his armor-bearer to kill him, but the armor-bearer was afraid.

So Saul fell on his own sword.

The armor-bearer saw that Saul was dead and fell on his sword beside him.

Then this Amalekite arrives in the next chapter claiming, "I killed him."

The narrator has given us every reason to distrust his version.

The most natural reading is that he found Saul's body, took the crown and armband, and wrote himself into the story on the way to Ziklag.

We are not told every step, so I will not pretend we are.

But we know this much:

David does not execute him because the narrator proved that he killed Saul.

David executes him because his own mouth claimed that he killed the Lord's anointed.

He thought he was telling David what David wanted to hear.

He thought pragmatism would pay.

It worked long enough to get him into the room.

Then it cost him everything.

That is what makes pragmatism dangerous.

What works is not necessarily what is faithful.

---

## Do Not Miss the Word "Amalekite"

Now do not move too quickly past the word *Amalekite*.

That word has been following Saul ever since the day he lost the kingdom.

First Samuel 15.

God commanded Saul to carry out judgment against Amalek.

Saul spared Agag and kept the best of the livestock.

Samuel told him, "To obey is better than sacrifice."

And because Saul rejected the word of the Lord, the Lord rejected Saul as king.

Then, on the night before Saul died, Samuel said it again.

First Samuel 28:

"Because you did not obey the Lord or carry out his fierce wrath against the Amalekites, the Lord has done this to you today."

The Philistines won the battle at Gilboa.

Saul fell on his own sword.

I am not telling you this messenger physically killed Saul.

But I am telling you that Saul's collapse began years earlier when he refused to obey God concerning Amalek.

Then Amalekites burned Ziklag.

And now an Amalekite walks into Ziklag carrying Saul's crown.

The man Saul was trying to kill never took his crown.

The man Saul never expected is carrying it.

Saul tried to keep the crown by killing David.

David refused to take the crown by killing Saul.

And in the end, the crown came to David without David ever laying a hand on him.

That is not sentimentality.

That is loyalty to the Lord and loyalty to Israel.

---

## What David Is Grieving

And now the chapter refuses to end with the sword.

David writes a lament.

He orders the people of Judah to learn it.

He is not merely grieving one person.

He is grieving Jonathan.

Jonathan was his brother by covenant.

Jonathan took off his robe and laid it on David's shoulders. He gave David his sword, his bow and his belt.

The son of the king recognized what God was doing before almost anybody else did.

Jonathan protected David when Saul wanted him dead.

He found David in the wilderness and strengthened his hand in God.

He looked at the man who would receive the throne and called him brother.

Now Jonathan is dead on Mount Gilboa.

David is grieving Saul too.

He does not have to pretend Saul was a good father-in-law.

He does not have to forget the spears, the caves or the years Saul hunted him.

But Saul was still the Lord's anointed.

God had poured oil on that man.

Saul was supposed to stand beneath the word of God and shepherd Israel.

He was supposed to protect the people, unite the tribes and lead the army of the Lord.

Now Israel's king is dead on a mountain.

You do not have to approve of everything a president has done to understand what it means when the President of the United States is assassinated.

People who did not vote for John F. Kennedy still grieved when he died because something larger than one man had been struck.

The office had been struck.

[Pause.]

And now Israel is exposed.

The army of the Lord has been shattered.

The people of God have been humiliated.

The Philistines are celebrating.

That is why David sings:

"Tell it not in Gath. Proclaim it not in the streets of Ashkelon, lest the daughters of the Philistines be glad."

David knows those streets.

He has lived among those people.

He knows what their celebration sounds like.

This is not David trying to cover Saul's sin.

The whole book has already told the truth about Saul.

David is refusing to turn Israel's catastrophe into Philistine entertainment.

He can tell the truth about Saul's failure without treating Saul's fall like something cheap.

That is the conflict in this chapter.

The Amalekite looks at Saul's death and sees a transaction.

David looks at Saul's death and sees a covenant catastrophe.

The Amalekite thinks the crown is something you grab.

David understands that the crown is something only God can give.

The kingdom does not come by seizing what God has promised.

---

## The AHA — The Crown Is Not His to Give

But do not clean David up too quickly.

For sixteen months, David has been living in Philistine territory and playing a dangerous game with Achish.

He lied about where he went.

He left no survivors who could tell the truth about him.

The text never says God commanded him to do that.

David had learned to survive by calculating, hiding and doing what worked.

He sometimes tried to manufacture survival.

But when the Amalekite laid that crown at his feet, David refused to manufacture succession.

He drew a covenant line.

The Amalekite possessed the crown.

But he did not possess the authority to make David king.

The crown was not a political prize to be handed out by a mercenary.

It was a divine weight to be placed in God's time, through God's direction and among God's people.

The Amalekite could carry the crown.

He could not confer the kingdom.

[Slow down.]

And look at how alone David is in this moment.

Samuel, the prophet who poured the oil, is dead.

[Pause.]

Saul, the king who wore the crown, is dead.

[Pause.]

Jonathan, the king's son who recognized David's future, is dead.

[Pause.]

Everybody David might have expected to stand beside the promise is disappearing.

But God is not dead.

The prophet died.

The word God spoke through him did not die.

The king died.

The anointing did not die.

Jonathan died.

The covenant faithfulness of God did not die.

The Amalekite believes Saul's death created David's future.

But Saul was never the author of David's future.

God was.

That is why the crown can lie at David's feet and he does not have to touch it.

When chapter 2 opens, David still does not crown himself.

He asks the Lord, "Should I go up?"

God sends him to Hebron.

The people of Judah anoint him there.

Later, all Israel comes to him.

The crown came to David anyway.

David did not refuse the crown.

He refused to receive it from the wrong hand, by the wrong means, at the wrong time.

Saul's death removed a threat.

It did not create the promise.

David's future did not depend upon enjoying his enemy's ruin.

It depended upon the faithfulness of God.

---

## The Gospel — The Son of David

And a thousand years later, the true Son of David faced the same temptation.

Satan showed Jesus the kingdoms of the world and offered them to Him if He would bow.

A kingdom without obedience.

A crown without a cross.

Jesus refused.

Later, when the crowds saw Him multiply bread, they tried to take Him by force and make Him their kind of king.

Jesus withdrew.

Jesus never refused to be King.

He refused to become King on their terms.

He refused every crown that bypassed the will of His Father.

Philippians 2 says that although He existed in the form of God, He did not use equality with God for His own advantage.

He emptied Himself.

He took the form of a servant.

He humbled Himself and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross.

When this world finally placed a crown upon His head, it was woven out of thorns.

They pressed it into His brow.

They mocked Him.

They crucified Him beneath a sign that said, "King of the Jews."

But He did not shed the blood of His enemies to seize a crown.

He shed His own blood to save His enemies.

Then God raised Him from the dead and crowned Him with glory and honor.

David shows us the shape of that King.

But David cannot be that King.

Later, David will take another man's wife and arrange another man's death.

David refused to shed Saul's blood to receive a kingdom, but one day he will shed Uriah's blood to take what he wants.

Even a man after God's own heart cannot give Israel the King it needs.

A man after God's own heart still needs the Son of David to give him a new heart.

We need the King who never seized.

The King who gave Himself.

The King whose promise still lives when everything around it has died.

The gospel according to the Amalekite is, "Your enemy is dead. Now you can reign."

But the gospel of Jesus Christ says, "The King died so His enemies could live."

---

## The Experiential Door — The Heart Is Still Beating

[Step away from the pulpit.]

[Lower your voice.]

Let me take you back to that phone call.

Rod Carew had spent his life doing things most people could never do.

But when his heart began to fail, there was nothing left for him to accomplish.

He could not hit his way out of it.

He could not earn another heartbeat.

All he could do was receive.

When the call came, it was the answer he had been praying for.

But before Rod's phone rang with good news, another family's phone had rung with the worst news of their lives.

Konrad Reuland was gone.

Rod Carew was going to live.

Both things were true at the same time.

Life for one family.

Loss for another.

Relief in one room.

Grief in another.

And when Rod learned whose heart was beating inside him, gratitude and grief were not merely living in the same chest.

They were beating in the same heart.

[Pause.]

David's situation is different, but the emotional collision is familiar.

There is a crown at his feet.

The road ahead has finally opened.

The man who hunted him cannot hunt him anymore.

But Jonathan is dead.

Saul is dead.

Israel has been humiliated.

David does not have to choose between believing God's promise and grieving all that has been lost.

He can do both.

He can grieve today.

And when God gives him the kingdom, he can receive it tomorrow.

Receiving what God gives next is not a betrayal of what you lost.

In Rod Carew's story, receiving life did not erase grief.

In David's story, trusting God did not require fake celebration.

Different situations.

The same human truth.

Grief does not require you to refuse life.

[Slow down.]

Some of you have been afraid to live again.

You have been afraid that laughing means you have forgotten.

Afraid that moving forward means it did not matter.

Afraid that relief means you never really loved them.

Afraid that healing means you are calling what happened good.

But the gospel does not ask you to call death good.

It tells you that death does not get the last word.

The gospel does not ask you to pretend the wound never happened.

It tells you that the wound does not get to determine your future.

The gospel does not rush you through your grief.

It meets you inside it with a living King.

Konrad Reuland did not choose the tragedy that took his life.

Jesus chose the cross.

"No one takes My life from Me. I lay it down."

He did not shed the blood of His enemies to seize His kingdom.

He shed His own blood to give life to His enemies.

And what did we bring?

Not a Hall of Fame resume.

Not three thousand hits.

Not enough goodness to earn another heartbeat.

We brought a failing heart.

And He gave us life.

[Pause.]

That is why we call it grace.

You did not earn it.

You did not arrange it.

You did not seize it from somebody else's hand.

You received it.

So you are allowed to grieve what was lost.

And you are allowed to receive the life God is giving you now.

Your joy does not dishonor your grief.

Your grief does not cancel your gratitude.

You can remember what was.

You can mourn what should have been.

And you can still walk into what God is giving next.

You are not betraying the past by living.

You are not betraying the person you lost by breathing.

You are not betraying the marriage you hoped for by healing.

You are not betraying the church that wounded you by finding your way back to God.

You are not betraying anybody by receiving life from the hands of Jesus.

But I did not tell you everything about Rod Carew's story.

When Rod received the transplant, he did not know whose heart was beating inside him.

He learned that later.

Years earlier, when Konrad Reuland was eleven years old, he had met Rod Carew.

Rod was his favorite athlete.

Konrad went home talking about the professional athlete he had met and telling his mother that he wanted to become one too.

And he did.

Years later, the heart of the boy who admired Rod Carew became the heart that kept Rod Carew alive.

[Pause.]

That connection does not explain Konrad's death.

It does not make his family's loss smaller.

But it brings us back to the feeling we have been tracing.

Not the same circumstances.

The same human truth.

David can lament and still walk toward Hebron when God sends him.

Rod can honor Konrad and keep living.

And you can grieve what ended while receiving what grace gives next.

[Let it settle.]

So what is wrong with us?

Nothing is wrong with you because tears came with your freedom.

Nothing is wrong with you because relief and sorrow arrived together.

Nothing is wrong with you because part of you still mourns what should have been.

Sometimes deliverance comes carrying grief.

Sometimes sorrow and gratitude arrive together.

Sometimes grief and grace beat in the same heart.

And the heart is still beating.

By the grace of God, the heart is still beating.

---

## Conclusion — Live With Open Hands

[Remain away from the pulpit.]

And if the heart is still beating, tomorrow has to look different.

Because the gospel does not only change what we believe about death.

It changes the way we live.

So here is what I want you to carry out of this room:

My future is in God's hands.

So mine can stay open.

[Open your hands.]

A closed hand grabs.

A closed hand holds on to bitterness.

A closed hand can even refuse a gift because receiving it feels like betrayal.

But an open hand does not have to seize.

It does not have to strike back.

And it is able to receive whatever God gives next.

So when you walk out of here, do three things.

Do not grab.

Do not gloat.

And do not stop living.

[Pause.]

Do not grab.

When a door opens because somebody else fell, slow down.

When you are tempted to force the opportunity, manipulate the conversation, make the phone call or tell the version of the story that makes you look necessary, stop.

Ask the Lord:

"Is this mine to receive, or am I trying to seize it?"

David had the crown at his feet.

But he still waited for God's direction.

If God has something for you, you do not have to climb over somebody's body to get it.

You do not have to compromise your character to secure your future.

God will never require you to sin in order to secure what He gives.

Do not grab.

And do not gloat.

The next time somebody brings you news about the person who hurt you, remember the Amalekite.

Everybody who carries news is not carrying good news.

Do not make another person's collapse your entertainment.

Do not forward the message.

Do not gather people around the wreckage.

Do not disguise celebration as a prayer request.

If wrongdoing must be reported to protect somebody, tell the truth to the people who can act.

But do not turn another person's ruin into a feast.

You can want justice without enjoying destruction.

You can thank God that the danger has ended without dancing around the grave.

Do not gloat.

And do not stop living.

When laughter comes back, let yourself laugh.

When you finally sleep through the night, thank God for the rest.

Make the counseling appointment.

Accept the help.

Fill out the application.

Walk into a healthy church again.

Sit down at the table with people who love you.

Receive what God is giving you now.

Moving forward does not mean what happened did not matter.

Healing does not erase the scar.

Rod Carew's new heartbeat did not erase Konrad Reuland's life.

It carried something of his life forward.

Your next chapter does not have to dishonor the chapter that closed.

You can grieve what was lost and still receive what grace is giving.

[Silent cue to the musician.]

Some of you came into this room with your hands clenched.

You are trying to force a future.

You are holding the person who hurt you.

You are holding so tightly to what was lost that you cannot receive what God is giving next.

I am not asking you to solve every feeling this morning.

I am asking you to place your future back into the hands of the King.

The King who did not seize.

The King who did not save Himself by destroying His enemies.

The King who opened His hands and let them drive nails through them.

Those wounded hands are holding your future now.

So you can open yours.

[Open your hands again.]

Do not grab.

Do not gloat.

Do not stop living.

Your grief is real.

Your deliverance is real.

And the grace of God is strong enough to carry them both.

My future is in God's hands.

So mine can stay open.

This altar is open.

---

## Closing Prayer

Lord, our future is in Your hands.

Open ours.

Take away the need to seize what You have not given.

Take away the desire to celebrate another person's fall.

And give us courage to receive the life Your grace is giving.

Teach us how to grieve without going back.

Teach us how to heal without pretending.

Teach us how to wait without grasping.

Thank You for the true King who did not seize His kingdom by taking the lives of His enemies, but gave His life to save them.

Thank You that the cross was not the last word.

Thank You that the grave could not hold Him.

And thank You that because He lives, grace is still beating in us.

In Jesus' name.

Amen.
