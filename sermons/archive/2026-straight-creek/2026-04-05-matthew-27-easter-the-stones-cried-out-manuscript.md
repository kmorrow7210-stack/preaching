**III. COLD OPEN — THE MINERS**

You can be seated.

On August the 5th, 2010, in the Atacama Desert in northern Chile, a copper and gold mine called San José collapsed. 700,000 tons of rock came crashing down and sealed the main tunnel. 33 miners were trapped 2,300 feet underground.

Now I want you to feel how deep that is. The Empire State Building is about 1,450 feet tall. These men were deeper than that. Below the surface of the earth. In the dark. In a refuge chamber about the size of a small apartment. And 700,000 tons of rock between them and the sky.

When the rescue teams tried to drill down to find them, the first drill broke. The rock was too hard. The geological surveys came back and the engineers said: the odds that these men are alive are about 2 percent. The Chilean government told the families to prepare for the worst. News agencies around the world ran the story as a tragedy. 33 men buried alive.

For 17 days, there was nothing. No signal. No communication. No sound coming from below the surface. Just silence. And a mountain of rock on top of them.

Everybody on the surface assumed they were in a grave.

That's the thing about sealed places. Once the rock is in position, everybody writes the ending. The conversation is over. The case is closed. Nobody's coming out of there.

That's what Rome thought, too.

**IV. THE SEAL — ROME'S LAST WORD**

For those of you who were here Palm Sunday I asked you a question. Did these stones cry?

We're going to answer it today. But first I've got to show you what happened between Friday and Sunday, because most people skip Saturday. And Saturday is where Rome gets its turn.

Jesus dies on Friday. The disciples scatter. Peter denies him three times. Judas is dead. The crowd that sang the coronation psalm on Palm Sunday is camped outside the city walls and doesn't even know there was a trial. The trial happened before dawn. By design. Every human voice that praised him has gone silent.

And then the chief priests and the Pharisees go to Pilate. And look at this ... Jesus told the disciples he would rise. He told them more than once. But on Saturday morning, they are silent. They're in shock, and trauma is real good at making us forget what was promised.

And on top of that — it's Passover. A Torah-observing Jew cannot travel from sundown Friday until sunup Sunday. They couldn't have gone to that tomb if they wanted to. The disciples are locked in place by their own law while Rome moves freely to seal the tomb and post a guard. 

But the Pharisees? They remembered exactly what Jesus said. Verbatim. "After three days I will rise." The people who didn't believe in Him were the ones who took his words seriously enough to mobilize the Roman military.

You don't deploy the Roman military against a group of fishermen hiding in an upper room because you're worried about grave robbers. Something about what Jesus said got under their skin deep enough to make them overreact. They couldn't name it. But their response named it for them.

So Pilate says, "You have a guard. Go. Make it as secure as you know how."

They rolled a massive stone against the opening. A lithos — a worked stone. They stretched a cord across it and pressed the Roman imperial seal into soft wax on that cord. And they posted an armed guard.

Let's talk about the seal. It wasn't a physical barrier. It was a wax cord. A child could snap it. But nobody would dare. Because the power of the Roman seal was never in the strength of the cord. It was in the jurisdiction behind it. 

That seal said: Caesar's authority governs this tomb. Breaking it is not vandalism. It's treason. And the penalty is execution.

Rome wasn't trying to physically contain Jesus. Rome was making a legal claim. This body is ours. This conversation is over. 

But God isn't through.

**V. THREE STONES, THREE CRIES**

Now for those of you who weren't here last Sunday, let me catch you up real quick.

The week before the crucifixion, Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a donkey. The crowd is shouting Psalm 118 — the coronation psalm. The Pharisees are terrified because if this keeps up, Rome is going to send cavalry. So they tell Jesus, "Rebuke your disciples."

And Jesus says this. Luke 19:40. "I tell you, if these keep silent, the stones will cry out."

That's not a worship song lyric. That's a prophetic indictment. And the grammar backs it up — it's future indicative, not subjunctive. He's not saying "might." He's saying "will." This coronation cannot be cancelled.

So here's the question I left you with last Sunday: Did the stones cry out?

Three stones. Three cries.

— — —

**Stone #1: Friday — The Foundation Cracks**

Go back with me to what we just read. Matthew 27:50.

Jesus cried out with a loud voice and yielded up his spirit. That word "yielded" — in the Greek it's aphiēmi. That's an active verb. He released it. He let it go. This is not a man dying against his will. This is a man choosing the exact moment of his own death. And the instant he releases his spirit, everything breaks loose. The veil tears. The earth shakes. The rocks split. The tombs open. One act. One push. Dominoes.

Now look at what splits. Matthew uses the word petra. That's not lithos — not a worked stone, not a building block. Petra is bedrock. The foundation underneath everything else. The rock that doesn't move because there's nothing below it to move to. And it cracked.

And the verb — "were split" — that's what scholars call a divine passive. The actor is unnamed because the actor is God. The bedrock didn't just crack. It was cracked. By whom? Matthew doesn't say. He doesn't have to.

The Father's response to watching his Son die was to tear the planet open.

Now see the shape of what happened. Two things tore at the same moment. The veil in the temple tore top to bottom — from the heaven side down to the earth side. Access to God, ripped open. And the petra split from the surface downward — into the realm of the dead.

One tore up. One tore down. Same moment. Same cause.

That's a cross.

And the man hanging at that intersection holds the keys to both.

Every voice that praised him on Palm Sunday had gone silent. The disciples fled. Peter denied him. The crowd that sang "Hosanna" didn't even know there was a trial. At 3 PM on Friday, not one human voice is speaking on his behalf.

But the petra — the foundation of the planet — split open at the death of its Creator. And a Roman centurion, a pagan soldier with no Torah and no prophets, stood in the middle of that earthquake and said, "Truly this was the Son of God."

And here's something Paul knew that I don't want you to miss. In 1 Corinthians 10:4, he writes that when Moses struck that rock in the wilderness and water gushed out to save the people — that rock was Christ. The rock Moses struck was always pointing to this moment. Jesus is the true Rock. And when the Roman soldier thrust his spear into the side of Jesus on that cross, John 19:34 says blood and water poured out. Moses struck the Rock and water gushed. The builders struck the Cornerstone — and grace gushed.

The bedrock preached a sermon. And a Roman heard it.

That's Cry #1. Judgment.

— — —

**Stone #2: The Sealed Stone**

Now we just talked about the seal. But I need to show you the irony that's buried in it, because it's too good to miss.

The prophet Habakkuk, hundreds of years earlier, says this: "The stone will cry out from the wall, and the beam from the woodwork will respond." In context, God is pronouncing judgment on a corrupt regime — one that built itself on the backs of the poor. And God says the building materials will testify against the builders.

The Pharisees and Rome are the builders here. They built a case. They built a seal. They built a guard detail. And the lithos — the worked stone they used to lock him away — is about to become the evidence of their failure.

They sealed the Stone behind a stone. And that building material is about to take the witness stand.

That's Cry #2. The world's last word. Or so they thought.

— — —

**Stone #3: Sunday Morning — The Announcement**

Second earthquake. But this one is different.

On Friday, the earth was shaken. Passive. Something happened to it. Creation reacting to the death of its Creator. But on Sunday, Matthew says "there was a great earthquake." Something showed up. Friday, the earth responded to a death. Sunday, something arrived.

An angel descends from heaven and the earthquake comes with him like a herald's trumpet. This is not creation mourning. This is the announcement of a King.

And look at the direction. On Friday, the crack went down — the petra split, the breach went into the underworld. Now on Sunday, the movement is reversed. Heaven opens and something comes down. But where does it come? To the exact same spot. The tomb is the intersection. The crack went down on Friday. Heaven comes down on Sunday. Both movements converge at the same stone.

The angel grabs the lithos that Rome sealed, the stone stamped with the emperor's name, the stone guarded by soldiers who would die if it moved — and the angel rolls it away.

But here's the detail I need you to see. This is only in Matthew. Mark doesn't have it. Luke doesn't have it. John doesn't have it. Only Matthew tells us what the angel does next.

He sits on it.

Not inside the tomb. Not beside the tomb. On the stone. In the open. Where everyone can see.

Why? Because in the ancient world, sitting on something was a claim of authority. Kings sit on thrones. Conquerors sit on the spoils. And Psalm 110 — the most quoted psalm in the New Testament — says this: "The LORD says to my Lord: Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool."

Rome's sealed stone was the last display of the empire's authority. And heaven's messenger sat on it like furniture.

Rome stamped this stone with the emperor's name. Heaven is using it as a footstool.

And look at the guards. They "trembled and became like dead men." The living guards play dead. The dead man inside is alive. Everything Rome built to contain this moment has been turned upside down.

Now — the stone was not rolled away for Jesus to get out. A risen, glorified body does not need a door. John 20 — he walks through locked walls. The stone was rolled away for the witnesses. So they could look inside and see for themselves.

And notice who those witnesses were. Women. In the first century, a woman's testimony was not admissible in court. If the disciples were fabricating a story, they would have put Peter or John at the tomb first. But all four gospels say it was the women. Why? Because that's what happened. Nobody would invent it that way.

The God who splits bedrock and breaks Roman seals is the same God who bypasses the powerful and entrusts the greatest news in human history to the people the culture cast aside.

And one more thing. Luke wrote both scenes. Luke 19:40 — Palm Sunday: "the stones will cry out." Luke 24:2 — Easter morning: "they found the stone rolled away." Same word. Lithos. Same author. The stone testified not with sound but with movement. Its displacement was its testimony.

The stones cried out. Cry #3. Victory. The seal is broken. The tomb is empty. And heaven is sitting on Rome's authority like it weighs nothing at all.

**VI. THE MOUNTAIN**

After the resurrection, Jesus meets his disciples on a mountain in Galilee. Matthew 28:16.

I need you to see what mountain this is. At the beginning of Matthew's gospel, Satan takes Jesus up on a mountain and shows him all the kingdoms of the world. "All this authority I will give you, if you fall down and worship me." Jesus says no.

Now at the end of Matthew's gospel, Jesus stands on a different mountain. Same offer. Different source. And he says: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me."

What Satan offered as a shortcut on the first mountain, the Father gave as a reward on the last mountain. And the path between those two mountains went through a cross. Through a cracked petra. Through a sealed lithos. Through an empty tomb.

And notice the verb. "Has been given." Not taken. Not seized. Given. By the Father. To the Son who was obedient unto death.

Now look at who he's talking to. Matthew says, "When they saw him they worshiped him. But some doubted." Some doubted. He could have left that out. Nobody would have known. But he left it in. Because those eleven men are standing on that mountain looking at the risen Christ and some of them still aren't sure about what they're seeing.

And Jesus doesn't wait for a show of hands. He doesn't pull the doubters aside. He looks at the whole group — worshipers and doubters, side by side — and he says: "Go."

The Great Commission is not a reward for certainty. It's a calling extended to people who showed up on the mountain with what they had. Bring your doubt. He'll bring the authority.

**VII. YOUR STONE**

I want to talk to you for a minute. Not as a preacher. As your pastor.

Every person in this room walked in here this morning carrying a stone.

Some of you have a stone of grief. You lost somebody. And the weight of it sits on you like a sealed tomb. You go to work. You smile. You say you're fine. But there's a stone over a part of your heart that nobody's been able to roll away.

Some of you have a stone of shame. Something you did. Something done to you. And somewhere along the way somebody rolled a rock over that part of your story and said, "Don't talk about it." And you've been living with that sealed tomb inside you ever since.

Some of you have a stone of doubt. You used to believe. Maybe you used to sit in this very church and feel something and now it's gone quiet. Some of the eleven doubted too. He commissioned them anyway.

And some of you have something deeper than all of that. The diagnosis. The marriage that died so quietly nobody noticed. The phone call from your kid that ended with a click and three years of silence. You sealed it. You posted a guard. You told yourself: that's over. Don't go back there.

And every day you walk past that tomb and you believe the seal.

I need you to hear this. The Roman seal was not a physical barrier. What made it powerful was that everyone believed in the authority behind it. And that's how the enemy works — not with unbreakable chains, but with the lie that the seal is legitimate. The thing holding you isn't the strength of the lock. It's your belief that nothing has the authority to break it.

But the same God who cracked the bedrock of Jerusalem on a Friday sent an angel on a Sunday morning who didn't even acknowledge that seal. He rolled the stone, sat down on it, and waited for the witnesses to arrive.

"Do not be afraid. He is not here. He is risen. Come and see."

Whatever stone is sitting over your life today — He didn't roll away the stone of death just to leave yours in place.

**VIII. WE ARE THE STONES**

I want to end by showing you where this story is going.

The stone motif doesn't stop at the empty tomb. It has one more destination.

About fifty days later, in Jerusalem, the Spirit falls. And a man stands up. A man Jesus renamed "The Rock." A man who went silent in a courtyard and denied knowing Jesus three times. Two months ago, he was hiding behind a locked door.

Peter opens his mouth on the day of Pentecost. And the rock cried out.

He preaches and 3,000 people are saved in a single day.

You want the depth of that? At Mount Sinai, when Moses came down with the tablets of stone — the law written on rock — 3,000 people died in judgment that day. The letter of the law carved in stone killed 3,000.

But at Pentecost, the Spirit speaking through a rock named Peter gave life to 3,000.

The letter kills. The Spirit gives life.

Peter — Petros — the rock that went quiet on Friday, cried out on Pentecost exactly the way Jesus said the stones would cry out on Palm Sunday.

And then Peter writes this. 1 Peter 2:5. "You yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house." Living stones. Built on the cornerstone the builders rejected.

We are the stones that cry out now.

**IX. CALLBACK — THE FÉNIX**

On day 17, a drill bit came back up from that mine in Chile. And taped to it was a piece of paper. Written in red marker: "Estamos bien en el refugio, los 33." We are fine in the shelter. All 33 of us.

They weren't dead. They were alive in the dark. And the world found out because a message came up from below.

69 days later, the rescue came. They built a capsule. Barely wider than a man's shoulders. And they named it — and church, I need you to hear this — they named the capsule Fénix. That's Spanish. You know what it means in English? Phoenix. Rising from the ashes. That's what they called the thing that was going to pull these men out of the ground.

But here's the thing that stopped me. The Fénix didn't rise. They lowered it. They sent it down. 2,300 feet into the earth. Into the dark. Into the place everybody on the surface had already called a grave. And it came back up with a man inside it. And then they sent it down again. And again. 33 times.

The world names its rescue "Phoenix" because rising is the only rescue we understand. You burn, you rise, you leave the ashes behind. But that's not how the Fénix worked. It didn't start by going up. It started by going down.

And church, that is exactly what Jesus did.

Before He ever rose from that tomb on Sunday morning, He descended. The petra cracked open on Friday and the breach went down. Peter writes that He went and made proclamation to the spirits in prison. Paul says in Ephesians 4: "He who ascended far above all the heavens — what does it mean except that he had also descended into the lower parts of the earth?"

He went down. Into the dark. Into the sealed place. Into the territory that death and the grave and every empire that ever stood had claimed as theirs. And He didn't go down empty. He went down with a decree. He went down with the keys. And He came back up — and He didn't come back up alone.

The Fénix went down 33 times and brought up 33 men.

Jesus went down once. And He's been bringing people out ever since. 3,000 at Pentecost. And He's still sending that capsule down. Every time the gospel is preached, every time an altar is opened, every time somebody in a dark place hears a voice that says "you're not dead yet" — that's the Fénix descending. That's the God who doesn't just rise from His own ashes. He descends into yours to bring you out.

The stones cried out. And we — we are the living proof that somebody went into the dark and brought us up.

I want us to do something together. Something Jesus asked us to do.

**X. COMMUNION — THE SCARS AND THE STONES**

[Ushers begin distributing communion]

While they're passing these out, I want you to see something.

In the Old Testament, when God delivered His people through the waters of the Jordan, they piled up twelve stones at a place called Gilgal. Memorial stones. So that when their children asked, "What do these stones mean?" — they could tell the story. Later, the prophet Samuel set up a stone called Ebenezer and declared, "Thus far the Lord has helped us."

Stones of remembrance. Stones that cry out across generations.

This morning, we don't look to a pile of rocks. We look to a table. And the table tells the same story the stones told. Something happened here. God delivered. God broke through. God rolled away what was in the way.

Now picture heaven for a moment. The Apostle John, in Revelation chapter 5, is looking for a conquering lion. He's been told the Lion of the tribe of Judah has triumphed. But when he looks — he sees a Lamb. Standing as though it had been slain.

It is Jesus. The King of Glory. Resurrected. Immortal. Seated above every power and principality. And He still has His scars.

In a place where there are no more tears, no more pain, no more mourning — the Creator is the only one in the room with wounds. In a world made entirely whole, He alone carries the marks of severe trauma.

Why would He keep them?

Because they are not flaws. They are not weakness. They are His trophies. They are the eternal proof that the darkness was real, that it had a cost, and that it did not win. His scars are the receipt.

[Pause — let the cups finish being distributed]

Look at what you're holding.

Now look back with me two days before the resurrection. Thursday night. Matthew 26. It's the Passover meal — the feast that celebrates God delivering His people from Egypt. Jesus and His twelve disciples are in an upper room in Jerusalem. And Jesus takes the bread, blesses it, breaks it, and gives it to them.

*"Take, eat; this is my body."* — Matthew 26:26

Then He takes the cup.

*"Drink of it, all of you, for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins."* — Matthew 26:27-28

He's at a Passover table, surrounded by men who don't yet know what's coming. He knows what's coming. And He takes the bread — the bread of deliverance — and He reframes the entire feast around His own body. The exodus from Egypt pointed here. Every Passover meal across fifteen centuries of Jewish history was building toward this Thursday night, this upper room, this table.

And then Paul picks it up. Nearly twenty years later, writing to the church at Corinth, he explains what Jesus meant. 1 Corinthians 11:26: *"For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes."*

You proclaim the Lord's death.

Every time we take communion, we are stones that cry out. Not with sound. With the act itself. We are testifying — in the same way that empty tomb testified — that something happened. That God broke through. That the seal was broken. That He is not in the grave.

Now look at what you're holding.

The bread. When we break this bread, we are remembering the moment the foundation of the earth broke. The petra cracked. The veil tore. The body of Jesus was broken — and in that breaking, every barrier between God and humanity shattered. The ceiling and the floor came off the human condition in the same moment.

The bread breaks the way the bedrock broke. Deliberately. Sovereignly. For you.

*"This is my body, which is broken for you. Do this in remembrance of me."*

[Break the bread. Eat together.]

Now the cup.

Remember what I showed you in Stone #1. Moses struck the rock in the wilderness and water gushed out. And Paul says that rock was Christ. The whole story was building toward a moment when the true Rock would be struck — and what would pour out wouldn't just be water.

John 19:34. The crucifixion is over. Jesus has cried "It is finished" and yielded up his spirit. And a soldier takes a spear and thrusts it into his side. And what comes out? Blood and water. Both. Together.

The stone the builders rejected cried "It is finished" — and grace gushed out.

This cup is that grace. The blood of the new covenant. Not a wax seal that an angel can sit on. This is the seal God pressed over your life with the blood of His own Son — and no power in heaven, on earth, or under the earth has the authority to break it. Rome's seal was wax. This seal is blood. And it holds forever.

*"This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me."* — 1 Corinthians 11:25

[Drink together.]

I told you about 33 miners sealed 2,300 feet underground. And a rescue capsule named Fénix that went down into the dark to bring them up.

You are holding the proof that the Fénix descended for you. The bread is the body that went down. The cup is the blood that sealed the rescue. And the scars on the Lamb in Revelation 5 tell you that He didn't forget what it cost. He wears it. In glory. Forever.

Some of you walked in here today carrying scars of your own. Grief. Betrayal. Shame. Loss. And somewhere along the way, the world — or maybe even the church — made you feel like you had to hide your brokenness to be acceptable.

But we serve a wounded healer. Our King did not erase His trauma. He carried it into glory. And if He did, then your scars do not disqualify you. Your wounds are not the end of your story. They can become the very places where His grace flows out to heal others.

You don't have to have it all figured out to be at this table. Some of the eleven doubted on the mountain and He commissioned them anyway. Come with what you have.

Roll the stones in this room today. Whatever is sealed up — break it open. Whatever tomb somebody's been living in — send your Spirit down. Roll it away. And let them see that it's empty. Let them see that You are alive. Let them see that Friday was not the end of the sentence.
