

## **Hook: Mark 1:9-13**

Last night in Baton Rouge, two football coaches stood on opposite ends of the same scoreboard. LSU fifty-one, Clemson ten.

The man who lost — Dabo Swinney — has two national championship trophies. He once went a whole season without losing a single game. And when they put a microphone in front of him afterward, the best he could offer was this: "The best thing about that game is it's over."

Two championships. And the only mercy he could name was the final whistle.

Now surely the winner slept well. He didn't. Lane Kiffin won by forty-one points and spent his press conference explaining himself — rehearsing what the critics would have said if he'd only won by a little. At halftime, ahead by four touchdowns, he was already dissatisfied: "This game should be thirty-eight to nothing." He knows how that world works. Thirteen years ago, another school fired him at the airport, in the middle of the night, on the way home from a loss.

So the loser needed it to end. And the winner couldn't enjoy it. Because in that world, a trophy is good until next Saturday. The film is already waiting. The next opponent is already circled. You're only as good as your latest win — and your latest win is just the demand to go earn the next one.

And you don't need a headset to live in that world. Some of us clocked into it last week. It's the review that resets every quarter. The sales number that goes back to zero on the first of the month. The parent lying awake, wondering if yesterday's patience will stretch to cover today. That quiet accounting we run in the dark: what have I done lately — and will it be enough tomorrow?

Into that world, Mark tells a story. Jesus comes up out of the Jordan — no miracles yet, no sermons, no crowds, nothing on the résumé — and the sky tears open, and a voice says: 

## ***"You are my beloved Son. With you I am well pleased."***

Beloved. Before we see him heal a single person, drive out a demon, preach a sermon.

And the water is still running down his face when the same Spirit that came down on him gentle as a dove *drives him out into the wilderness*. 

**Beloved in the water — and *immediately*, the wilderness**

## **MOVE 2 — COMPLICATE THE PROBLEM**

At the Jordan: 

1. Tthere is water.   
2. There is John.   
3. There are people.   
4. Heaven opens.   
5. The Spirit descends.   
6. The Father speaks.

***And then Mark says, immediately.***

The scenery changes.

1. Water becomes wilderness.  
2. The crowd disappears.  
3. John is gone.  
4. The voice from heaven goes quiet.  
5. And instead of people coming to repent, Jesus is there with the wild beasts — and with the adversary.

***That is some kind of whiplash.***

Have you ever had an immediately?

* You finally get the job — and then the doctor finds something on the scan.  
* You spend months praying for your child, think things are finally turning around, and the phone rings again.  
* You walk out of church on Sunday convinced God spoke directly to you, and by Monday morning you are sitting in the parking lot wondering where God went.  
* You finally get the finances under control — and the transmission goes out.  
* You think the marriage is getting better. You laugh again. You think, Maybe we are going to be okay. And then one conversation puts you right back in the wilderness.

And we have a way of reading those moments as a verdict.

* I must have missed God somewhere.  
* Maybe I made the wrong decision.  
* Maybe God is disappointed in me.  
* Maybe if I had prayed harder.  
* Maybe if I had been better.

But Jesus has not wandered into this wilderness.

**The Spirit drove Him there.**

So if the Father has just said, **“You are my beloved Son,”** why does the very next sentence put the Son in the wilderness with Satan?

## **MOVE 3 — FOLLOW THE CLUES**

One of the reasons I love Mark is that Mark will pull the curtain back.

Not because there are two different worlds — *a natural world down here and a spiritual world somewhere else* — but because there is one reality, and most of the time we can only see part of it.

At the Jordan, you can see the water. You can see John. You can see Jesus. You can see people coming to be baptized.

### **But Mark lets us see more.**

1. The heavens are torn open.  
2. The Spirit descends.  
3. The Father speaks.

Then in the wilderness: 

1. You can see the desert.   
2. You can see Jesus.   
3. You can see the wild animals.

### **But Mark lets us see more there too.**

1. The Spirit is at work.  
2. Satan is there.  
3. And before the scene is over, angels are ministering to Him.

Mark does this again and again. On the mountain you have **Jesus, Peter, James and John** — but then Jesus is transfigured, **Moses and Elijah appear**, a cloud overshadows them, and the voice speaks again: ***“This is my beloved Son. Listen to Him.”***

**Mark is telling the story with the curtain pulled back just enough for us to know that what we can see is not all that is happening.**

And then there is that word **Mark** uses in **1:12**: driveth.

**The Greek verb is *ekballō*** — *to drive out, thrust out, cast out*. Mark uses that verb elsewhere when something is being forcibly driven out. Here it is the Spirit who drives Jesus into the wilderness.

And if you go back to the Greek Old Testament, that word has been heard before.

**Genesis 3:24** says ***God drove Adam out of the garden.***

**Adam had been placed in abundance.**

1. A garden.  
2. Food everywhere.  
3. The presence of God.  
4. And when the tempter came, Adam failed — and Adam was driven out.

*Now Mark gives us another man.*

1. But this Man is not standing in a garden.  
2. The Spirit drives Him into a wilderness.  
3. And the tempter is waiting there too.

*Then there is Israel.*

God called Israel His son. Israel passed through the water. Israel entered the wilderness. Israel was tested there.

1. And God provided.  
2. Bread every morning.  
3. Water from a rock.  
4. A cloud by day.  
5. Fire by night.

*And still Egypt kept calling their name.*

1. “We remember the fish.”  
2. “We remember the cucumbers.”  
3. “We remember the melons.”  
4. “We remember the leeks and the onions and the garlic.”

**Freedom was in front of them, but Egypt was still inside them.**

A generation died in the wilderness. Another generation grew up with memories they had inherited from somebody else. They had stories of Egypt. Stories of the sea opening. Stories of Sinai. Stories of manna on the ground every morning.

And when they finally stood at the Jordan, before the water stopped and before Jericho fell, God said to **Joshua** 

## ***“Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel, that they may know that, as I was with Moses, so I will be with you.”***

Moses could not take them into the land. Joshua led them across.

But even Joshua did not finish the story.   
Enemies remained. Then came judges. Then kings. Then prophets.   
God had given them the law. God had given them the cloud and the fire and the manna — and still Israel kept turning away.

* They became unfaithful.  
* They became unjust.  
* They chased after other gods.  
* The temple was destroyed.  
* They were carried into exile.  
* They came home, but things were not the same.

### **And the story keeps asking the same question:**

Are we completely hopeless

## **MOVE 4 — THE RESOLUTION**

Then Jesus stands in the Jordan.

The heavens tear open.  
The Father identifies Him.  
The Father affirms Him.  
The Spirit rests upon Him.  
And the Spirit sends Him into the wilderness.

Adam failed in a garden.  
Israel failed in a wilderness.  
But Jesus walks into the wilderness with Satan waiting on Him — and Jesus comes out the other side faithful.

Mark does not give us the long dialogue that Matthew and Luke give us. Mark just tells us He was there forty days, tempted by Satan. Then Jesus comes out preaching: “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand.”

The faithful Son has come.

But the wilderness is not the last place His obedience will take Him.

Because by the end of Mark, the beloved Son is hanging on a cross.

And here Mark pulls the curtain back again.

At the beginning of the Gospel, the heavens are torn open.  
At the end of the Gospel, the temple veil is torn in two from top to bottom.

Same root word: torn.

At the Jordan, the Father says, “You are my beloved Son.”  
At the cross, a Roman centurion watches the way Jesus dies and says, “Truly this man was the Son of God.”

The first human character in Mark to say it outright does not say it after watching Jesus multiply bread. He does not say it after watching Jesus calm a storm. He does not say it on the mountain when glory shines through.

He says it while looking at a dead Man on a cross.

Because the victory of the beloved Son does not look like the victories this world knows how to count.

The world says you are only as good as your latest win.

Jesus shows us a victory that looks like surrender.  
A crown that looks like thorns.  
A throne that looks like a cross.  
A conquest that looks like death.

Our disobedience brought separation and death. The obedient, sinless Son entered all the way into our death — and remained faithful.

And when He died, the veil tore.

The story is no longer about whether we can perform enough to get ourselves behind the curtain. In the Son, the curtain has been opened.

## **MOVE 5 — RESPONSE / CONCLUSION**

And before Mark is finished, Jesus has already told His disciples what following this kind of King looks like:

## ***“If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me.”***

Later He even speaks of His coming suffering as a baptism: “Can ye be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with?”

Mark never stops in chapter 1 to explain why the sinless Jesus entered a baptism of repentance. But He does put the sinless Son down in the water among the people He came to save. And later the New Testament will say that we are baptized into His death and raised to walk in newness of life, and Paul will speak about the fellowship of His sufferings.

He identifies with us — and then calls us to identify with Him.

And Revelation pulls the curtain back one more time.

There is the dragon.  
There is the accuser.  
There is the Lamb.  
There are the people of God.

And how do they overcome?

By the blood of the Lamb.  
By the word of their testimony.  
And because they loved not their lives unto the death.

The cross that looked like defeat was the way the Lamb conquered.

So how do we overcome?

Not by proving ourselves every Saturday.  
Not by stacking enough wins that nobody can question us.  
Not by avoiding every wilderness.

We overcome because of the blood of the Lamb, and we remain faithful to the testimony even when faithfulness costs us something.

That means I need to be careful about the way I interpret the wilderness.

Not every hardship is a divine assignment. Mark does not give me permission to call every tragedy something God sent.

But Mark does destroy one equation:

Wilderness does not equal abandonment.

Jesus was beloved before He entered it.  
The Spirit was with Him in it.  
Satan was there — but Satan was not the only one there.  
The angels were there.  
And the Father's word over the Jordan had not expired because the scenery changed.

Some of us have spent the whole wilderness trying to earn again what the Father already spoke over us in Christ.

Maybe the question is not, “What did I do wrong to end up here?”

Maybe sometimes the question is, “What if being here does not mean He has left me?”

What if the wilderness does not mean you have been disowned?

What if sometimes the wilderness means you have been deployed?

The world says you are only as good as your latest win.

But the gospel says the beloved Son was beloved before the wilderness, faithful through the wilderness, faithful all the way to the cross — and the cross was not His defeat.

It was His victory.

And because we belong to Him, we do not have to spend our lives trying to earn the Father's love one more win at a time.
