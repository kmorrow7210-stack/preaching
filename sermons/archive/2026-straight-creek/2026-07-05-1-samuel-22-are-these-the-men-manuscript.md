Philadelphia. Summer of 1776\. The kind of heat that just sits on a room and doesn't move.

Fifty-six men are about to sign their names to a piece of parchment.

These are not outcasts. These are not men with nothing to lose. They are wealthy merchants. Elite lawyers. Plantation owners. Men with land, standing, and estates that took a lifetime to build.

And by putting their names on that page, they are confessing to high treason against the Crown.

If this war is lost, that signature is not a statement. It is a rope. And it does not stop with the man who signs it — his family loses the land, the house, everything, right along with him.

When it comes time for Stephen Hopkins to sign, his hand is shaking. Palsy. Everybody in the room can see it.

He signs anyway. Holds his right hand steady with his left, and says, "My hand trembles, but my heart does not."

That's the signers. Elite men, betting everything they had on a piece of paper.

But once that paper was signed, somebody still had to go hold the line for it.

That fell to the Continental Army. And the Continental Army was not a polished fighting machine. These were poor men. Starving men. Freezing men. Farmers and laborers who had a lot more experience with a pitchfork than a bayonet.

September 15, 1776\. Kip's Bay.

British ships anchored close enough to shore that the men on the beach could read the names painted on the hulls. An hour of bombardment, and then British and Hessian troops came through the smoke.

The American line broke. Men ran for their lives.

George Washington — a man who almost never lost his composure — rode straight into the panic, trying to make men stand who would not stand. And according to the accounts, he threw his hat down and cried out, "Are these the men with whom I am to defend America?"

So here's the picture.

On one side, elite men signing what amounted to their own death warrant, with a shaking hand.

On the other side, poor men running from cannon fire they had no way to survive.

Both afraid. Both risking everything. Both, in their own way, about to step into a cave.

Which brings me to our text this morning. Would you stand with me as we read 1 Samuel 22:1-5?

In September 1780, three militiamen stopped a British officer near Tarrytown, New York. His name was John André. When they searched him, they found papers hidden in his boot. The plans to West Point. The layout. The weak points. Everything the British would need to take it.

The plans were written in a hand every one of us has heard of. Benedict Arnold.

Washington loved Arnold. Thought he was the best combat general he had. When Congress passed him over for promotion and dragged him through a court-martial, Washington shielded him everywhere he could. And then Washington handed him the most important fort in America. West Point. The key to the whole Hudson.

And Arnold sold it. Sold the fort, and very nearly the whole war, to the enemy. The man Washington trusted most is the man who almost ended it all.

That is what the men of Keilah were ready to do to David. He bled for that city. And the moment Saul turned up the heat, they were ready to hand the man who saved them over to the man hunting him.

So what gave these four hundred, then six hundred, men the resolve to keep gathering to David.

And what gave a farmer from Virginia the resolve to lead an army against an empire.

Nine days after the worst defeat the British Army suffered that century, a twenty-three-year-old officer named George Washington sat down and wrote a letter to his brother. Roughly two out of every three men who crossed the river that morning at the Monongahela were killed or wounded in under four hours. The officer corps was almost wiped out.

And Washington wrote this:

By the All-powerful Dispensations of Providence, I have been protected beyond all human probability or expectation, for I had four Bullets through my Coat, and two Horses shot under me, yet escaped unhurt, although Death was leveling my Companions on every side of me.

That same month, a Virginia pastor named Samuel Davies preached a sermon to a company of volunteers. In a footnote to that printed sermon, he wrote this about the young officer who'd survived Monongahela:

That Heroic Youth Col. Washington, whom I cannot but hope Providence has hitherto preserved in so signal a manner, for some important Service to his Country.

Twenty-two years later, in the middle of the Revolution, somebody mailed Washington that old footnote. He read a preacher's guess about his own life from half a lifetime earlier, arriving right in the middle of the very war that guess had been about all along.

Now hear me on this, because it matters. We are not saying God loved Washington more than the men who fell around him on that field. Scripture will not let us say that, and neither will I. Jesus himself refused to say the people a tower fell on in Siloam were somehow more guilty than everybody else. The same holds in reverse. Survival is not a verdict. It never has been.

What we can say is what Washington himself said, twenty-one years later, in the middle of this very revolution, writing to a fellow officer:

The same Providence that protected us upon those occasions will, I hope, continue his Mercies, and make us happy Instruments in restoring Peace and Liberty to this once favoured, but now distressed Country.

That's about as close as you'll get to Washington's own definition of Providence. Not luck. Not fate. Not a God who winds the clock and walks away. An active, personal, ongoing Mercy, governing things you cannot yet see the shape of.

And here's what that means for us.

It means though we walk through the valley of the shadow of death, God is with us.

It means if we ascend to the heavens, He is there. If we make our bed in the depths, He is there.

There is no mountain too high, no river too wide, no valley too deep to keep Him away from you.

You have a King.

And He is not a tyrant.

He is a King who was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief. Betrayed by one of His own. Abandoned by the rest when they scattered and fled. Hunted, the same way Saul hunted David, the same way the Crown hunted every man who signed his own name to that parchment.

And in all of it, He showed us exactly who He is.

Emmanuel.

God with us.
