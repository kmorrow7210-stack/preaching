# The Richest Man in Jericho

**Subtitle (working):** When Mr. Potter Got Saved
**Text:** Luke 19:1-10
**Date:** Sunday Evening, May 3, 2026
**Venue:** Blue Pond Barn (Guest)
**Big Idea:** Wealth, position, and reputation cannot make a man rich. Only grace can. Zacchaeus climbs past everything the world calls greatness so he can see Jesus, and grace meets him in the branches and rewrites the ledger of his life.
**Central Question:** What makes a man actually rich, and which rich man are you on your way to becoming?

## II. Bedford Falls

I want to start somewhere most of you have already been. Bedford Falls. The town in It's a Wonderful Life. We've all watched it. Most of us have watched it twelve times. Some of us watched it again last Christmas because that's just what you do.

There are two rich men in Bedford Falls.

The first one is Mr. Potter. He owns the bank. He owns most of the property in town. He sits in his chair and pulls strings, and the whole movie is set up for us to want him to lose. He is mean. He is small. He has all the money and none of the friends. And the part of the movie that has always stuck in my throat a little is that the credits roll on Mr. Potter and he is still Mr. Potter. He never repents. He never changes. He keeps the eight thousand dollars. The angel doesn't visit him. Frank Capra was not interested in saving Mr. Potter. Frank Capra was interested in saving George.

The second rich man is George Bailey.

Now George doesn't look rich. George never made it to college. George never got to travel. George ran a savings and loan in a small town. He gave the honeymoon money away the day of his honeymoon. He watched his little brother get the life George had been planning for himself. George spent his entire life giving things up. And at the end of the movie, his brother Harry stands up at the table, in the same room with Sam Wainwright who actually does have money, and he says, "To my big brother George, the richest man in town."

The richest man in town.

The whole movie is asking a question. Not a deep theological question. Just a real one. What makes a man actually rich? Because Bedford Falls has two rich men, and at the end of the movie, only one of them is in the room with people who love him.

The book of Luke is asking the same question.

In Luke chapter 18, just one chapter before our story, a rich young ruler walks up to Jesus. He has the money. He has the power. He has the religious resume. He asks Jesus the right question, listens to the answer, and walks away sad because he had great possessions. Same as Mr. Potter. Walks off into the sunset with all his stuff. Frank Capra did not write his rescue. Luke did not write his rescue either. The man kept the money and lost the moment.

And then in chapter 19, one chapter later, Luke walks us into Jericho. And Luke says, behold, there was a man named Zacchaeus. He was a chief tax collector, and he was rich.

Same diagnosis. Different ending.

So let's go to Jericho.

## III. The Crowd That Wouldn't Move

Picture Jericho with me. It is one of the oldest continually inhabited cities in the world. It sits about 850 feet below sea level, which means it is hot. Like Alabama in August hot. But it sits at the base of the Judean wilderness next to the Jordan River, and it has water year round. Because of all that water, palm trees and balsam trees grow everywhere. The Romans called it the City of Palms. The air smelled like perfume because Jericho was where the high-end balsam oil was produced and shipped out to Rome.

It was a trade gateway. Anything moving from the east into Jerusalem came through Jericho first. Caravans. Merchants. Spices. Silk. Pilgrims headed up for Passover. Anything that could be taxed came across that road. Hasmonean kings built winter palaces in Jericho. Herod the Great built one. The wealthy had their villas there. Pools. Bathhouses. Gardens. Orchards. Royal luxury at the edge of a desert.

And right there in the middle of the trade route, Rome put a customs station. Because if anything was moving through Jericho, Rome wanted a cut of it. Rome's tax system was complicated. It worked through contracts, local officials, tolls, customs, and layers of collection. The man at the top of the local layer in Jericho was called the chief tax collector.

His name was Zacchaeus.

I want you to feel the weight of that name. Zacchaeus's name in Hebrew is Zakkai. And Zakkai means pure. Innocent. Clean. His mama named him pure. And he grew up to become the most compromised man in town. Every time somebody said his name in Jericho, it was an accusation. Pure. Clean. Innocent. The man who collected taxes for the empire that crushed his own people.

He has betrayed his nation. He has collaborated with the occupier. He has made his money on the backs of his neighbors. The Pharisees won't eat with him. The synagogue won't have him. The poor curse him under their breath when he walks past. The rich tolerate him because he is useful. Nobody loves him.

And then word comes through town. Jesus is coming. The Jesus. The healer. The teacher. The one who let a tax collector named Matthew follow Him. Zacchaeus has heard about Matthew. He knows this Jesus has friends who do what Zacchaeus does. And something in his chest moves.

He has to see Him.

Not has to talk to Him. Not has to touch Him. Just has to see Him. One look. One glance.

So he goes out into the street. And here is where I want you to slow down with me, because the text gives us a detail we read past every single time. Luke says he could not see Jesus because of the crowd, for he was small in stature.

Now we always preach that like the problem was Zacchaeus's height. But listen to what Luke is actually telling us. The crowd is the problem. In an honor-conscious culture, when an important man wanted to see something, the crowd parted. People gave space to power. The way the line at the buffet opens up when somebody important walks up. That is the way crowds worked in the ancient world. Honor moved bodies.

But not for Zacchaeus.

The crowd will not move for him.

You catch what Luke is doing? Luke is preaching with the crowd's body language before Jesus opens His mouth. The chief tax collector. The richest man in Jericho. Standing on his tiptoes in the back of his own town. Nobody steps aside. Nobody nudges his neighbor and says "hey, let the man through." Zacchaeus has the title and the bank account and the Roman backing, and he cannot see over the shoulders of the people who hate him.

The crowd is the verdict on his life.

You ever been in a room and known you weren't welcome? You ever walked into a place and felt every shoulder turn just slightly away from you? That is Jericho on the day Jesus comes through. And the man we are about to meet has been living in that crowd for years.

That is when Zacchaeus does two things a man of his position should never do.

He runs.

In an honor-conscious culture, this is not how a dignified wealthy man wants to be seen. Running was for slaves. Running was for children. A man with a position kept his pace. But Zacchaeus has stopped caring about his position because his position has stopped giving him anything he wanted. So he picks up his expensive robes and he sprints down the road ahead of Jesus.

And then he climbs.

He sees a sycamore tree up ahead. The Greek word is sycomorea. The fig-mulberry. A big tree with low spreading branches. Climbable. Even a short man could pull himself up. But you do not climb a tree as a grown man in Jericho. Especially not as the chief tax collector. The picture of a wealthy government official scrambling up a tree like a child trying to see a parade is humiliating. People are going to laugh. The crowd is going to point. His clothes will be filthy. His position will look ridiculous.

He doesn't care.

Because here is the secret of Zacchaeus the Holy Spirit wants us to see tonight. The man had already lost his dignity. He was the only one who knew how much. The crowd thought they knew his shame. They didn't know the half of it. He had been carrying the real shame around for years. So losing one more piece of dignity by climbing a tree was not a cost. It was a relief.

Let me say that line again so you can sit with it. The sycamore is not just where Zacchaeus sees Jesus. The sycamore is where Zacchaeus lets Jericho see him.

He stops pretending. He stops fighting. He stops trying to keep the appearance up. And in that branch, with his fancy robes hiked up around his knees and the whole town staring at him, for the first time in years, the man we see is the man he actually is.

And then the strangest thing happens.

Jesus stops.

Jesus looks up.

Jesus calls him by name.

"Zacchaeus, hurry and come down. For I must stay at your house today."

Now slow down. This is the chief tax collector. He has been a name in this town for years, but only as a curse. People say his name through their teeth. Mothers tell their children, don't grow up to be Zacchaeus. Husbands grumble his name when the bill comes due. He has heard his own name shouted in arguments and whispered in mockery his whole adult life.

And then Jesus calls his name like it was a blessing.

The version his mama gave him. The version the Father has been calling out since before Zacchaeus was born. Zacchaeus. Pure. Clean. Innocent. The name nobody had said with kindness in years. Jesus said it in front of the whole town.

And then Jesus said, "I must stay at your house today."

The Greek word is dei. Must. Necessary. It is not optional. It is not a maybe. Jesus is not asking for a favor. Jesus is announcing a divine appointment that Zacchaeus did not know he had on the calendar. I am coming to your house. I am eating at your table. I am crossing the threshold every other religious leader in this town has crossed off the map.

And the crowd loses their minds. The text says they all grumbled. Every one of them. The same crowd that wouldn't make space for Zacchaeus to see the parade is suddenly very offended that Jesus stopped the parade for him. He has gone to be the guest of a man who is a sinner. As if that was not the whole point of His coming.

## IV. The Pattern Behind the Story

Now church, lean in for a minute. Because something is happening in Jericho that goes way deeper than one rich man getting saved.

You remember Jericho's history. Joshua chapter 6. The Israelites march around the city. The walls come down. And Joshua pronounces a curse over the city. Cursed before the Lord be the man who rises up and rebuilds this city Jericho. Jericho was a cursed city. It was the first city of conquest. Every Jew listening to this story knew that.

And now, fifteen hundred years later, the Son of God walks through that cursed city. He does not curse it. He blesses it. He looks up into a tree and says, "today salvation has come to this house." The cursed city becomes the city of salvation. Not all at once. One house at a time. One Zacchaeus at a time.

And Jesus is doing in Jericho what He has been doing since the garden.

Go all the way back. Genesis 3. Adam has sinned. Adam is hiding. He is in the trees. And what does God say? He walks into the garden in the cool of the day, and He says, "Where are you?" God did not lose Adam. God knew exactly where Adam was. The question was not for God's information. The question was for Adam's heart. Where are you? Come out from where you are hiding.

Here in Luke 19, the Son of the same God walks into a town. He looks up into a tree at a man hiding from his own life. He calls him by name. Same God. Same voice. Same pursuit.

The whole gospel is God walking through the city looking for the one in the tree.

In Revelation 3, the same Jesus says, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him and he with me." Y'all hear that? That is Zacchaeus. That is exactly what happened in Jericho. Jesus stood at the door of the most compromised house in town and said, I am coming in. I am eating at your table. The way Jesus saved Zacchaeus is the way He still saves people. He stands at the door. He knocks. He calls. He invites Himself in. Not to condemn the house. To change the house.

And notice what Zacchaeus does. He receives Jesus joyfully. He does not negotiate. He does not ask for time. He does not say, give me a week to clean up, give me a chance to put on a different shirt. He comes down out of the tree as fast as he climbed up. And then he stands in front of his own door, in front of the whole town, in front of Jesus, and he opens the books.

"Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor. And if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold."

Now slow down on this, because the math matters.

Torah law required restitution for fraud. If you cheated somebody, you paid the principal back plus a fifth. Twenty percent on top. That was the standard. That was what was required.

Zacchaeus is not paying twenty percent. Zacchaeus is paying four hundred percent.

In Exodus 22, fourfold is the restitution required of a man who steals a sheep and slaughters it. Fourfold is thief-level repayment. The kind of repayment a man owes when the law has caught him red-handed with somebody else's livestock in his pot.

Y'all hear what Zacchaeus is doing? He is not just exceeding the requirement. He is voluntarily classifying himself as a thief. He is opening his own ledger and writing the word stolen across the top of every line he can remember. Jesus did not have to convict him. Zacchaeus convicts himself in front of the whole town. Charge me as a robber, Lord. Whatever I took, I will pay back like the law catches a thief in the act.

That is not religion. That is repentance. That is grace hitting the books.

Salvation comes to the house, and suddenly the money has to move.

I want to give you a line tonight and I want you to take it home. Zacchaeus's repentance had a forwarding address. The poor. The defrauded. The neighbors his old life had stepped over to get to the top. Half his goods to the poor. Fourfold to anybody he had cheated. Every dollar he gave away that day had a name on it.

That is what live faith looks like. It produces fruit. Not as the down payment for salvation. As the receipt.

Now I want to be careful, because some of y'all are going to hear this and panic. You are going to think, well I got saved last year and I have not given half my goods to the poor. Don't panic. Read the text again. The change is real, but the change is also formed. Zacchaeus's first response is immediate, but his whole life is going to be formed by this moment for the rest of his days. Some of what God does in us is in a single moment at the altar. Some of what God does in us is over the course of decades of walking with Him. Both are real. Both are grace. Change is not always forced into a thirty-second decision. Sometimes it is formed over thirty years of faithfulness. But the seed of it always begins the day Jesus calls your name.

And then Jesus says the line that holds the whole story together. Verse 10. For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.

That is the thesis. Jesus did not stumble into Jericho. Jesus did not happen to look up. Jesus came through Jericho on purpose because there was a lost man in a tree who needed to be found. The verb is zēteō. To search out. To actively look for. Jesus is on a search and rescue mission. He is not waiting for the lost to find Him. He is walking through cursed cities. He is looking up into trees. He is calling the names of men nobody else wanted to claim.

Now church, come back to Bedford Falls with me one more time. Because we have everything we need now to see the whole thing.

Bedford Falls had two rich men. So does the gospel of Luke.

Luke 18, the rich young ruler. He came up to Jesus with his dignity intact. He came with his head held high. He came with his religious resume in his pocket. He had everything to lose, and he could not let it go. He walked up to Jesus a wealthy man and walked away a wealthy man, and he was sad. Because his wealth was the thing he could not climb past. That is Mr. Potter. That is exactly the man Mr. Potter grows up to be. Has the right answer. Walks away. Keeps the money. Sits in the chair. Credits roll. Nothing changes.

But Luke 19. Zacchaeus. Came to Jesus the only way he knew how. Up a tree. With his robes hiked up. Looking like a fool. Past his pride. Past his shame. Past his reputation. Past his fear of the crowd. And Jesus saw him there, called him by name, and from that day forward Zacchaeus spent the rest of his life coming down. Coming down to the poor. Coming down to the defrauded. Coming down to the neighbors he used to step over.

That is George Bailey. That is the man who, the day after the tree, and the year after the tree, and the decade after the tree, kept finding people to come down for. Kept giving the honeymoon money away. Kept opening the books. Kept making space at the table. And if you had stood at his funeral fifteen years later and listened to who showed up, you would have seen the same scene Bedford Falls saw at the end of the movie. The whole town. The poor. The defrauded. The neighbors. Standing in his house lifting a glass to the richest man in Jericho.

That is the whole sermon. The Rich Young Ruler is what wealth becomes when grace never gets the books. Zacchaeus is what wealth becomes when grace finally does.

## V. The Altar Call

So church, here is the question I want to leave you with tonight. Two questions actually. And then I want to open this altar.

The first question. Who in your life have you written off as a Mr. Potter? Who have you decided is too far gone, too mean, too proud, too compromised, too far down the road of wealth or influence or addiction or anger for grace to ever touch? The estranged brother. The boss. The neighbor. The ex-husband. The cousin nobody invites to Thanksgiving. The father who never came home. The friend who burned every bridge. Jesus came to seek and to save the lost, and your list of people too lost to seek is shorter than His. He walked into the cursed city of Jericho on purpose for a man like Zacchaeus. He has been walking into harder cities for harder men for two thousand years.

The second question is the one that might do the work tonight. What if you are the Mr. Potter? What if you have spent your life building something good on paper that does not feel like anything when you put your head on the pillow at night? What if you have all the money or all the position or all the religion or all the reputation, and you cannot remember the last time you felt small enough to be loved?

Some of y'all came in here tonight and you have been hiding in the branches a long time. The tree was never about getting closer to Jesus. The tree was about staying just close enough to look without being seen. And Jesus is walking through your Jericho tonight and He is looking up. He is calling your name. The real one. The one the Father gave you. The one He has been waiting to call all your life.

He is saying, come down. Come down out of the tree. Come down from the position. Come down from the image. Come down from the hiding place. I must stay at your house today.

Behold, He stands at the door and knocks. The door of your marriage. The door of your children's room. The door of your bank account. The door of your secret folder on your phone. The door you have not opened to Him because you were afraid of what He would see when He came in.

He already knows.

He has heard the recording. He has read the text. He has seen what the Ring camera caught. He has watched the version of you that you have been hiding from the rest of the world. He still came through Jericho looking for you. He still looked up. He still called your name.

Come down.

This altar is open tonight. Come down out of the tree. Come down from your dignity. Come down from your reputation. Come down from your shame. Come down and let Him in.

If you have been climbing a tree all your life trying to see Jesus, come down tonight and let Him come to your house. If you have been hiding in the branches because you didn't think you were worth looking for, come down tonight and let the Son of Man finish what He came to do. He came to seek. He came to save. And He did not skip your tree.

Maybe you've been in church for twenty years and you have never come down. Tonight is your night. Maybe you are visiting and tonight is the first night you've heard your name called. Tonight is your night. Maybe you are saved and walking with God but there is a branch you have not let go of yet, a piece of pride you've been holding onto like Zacchaeus held onto his money. Tonight is your night to let it go.

The altar is open. Come down. Let grace change the house. Let salvation reach the books. Let your repentance find a forwarding address. The poor. The defrauded. The neighbors your old life stepped over to get to the top.

And when the day comes that they read your eulogy, let the people in the room agree that the line was right.

Let's pray.

Father God, You said the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost. Lord, You did not skip a single tree in Jericho, and You have not skipped a single tree in this barn tonight. God, I pray for the man, for the woman, for the teenager in this room who has been hiding in the branches for too long. Call their name tonight Lord. Let them hear it. Let them hear the version You have been speaking over them since before they were born. Give them the courage to come down. Give them the courage to stop pretending. Give them the courage to let You into the house.

Lord, change the house tonight. Not just the heart. The whole house. The marriage. The bank account. The phone. The calendar. The relationships. Lord, do what You did in Zacchaeus. Send him out into the morning a different man than he was when he climbed into the tree.

We thank You that grace pursues. We thank You that grace seeks. We thank You that grace walks into our cities and looks up into our trees and calls us by name. Have Your way tonight Lord. In Jesus' name, and the church said Amen.
