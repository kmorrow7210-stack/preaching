## Movement 3: Re-Narrate the Text

  

### A barren woman in a barren nation

  

Now before we meet Hannah, you have to understand the world she's living in.

  

1 Samuel chronologically follows the book of Judges. And you know how the book of Judges ends. It ends with that line that hangs over the whole period like a storm cloud. **"In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes."** That's the world. Spiritually, the nation is a wreck. The Philistines have their chariots headed their direction. The tribes are fractured. And the worship center at Shiloh, the place where the ark of God sat, the place that was supposed to be the beating heart of the nation, the place where heaven was supposed to touch earth, had gone rotten at the center.

  

Look who's running it. Eli is the old priest, and his eyes are going dim, and that's not just a medical note, that's a picture. The man who's supposed to see for the nation can't see. And his two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, they're the ones really running the temple like it's a house of ill repute. The priests were stealing the meat off the people's sacrifices and fornicating with the women who served at the tabernacle. The house of God had become a place where the people in charge oppressed those who came to worship.

  

So that's the nation. Religious activity, but no living word. A full parking lot and an empty sanctuary. Later on the writer is going to tell us flat out, "the word of the LORD was rare in those days." God's not saying a whole lot. Not because God ran out of things to say. Because the people quit listening.

  

Now. Into that barren nation, the camera zooms in on one barren woman.

  

Verse 2. There's a man named Elkanah, and he's got two wives. Already you know there's trouble, because every time you see two wives in the Bible you can just go ahead and brace yourself. One wife is named Peninnah, and Peninnah has got children. Houseful of them. And the other wife is Hannah, and verse 2 ends with five words that carried a lifetime of pain in that culture: **"but Hannah had no children."

**

For a woman in the ancient world, this was not just a private sadness. In that culture a woman's worth, her security, her future, her standing in the community, all of it was tied to whether she could bear a son. No son meant no provision when she got old. No son meant the family name died. People looked at a barren woman and they assumed God was against her. So Hannah is not just sad. Hannah is a woman the whole world has labeled a failure, through no fault of her own.

  

And the Bible says, in verse 5, **"the LORD had closed her womb."** Now hold on, because that's a hard sentence. It says the Lord did it. Hannah's empty arms were not an accident. They were not God forgetting. Somehow, in a way she could not see yet, her emptiness was God's plan.

  

But it gets worse, because Hannah's not allowed to grieve in peace. Verse 6 says her rival, that's Peninnah, **"used to provoke her grievously to irritate her."** Other translations say she would needle her, mock her, year after year. Picture this. Every year the family goes up to Shiloh to worship. It's supposed to be the holy trip, the spiritual high point of the year. And every year Peninnah uses the holiest trip of the year to twist the knife. Right there on the way to church. Reminding Hannah, in front of everybody, of the one thing she can't have.

  

And the Bible says Hannah wept and she would not eat.

  

Now Elkanah, bless his heart, her husband loves her. He really does. But Elkanah says one of those things that men say when they're trying to help and they make it worse. He looks at his weeping wife and he says, verse 8, **"Hannah, why do you weep? Why don't you eat? Why is your heart sad? Am I not more to you than ten sons?"**

  

Fellas, write this down. That is not the line. That is never the line. Hannah doesn't need to be reminded that she's loved by Elkanah.

  

And here's the connection the writer wants you to feel. Hannah's closed womb is a living picture of the whole nation. A nation that looks dead. A people that can't produce. A barren woman in a barren nation, and everybody assume"s God has gone silent and gone away.

  

But God is about to do what God loves to do. God is about to start the rescue of an entire nation in the most overlooked person in the story.

  

### A praying woman in a compromised sanctuary

  

So watch what Hannah does.

  

Verse 9. After they eat, Hannah gets up and goes to the house of the LORD—the very place that had been compromised, run by those corrupt sons and presided over by an old priest whose eyes had gone dim. Frankly, if anybody had a good reason to say "what's the point, that church is a mess, I'm staying home," it was Hannah.

  

But she goes anyway.

  

And verse 10, she was deeply distressed and prayed to the LORD and wept bitterly." The Hebrew there is heavy. It's **"bitterness of soul."** This is gut-level. This is the ugly cry. This is everything she's been holding for years coming up out of her at the altar.

  

And then she makes a vow, verse 11, and look at the title she uses for God. She says, **"O LORD of hosts."** Now church, that title, the LORD of hosts, the LORD of the armies of heaven, that is the first time in the entire Bible that anybody calls God by that name. The first time. And you know who says it? Not a priest. Not a king. Not a prophet on a mountain. A heartbroken, infertile woman, crying on the floor of a corrupt sanctuary, is the first person in Scripture to call God the Commander of the armies of heaven. She reached past the broken priesthood and grabbed hold of the God who actually runs the universe.

  

And she prays, **"if you will look on the affliction of your servant and remember me and not forget your servant,"** and then she makes her promise: **"I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life." **God, if you'll give me a son, I'll give him right back to you.

  

Now here's where it gets, I think, almost funny if it wasn't so sad. Verse 12. Hannah's praying like this, and she's praying so deep that her lips are moving but no sound is coming out. She's praying from a place too deep for words. And old Eli, the priest, he's sitting there by the doorpost watching her. And what does the man of God conclude about this woman pouring out her soul?

  

*He thinks she's drunk.

*

Verse 14. He walks over and says, **"How long will you go on being drunk? Put your wine away from you."** The spiritual leader of Israel looks at the most sincere act of worship happening in that whole building and completely misreads it. He's so used to seeing nothing real that when something real shows up, he can't recognize it. That's how dull the house had gotten. The priest couldn't tell the difference between a drunk and a saint.

  

And I love Hannah's answer, because she doesn't fire back, but she doesn't fold either. Verse 15. **"No, my lord. I am a woman troubled in spirit. I have drunk neither wine nor strong drink, but I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD."** Pouring out my soul. That's what real prayer is. It's not performance. It's pouring.

  

And something shifts in the old man. Maybe a flicker of the priest he used to be wakes up. And Eli says, verse 17, "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition that you have made to him."

  

Now let's not miss this.

  

Hannah's breakthrough did not happen in a perfect church. It happened in a compromised one. The leadership was corrupt. The priest misjudged her to her face. The whole institution was on its way to collapse. And God still heard her.

  

Did you hear me? Even when the house was imperfect, God still heard the desperate prayer.

  

So many are waiting on a perfect church to bring their real prayers to. Wait until the people get right, until the leaders get right, until you feel like the room deserves your honesty. And I want to set you free this morning. God is not limited by the dullness of the house. Hannah didn't pray to Eli. Hannah prayed past Eli, to the LORD of hosts. And he heard a prayer that the priest couldn't even recognize.

  

Look at verse 18.** "Then the woman went her way and ate, and her face was no longer sad."** Now watch this. She doesn't have the baby yet. Nothing has changed in her body. The circumstances are identical. But she got up off that floor and she ate, and the sadness was gone off her face. Why? Because she had handed it over. She came in carrying it and she left having laid it down. That's what happens when you actually pour your soul out to the God who hears.

  

### A hidden prayer that begins a public renewal

  

So now watch how big this little prayer turns out to be.

  

Verse 19. They go home,** "and the LORD remembered her."** That word "remembered" doesn't mean God had forgotten and then went, *"Oh, Hannah, right."* It means God moved. God acted on her behalf. And Hannah conceives, and she has a son, and she names him Samuel, because, she says, **"I have asked for him from the LORD."** The name sounds like the Hebrew word for "asked." Every time anybody says this boy's name for the rest of his life, they are saying, **"this is the answered prayer. This is the one God gave."**

  

And Hannah keeps her promise. This is one of the most stunning acts of faith in the whole Bible and it's so easty to read right past it. She waits till he's weaned, just a few years old, a little boy, and she takes him back up to Shiloh. To that same broken-down sanctuary. And she hands her only son, the son she cried years for, over to old Eli to be raised in the house of God. Verse 28. **"Therefore I have lent him to the LORD. As long as he lives, he is lent to the LORD."**

  

She asked God for a son so she could give the son back to God.

  

Now stop and think about who's in that room and who is not. There's no king in Israel. There's no army marching. There's no famous prophet on the scene. There's no platform, no crowd, no spotlight. There is one woman nobody respected, in a building nobody trusted, handing over one little boy nobody's heard of yet. And that hidden moment, that quiet handoff that the rest of the nation completely slept through, **that is the moment God starts the next chapter of Israel's whole story.**

  

Because that little boy Samuel is going to grow up to be the prophet who anoints Israel's first king. And then he's going to anoint Israel's greatest king, David. And out of the line of David, centuries later, is going to come a King with no end to his kingdom. The whole monarchy, the whole line that leads to the throne of the Messiah himself, the rescue of the entire nation, all of it traces back to a prayer that nobody in the building even recognized as a prayer.

  

God started a public revival with a private prayer.

  

And then Hannah does something that tells you she understood exactly what just happened. In chapter 2 she opens her mouth and she sings. And this isn't a little lullaby. This is one of the great songs in all of Scripture. Listen to it. **"My heart exults in the LORD. My horn is exalted in the LORD."** The horn, that's the symbol of strength, the empty woman is singing about strength now. **"There is none holy like the LORD. There is no rock like our God."** And then she sings the line that is the key to the whole book. Chapter 2 verse 8. **"He raises up the poor from the dust. He lifts the needy from the ash heap to make them sit with princes."**

## Movement 4: The Typological Reveal

  

But Hannah's song does not stay in 1 Samuel. Look where it shows up again.

  

About a thousand years later, there's another woman. She's young. She's poor. She's from a forgotten little town. SAnd an angel comes to her and tells her she's going to carry a son, and that this son is going to be given for the whole world. And when this young woman opens her mouth to praise God, listen to what comes out.

  

She says, **"My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior, for he has looked on the humble estate of his servant." **She says, **"He has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate. He has filled the hungry with good things."**

  

That's Hannah's song. Mary is singing Hannah's song. The Magnificat in Luke chapter 1 is built right on top of the prayer of the barren woman from Shiloh. The same God who raises the poor from the dust. The same God who lifts the needy from the ash heap. The same God who hears the overlooked woman and starts the renewal of the world through the son she carries.

  

And look at the pattern, because it's the same pattern, only bigger.

  

Hannah was barren. Mary was a virgin. Both of them, in the eyes of the world, were in a place where life could not come. And God brought life out of the impossible place. That's what he does. He specializes in the empty womb and the empty tomb. He brings life where life has no business showing up.

  

And here's the deepest one. Hannah asked God for a son, and then she gave the son back to God for the sake of the people. Now turn it around. God the Father had a Son. And he didn't wait for anybody to ask. He gave his Son. He sent him down into a world that was barren, into a nation whose word of the LORD was rare, into a system whose priesthood had gone corrupt, and he handed his only Son over for the people.
