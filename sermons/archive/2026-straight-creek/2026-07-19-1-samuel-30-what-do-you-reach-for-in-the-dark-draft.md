## Hook: The Held Punch

For nineteen years, he was just a number: 24601.

Jean Valjean stole a loaf of bread to feed his sister's starving family, and the law branded him a criminal for life. And for the rest of his life, he was hunted by one man: Inspector Javert.

Javert isn't a villain. That's what makes him terrifying. He is a man of absolute law, absolute order, total conviction. To Javert there are two kinds of people: law-abiding citizens and criminals. No gray areas. No grace. Break the law once, and you are a criminal forever.

But then the barricades go up in Paris. The tables turn. And Javert is captured by the revolutionaries.

He is unarmed. Helpless. Sentenced to die.
And the man holding the gun is Jean Valjean.

After decades of being hunted, Valjean finally has the man who ruined his life exactly where he wants him. Everybody watching knows what comes next. Take him out. He's earned it.

But Valjean lowers the gun. And he lets Javert walk away.

**(Pause. Shift tone. Let the transition do the work.)**

Three thousand years earlier, another king obsessed with his own authority was doing some hunting of his own.

King Saul has three thousand chosen men with him. He is not out to talk. He is out to end this. He is hunting David.

Needing a moment of relief, Saul steps inside a cave. He walks out of the glaring desert sun and into pitch blackness. He has no armor-bearer. No guard at the entrance. He is just a king in the dark, completely vulnerable.

What Saul doesn't know is that deep in the recesses of that exact same cave, David and his men are watching.

David's men whisper to him, *"This is it. This is the day God promised you. He has delivered your enemy into your hands."*

David begins to crawl forward in the dark. The man who has made his life a living hell is inches away. One strike, and the running stops. One strike, and David takes the throne.

He gets close enough to kill him. But he doesn't touch Saul.
Quietly, he takes his blade and cuts off the corner of Saul's robe.

Saul doesn't feel a thing. He finishes his business, gets up, and walks back out into the daylight, completely unaware that he was just a breath away from the grave.

And David is left standing in the shadows, holding a scrap of cloth in his hand.

**(Slow down. Deliver the question with weight.)**

David had his tormentor dead to rights, and he chose mercy.

What makes a man lower the gun? What makes a man sheathe the blade when everybody around him is saying *God said so*?

Let's look at the text.

## Movement 1: The Cave — Restraint

Turn with me to 1 Samuel 24.

Saul has three thousand chosen men with him. He is not out here to talk. He is out here to end this.

And the search brings him, of all places, to a cave cut into the rock. Saul steps inside to relieve himself. Alone. No armor bearer. No guard at the entrance. Just a king, in the dark, with his back turned.

And in the back of that same cave, David and his men are already sitting there. Have been the whole time. They can hear him breathing before their eyes even adjust.

Now notice what the men say next.

"Here is the day of which the Lord said to you, 'I will give your enemy into your hand.'"

Go back and read your Bible looking for where God actually said that to David. You will not find it.

That is not a promise being repeated. That is men in the dark, doing what men in the dark do — dressing up their own opportunity and packaging it in Scripture.

This is your moment, they tell him. God said so.

David does not answer them out loud. He crawls forward instead.

Now watch what happens.

David has Saul dead to rights. Every man behind David holding his breath, waiting to watch a king die. David reaches out. After all the chasing, the killing, could Saul finally be within reach?

But he doesn't even touch Saul.

He touches the robe.

Cuts a corner of it. Quiet as he can. Close enough to kill him, and instead he takes a scrap of cloth the size of his hand.

Saul never even feels it. He gets up, still not knowing how close he came, and walks back out into the daylight.

And that is when it hits David.

Verse five says his heart struck him.

Not relief. Not pride in his own restraint. His heart struck him, the way it strikes a man who almost did something and cannot stop shaking at how close he came.

He turns on his own men. Tells them the Lord forbid that he should stretch out his hand against the Lord's anointed.


David has the chance. Right here. Right now. To take the kingdom. 

And he will not take it.

He walks out after Saul instead. Bows to the ground. Holds up that piece of robe where Saul can see it.

"See, my father, see the corner of your robe in my hand. I cut it, and I did not kill you. Know and see that there is no wrong or treason in my hands." [[David and my father]]
]]
He had everything he needed to end this his way.

The men. The dark. The blade already in his hand.

And he walked out holding a scrap of cloth instead of a throne.

Saul weeps. Says David is more righteous than he is. Even says out loud that David will be king one day.

Then Saul goes home.

Back to his own house. Back to his own kind of darkness. A few tears in a cave do not undo years of chasing a man he was never supposed to chase. Before this story is finished, he will be hunting David again.

## The Eras Tour to the Madhouse

 This has to be disorienting for David. Just look at where he was before the cave. He wasn't a man born in the wilderness. He was a man who... He was not a man who grew up hiding. He was anointed by Samuel. He killed a giant. He was made secretary of war in Saul's administration. The guy was successful in everything that he did. We know the song, Saul has slain his tens of... Saul has slain his thousands, David his tens of thousands. 
 
 He didn't just have a hit song. David had the heiress tour of Israel. Every woman in every village was singing his name. He was the most famous man in the world
 
 This is what we need to understand about David. He has no chill. He can't do anything halfway. He's either all the way up or all the way down. 
 
 Just look, after Goliath, Saul offers David his oldest daughter, Merab, as his wife. What an honor. The king pulling you into the family. And David's response, "Who am I? What is my family that I should be son-in-law to the king?" Real humble. Real, "Aw, shucks, not me." Except watch what happens the next chapter. 
 
 Saul comes back around with the younger daughter, Michael. And this time, the price of admission is 100 Philistine foreskins. Now, that's not a romantic gesture. That's Saul trying to get David killed. So what does David do? With an assignment designed to be his own funeral, he brings him back 200. Not 100. Not 101 just to be safe. Double the requirement. 
 
 The same man who just told Saul, "Who am I? I'm nobody. My nobody's family," turns right around and shows up with twice the body count anybody asked for. That's not humility anymore. That's a man trying to prove something You don't overshoot a death mission by 100% unless some part of you needs everybody in that room to know exactly who you are. 
 
 That's David, underselling himself one minute, overproving himself the next. Never just in the middle where the truth actually lives. And you see it again in the cave. He has Saul right where he wants him, knife in his hand, close enough to end it, and all he does is take a piece of his robe Talk about ice water in your veins. But then 30 seconds later, the same man is standing at the mouth of the same cave weeping, calling out to Saul, calling him father, absolutely wrecked over cutting off a piece of fabric. 
 
 The man has went from lethal to sobbing in the same scene. No middle gear. Full throttle one direction, then full throttle the, then, then full throttle the other, and it keeps going. He stands in front of a valley of the whole Philistine army and tells Goliath out loud in front of everybody, "You come to me with a sword and spear, but I come to you in the name of the Lord." 
 
 Bold, honest. Nothing to prove because God has already proved it. But later, running for his life, scared and alone, he doesn't tell Ahimelech, the priest, the truth. He makes up a whole story, tells him he's on the king's business, and that lie gets 85 priests killed later on. 
 
 And after that, he stands in front of a Philistine king with nowhere left to run. He don't ask for mercy. He fakes insanity, scratches at the doors, lets spit run down his beard. A grown man who's been anointed king acting like a lunatic because it's easier than trusting God with the truth in that moment. 
 
 So here's my questioning for David, and honestly, it's a questioning for myself: Where's the boy with the sling and the stone? Where's that kid that didn't need 200 foreskins or a crazy act or a lie to Ahimelech because all he needed was a sling, five smooth stones in the name of the Lord of Hosts? 
 
 Somewhere between the sheep pasture and the palace, David's traded simply trusting God for constantly proving himself So let's ask ourselves the same thing this morning. 
 Where is the version of me that just trusted God? Where is the version of me that didn't have to overcompensate to hide what I was lacking?

## Movement 2: Nabal — The Snap

## The Second Story: Gatorade at the Wrong Second

I've got three kids. And I love them. I love being their dad. But some days — some days it's just noise. Constant noise. One needs a snack. One needs help with homework. One is crying because the other one looked at them wrong. And I'm doing my best. I'm being patient. I'm being a good dad. And then it settles. Two minutes of quiet. Blessed, holy quiet.

And then one of them — and it's always the sweetest one, always the one who wasn't even making noise — walks up and says, "Dad, can I have some Gatorade?"

And I snap. "*Can I have just one minute?*"

And he looks at me like I've betrayed him. I feel terrible. Because it wasn't his fault. He just asked. That's all. He just happened to be the one who asked at the wrong second.

**Terrible timing**.

David has just come out of the cave. He's done the hardest thing he's ever done. He spared the man who's been trying to kill him. He held up the cloth and said "**I'm a dead dog**." And Saul went home. Then Samuel dies. And David goes down to the wilderness of Paran. And he's just about at the end of his rope.

And then Nabal disrespects him after he and his men have been protecting Nabal's shepherds for months.

**Terrible timing**.

---
Nabal isn't the sharpest tool in the shed. His name means fool. He is harsh and badly behaved. He has three thousand sheep and a thousand goats, and he is shearing sheep in Carmel. It is a feast day. It is a time of celebration. It is a time when a man with that much wealth is expected to share.

David's men have protected Nabal's shepherds. They have been a wall around them, night and day. They have kept them safe. And now David sends ten young men to ask for provisions. Not payment. Not a fee. Just food for the men who have been guarding Nabal's flocks.

And Nabal says, "Who is David? Who is the son of Jesse? There are many servants these days who are breaking away from their masters."

**Who is David?
**
The man who killed Goliath. That's who David is. The man who has been singing in every village in Israel. The man who is the most famous person in the kingdom.

*Who is David?
*
Nabal knows who David is. Everybody knows who David is. Saul made sure of that. Nabal is not asking for information. He is insulting David. He is treating the most famous man in Israel like a runaway slave.

And David snaps.

**"Every man strap on his sword."
**
Not "Who am I? I'm a dead dog." Not "The Lord forbid." Just "strap on your sword, let's kill the whole lot of them."

The man who cut cloth and wept over it is ready to slaughter an entire household over an insult. And you want to ask: does the man's whole household deserve to die, David? Nabal is a fool. His wife is not. His servants are not. The shepherds who David protected are not. But David is going to kill them all because David is done.

He has been the bigger person for too long. He has swallowed too much pride. He has made himself small too many times. And Nabal is the straw that breaks the saint's back.

This is not righteous anger. This is not holy indignation. This is a man who has been pushed past his limit and is going to take it out on the nearest available target.

And then Abigail rides out.

She brings food. She brings wisdom. She falls on her face before David and says, "On me, my lord, let this iniquity be." She takes the blame. She redirects his rage. She says, "When the Lord has done for my lord according to all the good that he has spoken concerning you, and has appointed you ruler over Israel, this will be no grief to you, nor offense of heart to my lord, that you have shed blood without cause, or that my lord has avenged himself."

Don't do it, David. Don't become the thing you've been trying not to become. Don't let Nabal make you into Saul.

And David listens.

He says, "Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, who sent you this day to meet me! 33 Blessed be your discretion, and blessed be you, who have kept me this day from bloodguilt and from working salvation with my own hand."

Abigail didn't just save Nabal from David
Abigail saves David from David.

Ten days later, the Lord strikes Nabal and he dies. David hears the news, and what does he do?

David marries Abigail.

## Movement 3: Besor — Grace Shared

Now let's go to 1 Samuel 30.

David and his men have been gone three days. They left Ziklag expecting to come home to their wives, their children, their beds. They were marching with the Philistine army, sent back in disgrace, and now they're home. They're tired. They're ready for rest.

They see smoke first.

Then they see the city. Or what's left of it. Ziklag is gone. Burned. The Amalekites came while they were away. Took the women. Took the children. Took everything.

The text says David and his men "raised their voices and wept until they had no more strength to weep." These are warriors. Men who have fought battles, faced armies, lived in caves. They don't cry easy. But they cry now. They cry until their bodies give out. Until there's nothing left.

And then the text says something worse. "The people spoke of stoning him." The men who just wept until they couldn't weep anymore are now talking about killing David. They're bitter in soul. They need someone to blame. David is standing close enough to blame.

David has lost his home. His wives are gone. His men want to kill him. And the text says: "But David strengthened himself in the Lord his God."

He calls Abiathar. He asks for the ephod. He inquires of the Lord. "Shall I pursue?" And God says "Pursue, for you shall surely overtake and shall surely rescue."

So they go. Six hundred men, weeping, exhausted, grieving, angry. They march after the Amalekites. And when they reach the Brook Besor, two hundred of them can't go anymore.

Not "won't." Can't. Their bodies are done. They wept until they had no strength. They marched three days. They found ashes. They wanted to stone their leader. And now they're supposed to fight? They can't. They collapse at the brook. Four hundred cross. Two hundred stay.

David finds the Amalekites. He attacks them. And verse 18 says: "David recovered all that the Amalekites had taken."

All of it.

Not some of it. Not most of it. All of it.

The women are rescued. The children are rescued. The possessions are recovered. Verse 19 says: "Nothing was missing, whether small or great, sons or daughters, spoil or anything that had been taken. David brought back all."

The city had burned. But God had not abandoned them.

And when David returns, he comes back to the two hundred men who were too exhausted to cross the brook. They did not fight. They did not chase the Amalekites. They did not stand beside David in the battle. They stayed behind with the supplies.

And some of the men who fought have a problem with that.

**Verse 22** calls them "**wicked and worthless fellows**." Those are the Bible's words, not mine. They say: "**Because they did not go with us, we will not give them any of the spoil that we have recovered, except that each man may lead away his wife and children, and depart**."

*Do you hear what they are saying?
*
"*We fought for this. We earned this. They stayed behind. They do not deserve what we deserve*."

But David sees the victory differently.

Listen to what he says in **verse 23**: "**You shall not do so, my brothers, with what the Lord has given us**."

There it is.

The other men call it "the spoil that we recovered." David calls it "what the Lord has given us."

They see wages. David sees grace.

They see what their strength earned. David sees what God gave.

**The way you name the victory determines how you share it.**

If I believe I earned everything I have, I will hold it with a closed fist. I fought for this. I worked for this. I crossed the brook. I faced the enemy. I deserve more than the people who stayed behind.

But if I recognize that everything I have came from the hand of God, then I can respond like David.

David says: "He has preserved us and given into our hand the band that came against us."

Who preserved them? The Lord did. Who gave the enemy into their hands? The Lord did. Who brought the families home? The Lord did.

Yes, David fought. Yes, the four hundred men crossed the brook. Yes, they pursued the enemy. But their strength did not create the victory. God gave it.

And because the victory was a gift, David refused to let the exhausted men be pushed outside the community.

He says: "As his share is who goes down into the battle, so shall his share be who stays by the baggage. They shall share alike."

The strong receive a share. The weary receive a share. The men who fought receive a share. The men who could go no farther receive a share.

They all have a place because the victory was never theirs to own. It was God's gift to share.

**When victory is grace, the weary do not have to earn their place at the table.**

## The Stone-Throwers at the Brook

Some of those two hundred men who stayed behind were the same men who wanted to stone David.

They were bitter in soul. They wanted to kill him. They needed someone to blame, and David was standing close enough to blame. And now they are too exhausted to cross the brook, and David says they get the same share as the men who fought for him.

Not "the men who supported me." Not "the men who believed in me." The men who wanted to kill me. Same share. Same grace. Same table.

That is not just generosity. That is forgiveness. And it is the same David who, one chapter earlier, needed Abigail to stop him from killing Nabal's household.

The man who was stopped from becoming Saul is now the man who refuses to become Saul. The men who wanted to stone him get the same share. Because David learned what Abigail taught him: you do not have to swing the sword to prove you are somebody.

David did not bring a sword to Besor. He brought grace. And he lived out Acts 2 before Acts 2 existed — all who believed were together and had all things in common. The men who fought and the men who could not cross got the same share. Not because they earned it. Because the victory was God's gift to share, not David's to distribute according to merit.

## The Gospel Turn: The Champion Who Fought for Us

David gives us a real pattern, but he does not give us a perfect one.

David was not always faithful. Some days David strengthened himself in the Lord. Other days David needed the Lord to rescue him from David. And so do we.

But the story keeps moving until it brings us to the faithful Son of David.

Jesus entered our ashes.

He faced the enemy we could not defeat.

He refused the path of self-saving power.

When they mocked him and said, "Save yourself," he remained on the cross.

He entrusted himself to the Father.

He fought the battle alone.

He bore our sin.

He entered death.

And on the third day, he walked out of the grave with a victory we could never win.

Then he shared that victory with us.

We did not cross the brook. We did not fight the battle. We did not defeat sin. We did not conquer death.

Jesus did.

But the victory he won becomes the grace we receive.

Forgiveness for the guilty. Righteousness for the undeserving. Life for the dead. Rest for the weary.

We do not receive the benefits because we fought beside him. We receive them because he fought for us.

When victory is grace, the weary do not have to earn their place at the table.

## The Bridge: Your Brook Besor

I want you to think about the two hundred men who stayed at the brook.

They did not fight. They did not cross. They did not earn anything. Some of them were the same men who, hours earlier, were ready to stone David. And David says they get the same share as the men who fought.

That is not a story about ancient Israel. That is a story about you.

Some of you have been trying to cross the brook for years. You have been trying to be strong enough, good enough, faithful enough. You have been trying to earn your place at the table. And you are tired. You are so tired. You wept until you had no more strength to weep. You marched until your body gave out. You found ashes where your home used to be. And you are sitting at the brook, watching the fighters cross over, wondering if there is anything left for you.

Hear me. The victory is not yours because you fought. The victory is yours because he fought. The table is not yours because you earned it. The table is yours because he set it.

David did not bring a sword to Besor. He brought grace. And the men who wanted to stone him got the same share as the men who fought for him.

Jesus does not bring a sword to your brook. He brings grace. And the people who wanted to stone him — the people who failed him, who denied him, who ran when he needed them — get the same share as the people who fought beside him.

You do not have to cross the brook. You do not have to earn your place. You just have to come to the table.

When victory is grace, the weary do not have to earn their place at the table.

## Altar Call

I want to ask you to stand with me this morning.

Some of you are standing at the Brook Besor today. You are not just tired — you are spent. The fighters have moved on, and you feel like you have lost your right to the victory. If that is you, hear this: this altar is not a place for the strong. It is the place where the weary receive the spoils they did not earn.

David did not find his strength while the city was still standing. He found it while it was burning. If your "city" is in ashes right now — if your family, your health, your peace, or your future feels gone — do not wait until things get better to come to God. Strengthen yourself in the Lord right here in the middle of the mess. Walk out of those ashes and find your place at this altar.

Jesus won a battle you did not even have the strength to show up for. He already defeated the enemy you could not beat. He already recovered everything the enemy tried to steal. And now He is standing at the table, inviting the two hundred who could not cross the brook. You do not have to prove your worthiness to come. You just have to admit your weariness.

So if you are here today and you feel like the one the world wants to leave behind — if you are exhausted, grieving, or standing in the ashes of something you never asked for — this altar is your Brook Besor. You do not have to cross it on your own strength. The Champion has already returned with the spoil.

If you need the rest that only grace can give, I invite you to come. Come and receive what the Lord has given us.

## Closing Line

Being the bigger person is exhausting when everyone else is acting small.

But the victory is not yours because you were big enough to earn it.

The victory is yours because He was big enough to win it.

And He still shares His victory with weary people.

Come to the table.
