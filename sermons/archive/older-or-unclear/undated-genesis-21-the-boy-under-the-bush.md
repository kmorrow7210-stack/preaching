The Boy Under the Bush
I. Thematic & Evocative Introduction
Establish the "Normal" (Equilibrium):
Three years.
That's how long Sarah has been a mother. Three years of nursing, three years of watching this
impossible child: this miracle baby: grow from fragile infant to toddling boy. Three years of
laughter that replaced the bitter cynicism of her barren decades.
Today is Isaac's weaning party, and Abraham has spared no expense. In the ancient Near East,
this isn't just a milestone: it's a survival celebration. Most children didn't make it to age three.
Disease, famine, accidents. Childhood was a minefield. But Isaac? Isaac made it. Against all
biological odds, against every law of nature that said a 90-year-old womb can't produce life,
Isaac is alive.
The tents are full. The food is abundant. There's music, dancing, the sound of celebration
echoing across the camp. Servants are passing around wine. Friends and family are toasting
Abraham and Sarah. Everyone knows this story: the decades of waiting, the promise that
seemed like a cruel joke, the miraculous birth that finally came.
And now, here they are. God kept His word.
Sarah is standing there, holding Isaac, and for the first time in her adult life, she's not "the
barren one." She's not the woman people whisper about with pity. She's a mother. The laughter
on her lips isn't mockery anymore. It's joy.
Everything that was broken has been made whole. Everything that was impossible has become
real. This is the happily-ever-after moment. The end of the story.
Roll credits, right?

Disrupt the Normal (Upsetting the Equilibrium):
You remember the 2022 Oscars?
The biggest night in Hollywood. Chris Rock is on stage presenting an award, doing what Chris
Rock does: making jokes. He makes a comment about Jada Pinkett Smith's hair. It's a joke.
Maybe in poor taste, maybe not. But it's a joke at an awards show.
And then Will Smith: one of the most beloved actors in the world, sitting in the front row on what
should be the best night of his career: walks onto that stage.

And then Will Smith slaps Chris Rock across the face in front of millions of people watching live.
Record scratch.
The music's still playing. The cameras are still rolling. But the entire Dolby Theatre just went
silent. You could feel the collective "Wait... did that just happen?" ripple through the room and
across every living room in America.
What was supposed to be a celebration: Hollywood's biggest party: just became a crime scene.
And within minutes, the takes started flying: "Was Chris Rock wrong?" "Was Will Smith
justified?" "Who's the victim here?" "Who needs to leave?"
Back in Abraham's tent, at Isaac's weaning party, Sarah sees something that makes her react
like Will Smith just saw Chris Rock mock his wife.
But here's the thing: Ishmael didn't slap anyone.
Ishmael: fourteen years old now, Abraham's firstborn son, the boy Sarah herself told Abraham
to conceive when God's promise was taking too long: is metzahek.
That's the Hebrew word the text uses. It can mean "laughing." It can mean "playing." It can
mean "celebrating." But it shares the same root as Isaac's name: yitzchak, "he laughs." And in
this moment, with Sarah's eyes locked on him, it can also mean something darker: mocking.
Is Ishmael genuinely playing with his little brother? Is he laughing along with the celebration? Or
is there an edge to it: some teenage resentment, some jealousy about being displaced by this
miracle baby everyone's fawning over?
We don't know. The text doesn't say.
But Sarah decides what it means.
And in that split second, the party atmosphere shatters.
You know that moment: when a room goes cold? When one comment, one look, one laugh
lands wrong and suddenly everyone can feel the temperature drop? The music's still playing,
people are still eating, but there's a shift. An invisible line has been crossed.
Sarah walks straight to Abraham. No small talk. No easing into it. Just cold, calculated fury:
"Cast out this slave woman with her son, for the son of this slave woman shall not be heir with
my son Isaac." (Genesis 21:10)
Listen to those words. She doesn't say "Hagar." She doesn't say "Ishmael." She says "this slave
woman" and "her son." She's erasing their names. Reducing them to categories. Dehumanizing
them with pronouns.

And notice what she adds: "my son Isaac."
Not "our son." Not "Abraham's son." My son. Possession. Territory. A line in the sand.
The subtext is deafening: "It's him or us. Choose."
And Abraham? The text says this was "very displeasing to Abraham on account of his son."
(Genesis 21:11)
"Very displeasing": that's the sanitized translation. The Hebrew word is ra. It means evil.
Wicked. Grievous. This didn't just bother Abraham. It felt fundamentally wrong to him. Morally
repulsive. His stomach dropped. His heart broke.
Because Ishmael isn't just "the other kid." He's Abraham's son: the boy he's raised for fourteen
years. The child he loves. The son he's taught to walk, to hunt, to pray.
And now his wife is demanding that he send him away.
Not to another tent. Not to a relative's house. Away. Into the wilderness.
You'd have thought Ishmael had walked up and slapped Isaac across the face. But he didn't. He
just laughed.
And Sarah's verdict? "Cast them out."
Not discipline. Not correction. Elimination.
He gets bread, a skin of water, and the wilderness.

Curiosity/Empathy Sparks:
Have you ever been at a celebration where one moment suddenly poisoned everything?
Where someone said or did something: maybe innocently, maybe not: and the whole
atmosphere shifted from joy to suspicion? From gratitude to accusation?
Have you ever watched someone you love be reduced to a category?
Not a name. Not a person. Just "that person." "The problem." "The one who needs to go."
Have you ever been Ishmael?
Standing there, maybe you were just laughing, just playing, just existing: and someone decided
your presence was a threat?
Or have you been Abraham?

Trapped between two people you love, knowing that whatever you choose, someone you care
about is going to get hurt?
Or maybe you've been Sarah?
So protective of what you've finally received, so afraid of losing it, that you're willing to destroy
someone else to keep it safe?
The party's not over yet. But something has been set in motion that can't be undone.
And then: God speaks.
And what He says... makes everything so much worse.

II. IMMERSION & ANALYSIS
A. MULTI-ANGLE SCENE-SETTING (Textual Exploration)
$$MOVE OFF STAGE - Begin pacing, building the scene$$
So Sarah says, "Cast them out." And Abraham? The text says it was "very displeasing to
Abraham on account of his son."
That word in Hebrew is ra. Evil. Wicked. Grievous. This didn't just bother Abraham: it felt
fundamentally wrong to him. Morally repulsive.
But then God speaks. And here's where it gets uncomfortable.
God says, "Whatever Sarah tells you, do it."
Wait. What?
God sides with Sarah? The God of justice, mercy, and compassion tells Abraham to send a
fourteen-year-old boy and his mother into the desert with a loaf of bread and a water skin?
And Abraham does it.
$$PAINT THE SCENE - Sensory immersion$$
Genesis 21:14 says Abraham got up early in the morning, gave Hagar some bread and a skin of
water, and sent her away.
Picture this.
It's dawn. The camp is still quiet. Abraham hands Hagar a single leather water skin: maybe
holds a gallon, maybe two. A loaf of bread. That's it.
No tent. No servants. No map. No plan.
Just, "Go."

And Hagar takes Ishmael: this boy she bore for Abraham, this teenager who's spent his whole
life in this household: and she walks into the wilderness of Beersheba.
$$SLOW DOWN - Let them feel the wilderness$$
Now, if you've never been to the Negev desert, let me tell you: it is hostile.
The wilderness of Beersheba isn't like a hiking trail with rest stops. It's barren. It's brutal.
Temperatures can hit 120 degrees in the day and drop to freezing at night. There's no shade.
No streams. No oases for miles.
Just rock. Sand. Scrub brush. Sun.
And somewhere in that wasteland, Hagar is walking. Ishmael is beside her. The water skin is
getting lighter.
$$SHIFT PERSPECTIVE - Multiple viewpoints$$
Let's step into their shoes for a moment.
From Hagar's perspective:
She's an Egyptian slave. She didn't ask for any of this. Sarah handed her to Abraham like
property. She got pregnant. She gave birth. She raised this boy for fourteen years in Abraham's
household.
And now? She's been discarded. Cast out. Sent into the desert to die.
She's walking, and with every step, she's doing the math. "How many days can we stretch this
water? How far can we get before it runs out?"
From Ishmael's perspective:
He's fourteen. Old enough to understand what's happening. Old enough to know that his father:
the man who taught him to hunt, to pray, to be a man: just sent him away.
He didn't slap Isaac. He didn't curse God. He laughed.
And for that? Exile.
His lips are cracking. His throat is dry. And he's watching his mother try to hold it together, and
he knows: he knows: they're not going to make it.
From Abraham's perspective:
Back at the camp, Abraham is standing at the edge of the tents, watching them disappear into
the horizon.
This is his son. His firstborn. The boy he's loved for fourteen years.

And he just sent him to die.
Because God said, "Do what Sarah says."
You think Abraham slept that night?
From Sarah's perspective:
She got what she wanted. The threat is gone. Isaac's inheritance is secure.
But here's the thing about getting what you want through cruelty: it doesn't feel as good as you
thought it would.
I wonder if Sarah looked at Isaac that night and thought, "Was it worth it?"
$$RETURN TO THE TEXT - The crisis moment$$
Genesis 21:15-16:
"When the water in the skin was gone, she put the boy under one of the bushes. Then she went
off and sat down about a bowshot away, for she thought, 'I cannot watch the boy die.' And as
she sat there, she began to sob."
Let that image sit with you.
Ishmael: dying of thirst: cast under a shrub. The only shade in the entire desert.
Hagar: about a hundred yards away, just far enough that she can't see his face, but close
enough to hear him cry.
And she's sitting there, weeping, because she cannot bear to watch her son die.
$$THEOLOGICAL INSIGHT - Name actualization$$
Now here's something you need to catch.
Ishmael's name: Yishma'el: means "God hears."
His mother named him that fourteen years ago when God heard her cry in the wilderness the
first time she ran away (Genesis 16).
And now, in his darkest moment, under that bush, dying of thirst, forsaken by his father, expelled
from his home...
...his name is about to be actualized.
Because verse 17 says:
"God heard the boy crying."

Not Hagar's weeping. Not Abraham's guilt. Not Sarah's justifications.
God heard the voice of the boy.

B. NAME THE DEEPER ISSUE (The Real Discrepancy)
$$PAUSE - Shift to teaching mode$$
So what's really going on here?
On the surface, this is a story about family drama. Sibling rivalry. Inheritance disputes. Blended
family chaos.
But go deeper. What's the root of this tension?
Here it is:
Does God's choice of one require the rejection of another?
That's the question haunting this text.
God chose Isaac. That's clear. He's the son of promise. The covenant will flow through him.
But does that mean Ishmael has to die in the desert?
Does divine election require divine rejection?
Does God's "yes" to Isaac demand a "no" to Ishmael?
$$THEOLOGICAL TENSION - Raise the stakes$$
Because here's what makes this so uncomfortable:
God commanded this.
Abraham didn't want to do it. The text says it was ra: evil: to him.
But God said, "Whatever Sarah tells you, do it."
So now we're stuck with a God who sanctions the expulsion of an innocent teenager into a
death trap.
And that raises the hardest question of all:
Where is God when the innocent suffer as collateral damage in the fulfillment of His promises?
This is theodicy. This is the question of God's justice.
Ishmael didn't do anything wrong. He laughed. That's it.

And now he's under a bush, dying of thirst, crying out, while his mother sits a hundred yards
away, unable to watch.
$$PIVOT - The hidden God$$
But here's where the text does something brilliant.
Verse 17: "God heard the voice of the boy."
God heard before He revealed Himself.
Verse 19: "Then God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water."
Wait. When did the well show up?
It was always there.
Hagar was sitting in the desert, convinced they were going to die, and the whole time, there was
water right there. She just couldn't see it.
This is what theologians call Deus absconditus: the hidden God.
God is present in the suffering, even when He feels absent.
God is working in the wilderness, even when He seems silent.
God hears the cry before He opens the eyes.

C. AUDIENCE MIRROR (Connecting Then to Now)
$$MOVE TOWARD CONGREGATION - Direct address$$
Church, let me ask you something.
Have you ever felt like Ishmael?
Like you did everything right, or at least you didn't do anything that wrong, and yet you got cast
out?
Maybe it was a job. You worked hard, you were loyal, and then one day they just... let you go.
Maybe it was a relationship. You thought you were building something, and then they walked
away, and you're sitting there going, "What did I do?"
Maybe it's spiritual. You feel like everyone else is "chosen": they're getting the blessings, the
breakthroughs, the miracles: and you're out here in the wilderness, wondering if God even
knows your name.
Or maybe you've been Hagar.

You've been caught in the crossfire of other people's choices.
You didn't create this mess, but you're the one suffering the consequences.
You're the single mom trying to raise a kid on your own because someone else walked away.
You're the immigrant who left everything behind, and now you're in a strange land with no safety
net.
You're the one who keeps doing the right thing, and it keeps not working out, and you're sitting
there thinking, "God, I can't watch this fall apart. I can't do this anymore."
Or maybe: and this is harder to admit: maybe you've been Sarah.
You've got something precious, something you fought for, something you prayed for, and now
you're so afraid of losing it that you're willing to eliminate anyone who threatens it.
Maybe it's your position, your reputation, your family, your church.
And you look at someone else: someone who's different, someone who doesn't fit, someone
who makes you nervous: and you think, "They need to go."
And you justify it. You spiritualize it. You say, "This is about protecting what God gave me."
But deep down, you know: it's fear.
Or maybe you're Abraham.
Torn between two loves. Two loyalties. Two impossible choices.
You love your spouse. You love your kid. You love your parents. You love your calling.
And they're all pulling you in different directions, and no matter what you choose, somebody you
love is going to get hurt.
And you're standing there, praying, "God, please don't make me choose."
$$LAND THE MIRROR - Timeless struggle$$
This ancient struggle with fear, favoritism, and family chaos?
It's not so different from what we experience today.
Different desert. Same thirst. Same cry. Same desperate need for God to show up and open our
eyes to the well we can't see.
Because here's the thing:
The wilderness hasn't changed. But neither has the God who hears.

$$TRANSITION TO NEXT SECTION$$
And that's what we're about to see.
Because Genesis 21 doesn't end with Ishmael under a bush and Hagar weeping.
It ends with God speaking. With eyes being opened. With water in the desert.
It ends with a promise: "I will make him into a great nation."
God's election of Isaac doesn't require the destruction of Ishmael.
God's "yes" to one doesn't demand a "no" to the other.
But before we get to the water...
We need to sit in the cry.
Because it's in the cry: not the solution, not the provision, not the happy ending: it's in the cry
that we find out who God really is.

III. THE TURN (ALAS - DISCLOSING THE CLUE TO
RESOLUTION)
$$Transition from Section II$$
We've sat in that desert. Felt the sun. Watched Hagar turn her face because she couldn't bear to
watch her son die. We've asked the question: Where is God when the innocent suffer?
But here's the thing: this story isn't happening in a vacuum. This isn't the first time Scripture
shows us what happens when humans decide to write their own story.

The Pattern: When We Go Off on Our Own
Go all the way back. Genesis 3. The Garden. The serpent whispers, "You will be like God." Eve
looks at the fruit and thinks, I can decide for myself what's good. Adam stands there silent,
complicit. And they take. They eat. They choose independence over trust.
And what follows? Shame. Hiding. Blame. Exile. Cherubim with flaming swords guarding the
way back. The first humans make the first choice to go their own way, and the whole world
fractures.
Genesis 6. Humanity multiplies, and so does the chaos. "Every inclination of the thoughts of the
human heart was only evil all the time." God looks at what we've built and says, "I regret making
them." The flood comes. The world drowns. Reset button. But even Noah: the righteous one:
gets off the ark and gets drunk. The pattern doesn't stop.
Genesis 11. Babel. The people gather in the plain of Shinar and say, "Let us build ourselves a
city, with a tower that reaches to the heavens, so that we may make a name for ourselves."

They want glory. They want permanence. They want to be remembered. So they stack brick on
brick, reaching toward the sky.
And God comes down: not because the tower was tall, but because their hearts were proud:
and He scatters them. Confuses their language. What they built to unify them becomes the very
thing that fractures them. They wanted to make a name for themselves, and instead they got
Babel: confusion.
Genesis 12. God calls Abram. Promises him land, descendants, blessing. But famine comes,
and Abram panics. Goes down to Egypt. Lies about his wife. Says, "She's my sister," because
he doesn't trust God to protect him. Takes matters into his own hands. And Pharaoh's house
gets plagued because of it.
You see it? Over and over: We think we know better than God. We build our own plans. We take
control. And chaos follows.

The Towers We Build, The Carts That Topple
Lot looks at the Jordan Valley: lush, green, prosperous: and chooses Sodom. Pitches his tent
toward the city. Moves into the city. Sits in the gate. And what happens? Angels drag him out by
the wrists while fire rains from heaven. His wife turns to salt. His daughters get him drunk and
sleep with him. He chose his own path, and his family disintegrated.
Abraham and Hagar. Sarah hands her maid to her husband. "Build me a family through her."
Ishmael is born. And for fourteen years, it works: until it doesn't. Until Sarah sees him mocking
Isaac at the weaning feast and says, "Get rid of them." One decision. One shortcut. Fourteen
years of family wreckage.
Esau and his stew. Comes in from the field, hungry, impulsive. Sees the bowl of red stuff and
says, "Give me some of that." Trades his birthright for a meal. And decades later, he's weeping,
begging his father for a blessing that's already gone. One moment of I want what I want right
now, and he loses everything.
Ishmael wasn't sent away because God is unjust.
Let me say that again.
Ishmael wasn't sent away because God is unjust.
Ishmael was sent away because when we take things into our own hands: when we build our
own towers, when we write our own scripts, when we choose our own path, when we construct
our own carts: things topple. And people get hurt.

Two Mothers, Two Choices
Let's talk about the women for a second, because this whole mess started with a conversation
between Sarah and Abraham.

Sarah: her name means princess. She's the matriarch, the promise-bearer, the one God chose
to mother nations. But when the promise was delayed? When her womb stayed closed year
after year after year? She couldn't wait. She couldn't trust. So she handed her maid to her
husband and said, "Build me a family through her."
Hagar: her name means flight or stranger. She's the outsider, the Egyptian, the one with no
voice in this decision. Used as a surrogate, despised when she conceived, cast out twice. She
didn't ask for this role. She was handed a script she never auditioned for.
And here's the brutal truth underneath it all: Ishmael exists because Sarah tried to write God's
story for Him.
That's not a dig at Ishmael: he's blessed, he's loved, God hears him, God makes him a great
nation. But his birth was Plan B. His conception was the fruit of Sarah saying, "God's taking too
long. I'll fix this."
Sound familiar?

The Chaos We Create
Have you ever gotten ahead of God? Decided you knew better? Tried to force a door open that
He was keeping closed for a reason?
Maybe you manipulated a relationship because you were tired of being alone.
Maybe you cut a corner at work because you were tired of waiting for the promotion.
Maybe you lied because telling the truth felt too risky.
Maybe you said yes when God said wait, or no when God said go, because you had a better
plan.
Here's what Scripture teaches us over and over and over: When we get ahead of God, when we
build our own towers, when we forge our own way: chaos follows. And people get hurt.
Cain kills Abel.
The flood drowns the world.
Babel scatters the nations.
Abram's lie plagues Pharaoh.
Lot loses his family.
Ishmael gets cast into the desert.
Esau loses his birthright.

And even David: the man after God's own heart: builds his own cart to carry the Ark of the
Covenant. God had given specific instructions: the Ark was to be carried on poles by the
Levites. But David thought he had a better idea. Built a cart. Put the Ark on it. And when the
oxen stumbled and the cart tilted, Uzzah reached out to steady it: and God struck him dead.
Uzzah didn't die because he was wicked. He died because David built his own cart.
When we construct our own carts, things topple. And people get hurt.
It's not that God can't redeem the mess: He absolutely does. That's the whole point of the
gospel. But the pattern is undeniable: human shortcuts lead to human wreckage.
And in Genesis 21, that wreckage has a name: Ishmael, dying of thirst in the desert.

The Boy Under the Bush
Let's go back to him now. Not to rush past this moment, but to feel it.
He's fourteen, maybe fifteen years old. Old enough to understand what's happening. Old
enough to know his father sent him away. Old enough to feel the betrayal.
The water's gone. His lips are cracked. His tongue is swollen. He's stumbling, disoriented, and
his mother: who has fought for him, fled for him, carried him on her back when he couldn't walk
as a baby: now lays him under a bush because she can't bear to watch him die.
And he's crying out. Not to Abraham. Not to Sarah. He's crying out to the God who promised his
mother, "I will make him into a great nation."
But all he feels right now is thirst.
Have you ever been that thirsty? I don't mean physically: though some of you have. I mean soul
thirsty. So desperate for relief, for rescue, for someone to show up and say, "I see you. I hear
you. I'm here."
Ishmael's lying under that bush, and his cry is going up to heaven.
$$Bridge/Transition to Section IV$$
But church, here's the thing: Ishmael's cry in that wilderness wasn't the last cry God would hear
from a son dying of thirst.
Centuries later, there would be another son. Another wilderness: though this one was a hill
called Golgotha. Another moment where the only water in sight was the blood and sweat
running down a broken body.
And from that cross, another voice would cry out:
"I thirst."

IV. EXPERIENCING THE GOSPEL (YEA)
God Heard the Voice of the Boy
Let's go back to that desert. Back to Hagar, sitting a bowshot away, unable to watch her son die.
Back to Ishmael, fourteen years old, lying under a bush, lips cracked, tongue swollen.
And he cries out.
A desperate, anguished wail. The sound of a boy who knows he's dying. The sound of someone
who's been rejected, cast out, abandoned.
And then:
"God heard the voice of the boy."
Not Hagar's cry. Not her anguish. The boy's cry. The rejected son. The one nobody wanted. The
collateral damage of Sarah's shortcut. The kid cast out into the wilderness with nothing but a
skin of water and a loaf of bread.
God heard him.
And then the angel of God calls from heaven: "Do not be afraid."
Don't be afraid, Hagar. Don't be afraid, Ishmael. I see you. I hear you. I'm here.
And then comes the moment that changes everything:
"God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water."
Church, catch this: The well was already there. God didn't create water out of nothing. He didn't
rain down manna from heaven or split the rock. The provision already existed. Hagar just
couldn't see it. Her grief, her despair, her certainty that death was the only outcome: it blinded
her to the water that was right there the whole time.
God opened her eyes. She saw. She filled the skin. She gave the boy a drink. And he lived.

Ishmael Is Us
But here's what I need you to understand: Ishmael isn't just Ishmael.
Ishmael is us.
Go all the way back. Genesis 3. The Garden. The serpent whispers, "You will be like God." Eve
reaches for the fruit. Adam stands silent. They eat. And in that moment, a contagion enters the
human bloodstream.

Sin. Rebellion. Independence. The toxic belief that we can write our own story, be our own gods,
decide for ourselves what's good and evil.
And what happens?
They're cast out. Exiled. The cherubim with flaming swords block the way back to the Tree of
Life. Humanity is in the wilderness now. Cut off from the promise. Dying of thirst.
That's Ishmael under the bush.
And church, that's you and me.
We're the first son: the First Adam: infected with the Genesis 3 contagion, cast out of Eden,
wandering in the wilderness, spiritually dying, crying out, "God, where are You? Do You see
me? Do You hear me?"
Hagar laying Ishmael under that bush and turning away because she can't bear to watch him
die? That's humanity trying to cope with the reality of death. We turn our heads. We distract
ourselves. We numb the pain. Because we can't face the fact that we're dying and there's
nothing we can do about it.
But God hears the cry of the dying boy.
God hears your cry.

The Pattern of Grace
When God opened Hagar's eyes and she saw the well, that wasn't just a one-time miracle for
one rejected kid in the desert. That was a pattern. A type. A shadow pointing forward to
something greater.
Fast forward four hundred years. Exodus 17. The entire nation of Israel is in the wilderness.
They've left Egypt, crossed the Red Sea, and now they're at a place called Rephidim. And
there's no water.
They're dying of thirst. The whole nation. And they cry out: just like Ishmael cried out.
And God tells Moses, "Take your staff. Strike the rock. Water will come out of it."
Moses strikes the rock. And water gushes out. Not a trickle. Not a seep. A flood. Enough for two
million people and all their livestock to drink.
Later, the Apostle Paul looks back at that moment and says, "The Rock was Christ" (1
Corinthians 10:4).
Wait. What?

Paul's saying that rock in the wilderness: the one Moses struck, the one that gave water, the
one that kept Israel alive: was a type. A shadow. A preview of Jesus.
The water Ishmael drank from that well? It sustained him. For a day. Maybe a week. But
eventually, he'd thirst again.
The water Israel drank from the rock at Rephidim? It kept them alive. But they'd need more
water. And more. And more.
The pattern was pointing forward.
Pointing to Someone who would say, "Whoever drinks the water I give them will never thirst.
Indeed, the water I give them will become in them a spring of water welling up to eternal life"
(John 4:14).

Christ Becomes Ishmael
But here's where it gets devastating.
Jesus doesn't just stand at a distance and provide the water like God did for Ishmael.
Jesus becomes Ishmael.
Let that sink in.
The Second Adam doesn't just observe the First Adam dying in the wilderness. He enters the
wilderness. He takes our place. He becomes us.
Listen to the language. Listen to how the story of Ishmael echoes in the story of the cross:
Hagar turns away. She sits at a distance because she cannot bear to watch her son die.
The Father turns His face. When Jesus hangs on that cross, bearing the weight of our sin: the
Genesis 3 contagion: the Father turns away. Not because He doesn't love the Son, but because
He cannot look upon sin.
Ishmael is dying of thirst. His lips are cracked. His tongue is swollen. His body is shutting down.
Jesus cries out, "I thirst" (John 19:28). Not just physical thirst. Spiritual thirst. The thirst of a soul
separated from God. The thirst of bearing the weight of every human rebellion, every act of
independence, every time we tried to build our own towers and write our own stories.
Ishmael cries out. A loud, desperate wail. The sound of a dying boy.
Jesus cries out with a loud voice (Matthew 27:46, 50). And He doesn't cry random words. He
cries Psalm 22:
"My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?"

Church, do you know what Psalm 22 is? It's the song of the suffering servant. Written a
thousand years before crucifixion was even invented. And it describes: in excruciating detail:
what Jesus would experience on the cross:
"My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" (v. 1)

The Vaccine That Transforms
Here's the theology underneath it all:
Genesis 3 unleashed a contagion into the human race. Sin. Death. Separation from God. Every
human born after Adam and Eve inherited it. We're all infected. We're all dying. We're all
Ishmael under the bush.
And no amount of religion, morality, self-improvement, or good intentions can cure it. We need
an antidote.
But here's the problem: the only antidote to sin is death. The wages of sin is death. Someone
has to die. The contagion has to be dealt with.
So God does the unthinkable.
He sends His Son: the Second Adam, the Beloved, the Sinless One: into the wilderness. And on
a hill called Golgotha, outside the city gates, Jesus is lifted up on a cross. And He doesn't just
die a physical death.
He drinks the cup of God's wrath.
He takes the Genesis 3 contagion: your sin, my sin, the sin of the whole world: and He lets it
wash over Him. The full weight. The full fury. The full consequence.
He becomes sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). And the Father, who cannot look upon sin, turns His
face away.
And Jesus cries out: "I thirst."
He thirsts so we don't have to.
And then, He dies.
And in that death, something cosmic happens.
Jesus doesn't just become the antidote to the contagion. He becomes the vaccine.
An antidote cures the person who's already sick. But a vaccine? A vaccine transforms you. It
doesn't just heal what's broken. It makes you immune. It changes you from the inside out. It
makes you a carrier of life instead of death.
That's what Jesus does.

His blood: poured out like a drink offering: becomes the cure for the contagion. Death is
swallowed up. The power of sin is broken. And three days later, the Rock that was struck rises
from the dead.
And what flows from that resurrection?
Rivers of living water.
On the day of Pentecost, fifty days after the resurrection, Jesus pours out the Holy Spirit on His
followers. The same Spirit that hovered over the waters in Genesis 1. The same Spirit that gave
Ezekiel the vision of the valley of dry bones coming back to life.
Living water.
Not from a well in the desert. Not from a rock in the wilderness. But from the pierced side of the
crucified and risen Christ.
The Apostle John saw it happen. When the Roman soldier pierced Jesus' side with a spear,
"blood and water flowed out" (John 19:34).
Blood and water.
The antidote to the contagion.
The vaccine that transforms.
And now, Jesus stands and says to anyone who's thirsty: anyone dying in the wilderness,
anyone crying out under the bush, anyone who knows they're infected and desperate for
rescue:
"Let anyone who is thirsty come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me, as Scripture has
said, rivers of living water will flow from within them" (John 7:37-38).

Sarah took the reins. Abraham went along. Ishmael was born from a shortcut, not a promise.
And the chaos that followed: the rivalry, the mocking, the expulsion: that's what happens when

But even in the mess: even in the wilderness, even under the bush, even at the moment of

is.

water in the desert.

Because the God who cleaned up Sarah's mess, who rescued Ishmael from death, who brought
Isaac and Ishmael together at Abraham's grave: that same God sent His own Son into the
wilderness to take our place.

Who Can Open the Scroll?
Turn with me to Revelation 5.
John is in heaven. He sees the Father on the throne, holding a scroll: sealed with seven seals.
And an angel proclaims with a loud voice:
"Who is worthy to break the seals and open the scroll?"
Now, here's the question: What IS the scroll?
I believe it's the legal document of the covenant. The inheritance deed for God's sons. The title
to everything God promised humanity from the very beginning.
Remember Genesis 1? God made Adam: the first son: and said, "Be fruitful, multiply, fill the
earth, subdue it, have dominion." That was the inheritance. That was the scroll. Given to the first
son.
But the first son: Adam: sinned. Reached for the fruit. Listened to the serpent. Got cast out of
Eden. And in that moment, he was disqualified from opening the scroll.
So who can open it now?
John looks around heaven. And no one is found worthy.
Not Job: the Bible calls him blameless, upright, a man who feared God. But he couldn't open it.
Not David: a man after God's own heart, the king who wrote the Psalms. But he couldn't open it.
Not Moses: who talked with God face to face, who received the Law on Mount Sinai. But he
couldn't open it.
Not Isaiah: the prophet who saw the Lord high and lifted up. But he couldn't open it.
Not Jeremiah, Daniel, Ezekiel: none of the prophets. They couldn't open it.
Not Peter, James, John, or Paul: the apostles who walked with Jesus, who planted churches,
who wrote Scripture. They couldn't open it.

Not even an angel could open it. Because the inheritance wasn't given to angels. It was given to
man.
So the requirement is this: Someone has to be human: because the inheritance belongs to
humans. But someone also has to be holy: completely undefiled, never touched by sin. Because
only someone who never forfeited the inheritance can claim it.
And John weeps. Because no one is found worthy.

The Lamb Who Was Slain
But then:
Revelation 5:5-6:
"Then one of the elders said to me, 'Do not weep! See, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root
of David, has triumphed. He is able to open the scroll and its seven seals.' Then I saw a Lamb,
looking as if it had been slain, standing at the center of the throne."
Church, catch this:
John is told to look for a Lion: the symbol of power, kingship, dominion. The conquering King.
But when he looks, he sees a Lamb: a sacrificial victim, looking as if it had been slain.
And that Lamb? He's a man. Fully human. A beneficiary of the inheritance given to Adam.
But that Lamb is also a Lion. Fully God. Undefiled. Uncorrupted. Holy.
He's the only One who qualifies.
He never sinned. He never forfeited the inheritance. But He chose to enter the wilderness
anyway.
He felt the abandonment: "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?"
He experienced the thirst: "I thirst."
He took the chaos, the contagion, the curse of Genesis 3: and He bore it in His body on the
cross.
But here's the difference:
He didn't stay in the wilderness.
He didn't stay under the bush.
He didn't stay in the grave.

Three days later, the Lamb rose as the Lion.
Death died. Sin was defeated. The scroll was opened. The inheritance was unlocked.
And now, Revelation 5:12 says, the angels and elders and living creatures cry out:
"Worthy is the Lamb, who was slain, to receive power and wealth and wisdom and strength and
honor and glory and praise!"
