From “Come and See” To “Catchers of Men”
Big Idea:
Jesus doesn’t recruit disciples with a one-time altar call. He walks them through stages: from
curiosity, to commitment, to full-time mission. Along the way he builds a community where
natural enemies sit at the same table because their new identity in Christ is stronger than their
old labels.

1. Hook: When The Teacher Comes To You
Goal: Help students feel how shocking Jesus’ call really was.
● Starter story you can tell:
“Picture a village mechanic who never finished school. He is covered in grease, trying to
keep an old matatu running. A famous professor walks right into the shop, looks at him
and says, ‘I want you on my research team. Leave the tools. Come with me.’ He didn’t
apply. He didn’t qualify. He was chosen.”
● Bridge to 1st century:
○ In normal Jewish rabbi–student life:
■ Only the best boys kept advancing:
Bet Sefer → Bet Talmud → Bet Midrash.
■ The student went to the rabbi and begged, “Take me.”
■ If the rabbi thought you didn’t have it, you went home to the family trade.
○ Jesus flips the system:
■ The rabbi goes looking for the students.
■ He skips the elite schools and walks up to fishermen and a tax collector.
■ “Follow me” is not “sit in my class.” It is “reorder your entire life around
me.”

Teaching line:
“These guys are not Bible college stars. They are the ones who washed out of

Bible school and went back to the boats. Those are the men Jesus builds the
church with.”
Transition: “To see how that call unfolds, we have to watch it in stages.”

2. Stage One – John 1: ‘Come and See’
Initial contact: low pressure, high relationship
Text: John 1:35–51

a) From John’s movement to Jesus’ team
● Setting:
○ Andrew and the unnamed disciple (probably John) are already disciples of John
the Baptist.
○ John points at Jesus: “Behold, the Lamb of God.”
○ This is a transfer of loyalty: the forerunner hands them to the Messiah.

Line: “John doesn’t hoard his followers. A real pastor is happy when people move
closer to Jesus, even if it means they move past him.”

b) Jesus’ first words: “What are you seeking?”
● Jesus starts with a heart question, not a command: “What do you seek?”
● They answer: “Rabbi, where are you staying?”
○ They want proximity, not just a miracle.
○ John uses the verb meno (to stay, abide) which becomes a key theme for life with
Jesus.
● Jesus’ reply: “Come and see.”
○ Invitation to investigate.

○ Not “sign this card,” but “walk with me, watch me.”

c) Relational chain reaction
● Andrew spends the day with Jesus, then:
○ Finds his brother Simon: “We have found the Messiah.”
○ Jesus renames him “Cephas / Peter” on the spot, claiming his future.
● Jesus finds Philip, Philip finds Nathanael:
○ Nathanael’s skepticism: “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?”
○ Philip’s answer is the same line: “Come and see.”
○ Jesus answers Nathanael’s doubt with personal knowledge (“I saw you under the
fig tree”).

Takeaway for students:
● Stage 1 discipleship = “come and see.”
○ Commitment is low. They still have jobs.
○ They are checking Jesus out at close range.
● Evangelism pattern:
○ Meet Jesus
○ Tell your family and friends
○ Invite: “Come and see for yourself.”

Classroom application question:
“Who in your life might be at the ‘come and see’ stage, not ready to ‘leave the nets’
yet, but ready to take a closer look?”

3. Stage Two – Mark 1 / Matthew 4: ‘Follow Me’
Call to be students and leave security
Texts: Mark 1:16–20, Matthew 4:18–22

a) Mark’s spotlight on urgency
● Mark’s style:
○ Short, sharp scenes with that favorite word “immediately” (euthus).
● The scene:
○ Jesus walks along the Sea of Galilee.
○ Simon and Andrew are casting nets.
○ Jesus: “Follow me, and I will make you become fishers of men.”
○ “Immediately they left their nets and followed him.”

What has changed from John 1?
● Jesus is no longer, “Come spend the afternoon.”
● Now it is “Come after me” as a life direction.
● They know him now. This is not a stranger’s hypnotic call. It is escalation.

b) The metaphor: “Fishers of men”
● In the Old Testament, “fishers” language often pictured God catching people in judgment
(Jer 16:16).
● Jesus retools the image:
○ Same idea of pulling people out of the water.
○ But the goal shifts from judgment to rescue: drawing people out of chaos into
God’s kingdom.

c) Matthew’s framing: light in Galilee
● Matthew quotes Isaiah 9:1–2 before the call:
○ “Galilee of the Gentiles… the people dwelling in darkness have seen a great
light.”
● Why it matters:
○ Galilee had a mixed reputation. Not Jerusalem. Not the holy center.
○ The first disciples are called in “the wrong region” to prove the kingdom starts on
the margins.

d) The cost they feel
● “They left their nets”:
○ Nets and boats were serious capital, not cheap tools.
○ Walking away means walking away from economic stability.
● James and John leave their father Zebedee:
○ In a family-based culture that is massive.
○ Loyalty to the father ranks at the top of social duties.
○ Jesus’ call now stands above even family expectations.

Takeaway:
● Stage 2 discipleship = “follow me” as students.
○ They become full-time apprentices.
○ They are rearranging their lives around the Teacher.
● Question for class:
○ “Where might Jesus be pushing you from ‘come and see’ to ‘follow me’ in a more
costly way?”

4. Stage Three – Luke 5: ‘Catchers Of Men’
Call to full-time mission after the miraculous catch
Text: Luke 5:1–11

a) Luke fills in the emotional backstory
● Luke tells us:
○ Jesus already healed Simon’s mother in law before this scene.
○ Simon has seen Jesus up close in his own house.
● So when Jesus steps into Simon’s boat, Simon already respects him.

b) The failed night and the crazy command
● Fishermen have worked all night and caught nothing.
● Jesus tells a tired professional:
○ “Put out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch.”
● Simon’s honest tension:
○ “Master, we toiled all night and took nothing, but at your word I will let down the
nets.”
● Daytime deep-water fishing with those nets makes no sense, but he obeys.

c) The sign that breaks the boat and the heart
● The catch is so large that:
○ Nets begin to tear
○ They signal partners

○ Both boats start to sink.
● For a fisherman, this is the dream payday.

Peter’s reaction is shocking:
● He does not shout “Jackpot.”
● He falls at Jesus’ knees:
○ “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord.”
● This mirrors Isaiah’s reaction in the temple vision. Confronted with real holiness, the first
instinct is “I don’t belong here. I’m dirty.”

Jesus’ reply:
● “Do not be afraid.” Standard heaven-side greeting.
● “From now on you will be catching men.”

Luke uses a special verb: zogreo
● Means “to capture alive.”
● Not catch and kill, but catch and bring into life.

“They left everything and followed him.”
● Not just nets this time, but:
○ The biggest catch of their career still flopping in the boats.
● God calls some people right at the moment of visible success.

Takeaway:
● Stage 3 discipleship = “catchers of people.”
○ From students to co-workers in mission.

○ God often calls us deeper right when life feels comfortable or successful.

Good discussion question:
“Why does God sometimes call people in the moment of success instead of
failure? What does that show about what he values?”

5. The Progression In One Picture
You can sketch this on the board:
1. Come and see (John 1)
○ Location: Judea / Bethany beyond Jordan
○ Status: Investigators / new believers
○ Cost: Time and curiosity
2. Follow me (Mark 1 / Matt 4)
○ Location: Sea of Galilee
○ Status: Full-time students
○ Cost: Nets, boats, family expectations
3. From now on you will catch people (Luke 5)
○ Location: Sea of Galilee, same basic scene with more detail
○ Status: Mission partners
○ Cost: Even the “big catch,” not just the failed night

Later in the Gospels this continues:
4. Be with me – choosing the Twelve (Mark 3:13–15)
5. Come and die – take up your cross (Matt 16:24)

But in this lecture you are focusing on the first three steps.

6. Unity In Diversity – Matthew The Collaborator & Simon
The Zealot
Now pivot from “how they were called” to “who was called.”

a) Matthew the tax collector (Levi)
● Matthew sits at a customs booth in Capernaum:
○ A mokhes tax man, not just a basic tax clerk.
○ He can stop travelers, search their goods, set inflated fees.
● How Jews saw him:
○ Political traitor working for Rome.
○ Ritually unclean from constant contact with Gentiles.
○ Socially excommunicated. His testimony would not count in a Jewish court.
● When he gets up and walks away:
○ He leaves a very profitable career.
○ Unlike fishermen, he cannot just go back. Rome does not give his old spot back
to a deserter.

b) Simon the Zealot
● Called “Simon the Zealot” (Luke 6:15) or “the Cananaean” (from “zealous”).
● Likely linked to the Zealot movement:
○ Radical theocratic nationalists.
○ Motto: “No king but God.”

○ Opposed paying taxes to Rome; saw collaboration as treason against God.
● The extreme wing, the Sicarii:
○ Carried daggers under cloaks.
○ Targeted collaborators and elites supporting Rome.

In plain terms:
● Simon’s people killed Matthew’s friends.
● Matthew’s job funded the army that crucified Simon’s friends.

c) Jesus’ table as a miracle
● On Jesus’ team:
○ The tax man and the radical nationalist eat from the same pot, sleep on the same
ground, share the same mission.
● Jesus does not simply pick men in the same social bubble.
○ He picks people who would normally hate each other.
○ He does not baptize either political agenda.
○ He calls both to die to that identity and put his kingdom first.

Line you can use:
“In any other setting, Simon would think it was an act of worship to put a knife in
Matthew’s back. Jesus makes them teammates. That is not tolerance. That is
resurrection-level power.”
Application question for class (careful, but real):
● “Who are the ‘Matthew and Simon’ sides in your context – tribe, party, class,
denomination? What would it look like for Jesus to put them at the same table and send
them on mission together?”

7. Landing The Plane: What This Means For Us
Give students 3 simple hooks to take away:
1. Discipleship is a journey, not a single moment.
○ Jesus moves people from come and see, to follow me, to catch people.
○ Be patient with where others are, but also honest about the next step.
2. Jesus calls the unlikely, not the impressive.
○ Failed rabbinical students, fishermen, a corrupt tax agent, and a radical
nationalist.
○ Their qualification is not their resume, but their willingness to leave nets and
booths when he speaks.
3. The miracle is not just changed individuals, but a new community.
○ When Matthew and Simon can pray together, we are seeing the kingdom on
display.
○ A church that only gathers people “just like us” is not yet showing the full beauty
of Jesus’ call.

How this could play in class
● Read small chunks of each passage out loud and “walk through the story” with
commentary rather than front-loading all the background.
● Use the board diagram for the three stages so they see the progression visually.
● End with a short discussion:
○ “Which stage describes most Christians you know?”
○ “What would it mean for our churches to be places where Matthews and Simons
actually serve together?”
