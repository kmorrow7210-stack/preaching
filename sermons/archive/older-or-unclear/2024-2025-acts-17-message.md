1 Corinthians 8:4-6

Introduction
I want to give you a little background on the letter we’re pulling our theme from: Paul’s first letter
to the Corinthians.
If you want to picture Corinth, basically picture Las Vegas… with Greek philosophy.
It was a major Roman port city. Which means it was packed with travelers, packed with money,
and packed with temples. Idols on every street corner.
And for the people living there, that wasn’t just background noise. Idolatry was woven into daily
life.
If your boss threw a party, it was at a temple.
If you went to the market to buy meat for dinner, that meat was almost certainly leftovers from a
pagan sacrifice. You could not avoid it.
So you get this brand-new church planted right in the middle of that.
And the church is a mixed bag. You’ve got Jewish believers who are scandalized by all of it. And
you’ve got new Gentile converts who… maybe just last week… were in those temples,
worshiping those idols. They have history. They have baggage. They have trauma.
And that creates a huge problem.
Some of the believers, the “knowledge” Christians, are flexing.
They’re saying, “We’re free in Christ! We know an idol is nothing. It’s just stone. It’s just meat. It
doesn’t matter.”
And… they’re technically right.
But Paul has to write and say, “Hold on. What about your weaker brother?”
“For you, it’s just a meal. For him, the guy who just got saved out of that cult,
watching you eat that meat is like taking a recovering addict back into the old
house. It’s triggering. It’s wounding him. Your freedom is becoming your brother’s
downfall.”
Paul’s whole point is this:
Love is more important than liberty.
And your rights stop where your brother’s wounds begin.

So when Paul confronts this, he actually quotes their own slogan back at them. And that’s where
we’ll pick it up, in 1 Corinthians chapter 8, starting at verse 4.

Transition
That passage we just read from 1 Corinthians… “many gods and many lords”… Paul is
pastoring a church that is drowning in idol culture.
But the problem wasn’t just in Corinth. That’s the symptom.
Luke, in Acts 17, shows us the disease.
If 1 Corinthians 8 shows us the pastoral fallout of idolatry, Acts 17 shows us the spiritual
environment it grows in.
And just like in Corinth, the problem is not new.
We live in a world of “many gods.”
● The god of politics that promises salvation if our side wins.
● The god of success, that demands we sacrifice our families on its altar.
● The god of self, that says our personal happiness is the highest possible good.

And just like the Athenians, we are surrounded by a marketplace of ideas. Social media,
24-hour news, a constant scroll of opinions, and, as Luke says of Athens, people who “spent
their time in nothing else but either to tell or to hear something new.”
So the question for us, church, is the same question Paul is answering in both of these cities:
How do we live faithful to the “one God” and the “one Lord, Jesus Christ” in a world
full of “many gods”?
Paul gives the theology in Corinthians.
Luke gives us the travel log in Acts.
In Acts 17, Luke gives us three case studies, back to back. And I want to walk us into the middle
one this morning.

OOPS – Upsetting the Equilibrium
While Paul is waiting for Silas and Timothy in Athens, something happens to him.

Luke gives us one word for it.
Most of your Bibles say:
“His spirit was provoked within him when he saw that the city was full of idols.”
(Acts 17:16)
The Greek word behind “provoked” is parōxyneto.
You’ve heard its cousin in English: paroxysm.
It comes from a verb that means “to sharpen, to jab, to stir up.” In English, a paroxysm is a
sudden attack, a sharp flare.
This is not Paul going, “Huh, that’s interesting. Look at all this cultural diversity.”
This is a sudden inner storm.
His spirit seizes up.
And Luke uses this word on purpose.
This exact form only shows up here in the New Testament, and the verb itself only shows up
one other time, in 1 Corinthians 13, where Paul says, “Love is not easily provoked.”
So whatever Paul is feeling here, it’s not petty outrage. It is love that has hit a breaking point.
In the Greek Old Testament, the Septuagint, this same verb gets used for God’s reaction to
idolatry:
● When Israel makes the golden calf in the wilderness.
● When they bow to Baal.
● When they trade the living God for dead statues.
So Luke is tapping into that story.
Paul is not just mildly bothered.
He is feeling about their idols what God feels.
Why?
Because Athens, brilliant, sophisticated Athens, was drowning in gods.
Luke says the city was kateidōlon – under idols. Swamped. Dominated.

Ancient writers said Athens had over 30,000 public statues, plus all the household idols stuffed
into homes. One Roman satirist said it was “easier to find a god in Athens than a man.”
Everywhere Paul looks:
● Apollo.
● Athena.
● Poseidon.
● Demeter.
● Mystery cults from Egypt.
● Dionysus at the theater on the side of the Acropolis.
It is a forest of altars.
And what wrecks him is that they think they’re enlightened. They think they’re seekers.
Paul sees a city enslaved.

The Modern Paroxysm
Let me tell you when I felt a taste of that.
2024 Summer Olympics. Paris, France. Opening ceremonies.
They staged a scene that looked a whole lot like the Last Supper. Long table. Performers lined
up. Drag. And in the center, where Jesus would be, a figure painted blue, presented as
Dionysus, the Greek god of wine and chaos.
Christians all over the world were provoked.
Not just annoyed. Provoked.
Parōxyneto.
And the defenders came out immediately:
“You’re overreacting. It’s just art. It’s not even an accurate painting. Calm down.”
I made a post about it. And someone I went to high school with commented:
“Don’t attack my Dionysus. You should read a history book sometime.”

Now, I didn’t send her my bibliography. I didn’t have to.
But I do know a little about Dionysus.

The God of Losing Yourself
Dionysus is the Greek god of wine, ecstasy, madness, fertility.
His worshipers, the Maenads, would get so intoxicated in their rituals they could tear animals
apart with their bare hands. That was considered holy.
Dionysus worship was not “have a nice drink and relax.”
It was about losing control.
Breaking boundaries.
Rejecting authority.
Indulging every desire.
And here’s the key:
In that worship, getting drunk wasn’t just something that happened at the party.
Getting drunk was the worship.
Wine-induced ecstasy was the pathway to “the divine.”
You lose yourself. You feel carried.
Temporary transcendence through intoxication.
Now, do I think that person from high school literally bows down to Dionysus? No.
But here’s what provoked me:
A lot of people don’t worship Dionysus by name, but they devote their lives to what he stands
for.
● “Follow your heart, no matter what.”
● “You do you. No rules, no boundaries.”
● “The goal of life is to lose yourself in pleasure, in experience, in good vibes.”
● “Mock self-sacrifice. Celebrate self-expression.”

That’s not Christianity.
That’s Dionysus.
So when I saw Dionysus sitting in the place of Christ at that table –
Chaos where there should be sacrifice.
Revelry where there should be redemption.
It provoked me.
Because I realized: this isn’t just about the Olympics.

What Paul Saw in Athens
Athens had Dionysus too.
At the base of the Acropolis, cut into the hill, was the Theatre of Dionysus, where the great
festivals took place. Every year, the City Dionysia. Plays. Wine. Revelry. Acropolis Tickets+1
But it wasn’t just Dionysus.
After the glory days of Athens faded, the city became less of a shared story and more of a
marketplace.
Not just a marketplace of goods.
A marketplace of gods.
A marketplace of identities.
A marketplace of philosophies.
A marketplace of “salvation strategies.”
● Stoics promised peace through control.
● Epicureans promised peace through pleasure.
● Mystery cults promised transcendence through ritual.
● Political tribes promised belonging through loyalty.

Every one of them said:
“Worship here. Belong here. Sacrifice here. Then you’ll finally be saved.”

Athens thought it was enlightened.
They thought they were open-minded seekers.
Paul saw a city enslaved by its own options.
And that is when his spirit went into paroxysm.
Because he knew something they didn’t know.
And that’s where you’re headed next with the UGH/AHA:
that the God they think is unknown is not hiding, and the proof is an empty tomb.

UGH – Analyzing the Discrepency
Paul’s spirit seizes in Athens because he knows something they don’t.
So what does he do with that?
He doesn’t grab a megaphone and scream “Idolaters!”
He doesn’t start a boycott of the Parthenon.
He gets invited to speak at the Areopagus, the high council, the TED Talk stage of Athens. And
listen to how he starts.
“So Paul, standing in the midst of the Areopagus, said:
‘Men of Athens, I perceive that in every way you are very religious.
For as I passed along and observed the objects of your worship, I found also an
altar with this inscription, “To the unknown god.”
What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you.’”
(Acts 17:22–23)
Now that first line is genius.
“I see that in every way you are very religious.”
The Greek there can mean “devout” or “superstitious.” It is a little slippery. If you say it with a
smile, it’s a compliment. If you say it with an eyebrow raised, it’s a rebuke.
Paul is doing both.
He’s saying,
“I see your hunger. I see your sincerity. I see your effort.”
And at the same time,
“I see your confusion. I see your fear. I see your bondage.”
Then he brings up this altar.

Somewhere in that forest of temples and shrines, Paul has seen a little stone with a carved
inscription:
“To an unknown god.”
The Athenians had altars like that as a kind of spiritual insurance policy.
“In case we missed one. In case there is some god out there we forgot to honor. Just to be
safe, this one is for you.”
That altar is a confession.
It is the most honest thing in the whole city.
“We have gods on every corner, but we are still not sure we have met the God
behind it all.”
And Paul grabs that confession and says:
“What you worship as unknown, I now announce to you.”
In other words:
“You admitted you don’t know. I am here to tell you that God is not hiding.”
The problem in Athens is not that God has been unclear.
The problem is that they prefer Him unclear.
Because an unknown god is safe.
● An unknown god cannot tell you to repent.
● An unknown god cannot confront your idols.
● An unknown god cannot say “no” to your favorite sin.
● An unknown god can be whatever you need Him to be this week.
As long as He stays unknown, they stay in control.
● He's not safe. He's not controllable. He's not customizable.
● He's not a hired therapist who affirms all your choices.
● He's not a cosmic vending machine who dispenses blessings when you insert good
deeds.
● He's not a distant force you can ignore six days a week and acknowledge on Sunday.
● He's the God who made the world and everything in it. (v. 24)
● He's the Lord of heaven and earth. (v. 24)
● He doesn't live in temples made by hands. (v. 24)

“Spiritual but not religious”
How many times have you heard this line:
“I’m spiritual, but not religious.”
“I believe in a higher power.”
“I believe the universe has a plan.”
“I’m into good energy, good vibes.”
That is our altar to an unknown god.
It sounds humble.
“I don’t want to be dogmatic. I’m just seeking.”
But a lot of the time, it is not humility. It is strategy.
It is a way to keep whatever is “out there” from getting too close.
● “Higher power” sounds safe. “Lord” sounds intrusive.
● “The universe” sounds inspiring. “Judge of the living and the dead” sounds
uncomfortable.
● “Good vibes” never disagree with you. Jesus sometimes will.
So what do we do?
We keep God in the fog.
We like saying “everything happens for a reason” as long as the Reason never asks for
obedience.
We like saying “I’m on a journey” as long as the journey never ends at a throne.

Our culture is perfectly fine with a God who stays vague, abstract, inoffensive.
It is not fine with a God who walks out of a tomb in a specific body and starts talking.
That is Athens.

Spiritual insurance
That altar in Athens was a backup plan.
“If Zeus can’t fix it, maybe the unknown god will.”
“If Athena lets us down, maybe there’s Someone else out there.”
That is what a lot of “spiritual but not religious” talk is.
It is spiritual insurance.
● “I don’t really want a church. I don’t want doctrine. I don’t want commands. But I also
don’t want to be completely on my own, so I’ll keep this little vague ‘something more’ in
my back pocket, just in case.”
So we say things like:
● “I like Jesus, but I don’t believe in hell.”
● “I believe in God, but my God would never judge.”
● “I believe in a higher power, but I don’t think any one religion has the whole truth.”
Do you hear it?
We are not describing God.
We are editing Him.
We are building a god in our own image.
A god who never disagrees.
A god who never corrects.
A god who never says “go” when we want to stay, or “stay” when we want to go.
That is not seeking.
That is avoiding.

Bringing it home
Church folks do the same thing.

● We like sermons about “God’s plan for your life” more than sermons about God’s claim
on your life.
● We like “God loves me as I am” more than “God loves me too much to leave me as I
am.”
● We like “Jesus, my helper,” and we get real quiet when we hit “Jesus, my Lord.”
Those who do so are avoiding a God who is present.
And Paul isn’t finished. Next he begins to say, “Let me tell you who He really is. Let me tell you
why the ‘unknown’ excuse just expired. Let me tell you about a resurrection.”

Aha - Disclosing the Key to the Resolution
Paul's Strategy: Meet Them Where They Are
Now watch what Paul does. This is masterful.
He doesn't walk onto Mars Hill and say: 'You're all going to hell. Repent or burn.'
He doesn't mock their philosophers or tear down their altars.
He doesn't start with: 'Let me tell you what's wrong with everything you believe.'
He starts by quoting their own poets.
Look at verse 28:
'For in him we live and move and have our being; as even some of your own poets have
said, "For we are indeed his offspring."'
Paul just quoted three different Greek poets that the Athenians would have recognized
immediately:
1. Epimenides — a Cretan poet who wrote about Zeus
2. Aratus — a Stoic philosopher from Cilicia (Paul's home region)
3. Cleanthes — another Stoic who wrote hymns to Zeus
These weren't obscure references. This was like walking into a room today and quoting Taylor
Swift, George Jones, and Trump back to back to back.
Everyone knew these lines.
And Paul says: 'Your poets got something right.'

Not everything. But something.
They were groping toward truth. They just didn't know where to find it.
So Paul takes their language—language they trust, language they respect—and he baptizes it
with biblical truth.
He says: 'You've been talking about a God you can't quite name. Let me tell you His name. And
let me show you He's not far off. He's already proven who He is.'"

The "Logos" Strategy: John Did the Same Thing
Paul wasn't the only one who did this.
The Apostle John used the exact same strategy in John chapter 1.
The Stoic philosophers in Athens had a concept they called 'the Logos.'
Have you ever wondered why John 1 begins by calling Jesus the Word?
The term John uses in John 1 is Logos. It meant 'the word,' 'the reason,' 'the rational principle
behind the universe.'
They believed the Logos was the cosmic force that held everything together. The divine reason.
The organizing principle of reality.
It was abstract. Philosophical. Impersonal.
And John—writing to people steeped in Greek philosophy—takes that word and opens his
Gospel with it:
'In the beginning was the Logos, and the Logos was with God, and the Logos was God.'
(John 1:1)
The Greeks are reading this and thinking: 'Okay, yes. The Logos. We know about the Logos.
This makes sense.'
And then John drops the bomb:
'And the Logos became flesh and dwelt among us.' (John 1:14)
Wait. What?
The Logos—the eternal, cosmic, rational principle—became a man?

That's not philosophy anymore. That's incarnation.
That's not an abstract concept. That's Jesus.
John hijacked their language. He took the word they trusted and filled it with a truth that would
wreck their categories.

Paul does the same thing in Athens.
He takes their poets, their language, their search for the divine—and he points them straight to
Jesus."
Modern Translation: We Can Hijack Language Too
And church, here's the thing: We can do the same thing today.
Our culture has its own 'logos' words. Phrases people trust. Concepts people value.
And we don't have to reject all of them. We can hijack them for the gospel.
Let me give you a few examples of how this works.
Example 1: 'I'm on a journey.'
Everybody says this today, right?
'I'm on a spiritual journey. I'm on a journey of self-discovery. I'm still figuring things out.'
Here's what you do say: 'I love that. Tell me more about that journey.'
And then you listen. You really listen.
And when the time is right, you say:
'You know, a journey needs a destination. Otherwise you're just wandering. Jesus said, "I am
the way, the truth, and the life." Not A way. THE way. Maybe the journey you're on is meant to
lead you to Him.'
You didn't reject their language. You baptized it.
Example 2: 'Follow your heart.'
This one's everywhere. Disney movies. Instagram captions. Graduation speeches.
'Follow your heart. Your heart knows the truth. Trust your heart.'

And instead of immediately shutting it down, you can say:
'I hear you. But can I share something the Bible says about the heart? Jeremiah 17:9 says,
"The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?"
Our hearts lie to us all the time. What if instead of following your heart, you let God heal your
heart? What if you followed Him instead?'
You took their language. You didn't mock it. You subverted it with Scripture.
Example 3: 'I believe in setting boundaries with toxic people.'
This is huge in therapy culture right now. And honestly? Sometimes it's valid.
But here's where it can go wrong:
Sometimes people call someone 'toxic' because that person convicted them. That person
challenged their sin. That person spoke truth they didn't want to hear.
And instead of repenting, they cut them off and call it 'boundaries.'

Here’s an example:

Dr. Amanda Calhoun, a Yale University chief psychiatry resident, who gave advice on MSNBC
about how people could cope with the election results during the holidays. Dr. Calhoun stated
that it is "completely fine to not be around" family members who "voted in ways that are against
you" and "against your livelihood". She even suggested telling them why, giving the example: "'I
have a problem with the way that you voted, because it went against my very livelihood and I'm
not going to be around you this holiday'".
This advice was then discussed and amplified by other media figures. Hosts on "The View"
discussed this segment, with Sunny Hostin and Whoopi Goldberg agreeing with the sentiment.
Hostin, for example, said that "many people feel 'someone voted not only against their families
but against them'".
This public advice reflects a wider trend. Social media is also active with people discussing
skipping holiday plans to avoid relatives who voted for a candidate they don't support. This
sentiment is widespread enough that one survey found nearly a quarter of Americans were
considering "avoiding Thanksgiving with their family over the election".
The phenomenon of "political differences" is now recognized as a significant factor in family
estrangement. Another example is Fox News host Jesse Watters being disinvited from his
mother's Thanksgiving, have also become part of the public narrative.

And let me add this:
If you can't sit at a Thanksgiving table with someone who voted differently than you—but Jesus
could eat with tax collectors, prostitutes, and Pharisees—maybe the problem isn't them.
Maybe the problem is that your politics have become your religion.
And you're calling it 'boundaries' when really it's just... idolatry.
So here's how you hijack that language:
'Boundaries are important. Absolutely. But let me ask you something: Is that person toxic? Or
are they convicting? Because Proverbs 27:6 says, "Faithful are the wounds of a friend."
Sometimes the people who love you most are the ones who refuse to affirm your sin. That's not
toxic. That's iron sharpening iron.'
You didn't reject the concept of boundaries. You redirected it toward biblical discernment.
Example 4: 'Everything happens for a reason.'
People say this all the time when something bad happens.
And they mean well. They're trying to find meaning in suffering.
But here's the problem: 'Everything happens for a reason' is karma. It's fate. It's the universe.
That's not Christianity. That's Stoicism.
So here's how you hijack it:
'I think I know what you mean. You're saying there's purpose even in suffering. And you're
right—but not because of karma or fate. Romans 8:28 says, "God works all things together
for good for those who love Him." Not that everything that happens IS good. But that God
can redeem it. That's not the universe. That's a Father who loves you.'
You took the phrase they used. You didn't mock them. You filled it with gospel truth."

Paul's Ultimate Hijack: The Ressurection
The Resurrection: The Proof That Changes Everything
"And that brings us to the moment Paul's been building toward.
He's quoted their poets. He's used their language. He's established common ground.

And now he says the thing that makes half of them walk out.
Verse 30-31:
'The times of ignorance God overlooked, but now he commands all people everywhere to
repent, because he has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by
a man whom he has appointed; and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him
from the dead.'
There it is.
Not philosophy. Not a cosmic principle. Not an abstract Logos.
A man. Who died. And rose from the dead.
Paul didn't argue them into belief. He didn't out-philosophize them. He didn't win a debate.
He announced Jesus.
And he said: 'God proved all of this by raising Him from the dead.'
Because here's the thing:
You can argue about philosophy forever. Stoicism versus Epicureanism versus Platonism.
Round and round.
But you can't argue with an empty tomb.
Either Jesus rose or He didn't.
If He didn't, Paul's a liar and we're all wasting our time.
But if He did?
Then everything changes.
Your Unknown God has a name: Jesus.
Your 'spiritual but not religious' journey has a destination: Jesus.
Your 'follow your heart' philosophy has a Savior who can heal your heart: Jesus.
Your Dionysus worship—losing yourself in pleasure, experience, chaos—has an
alternative: Surrender yourself to Jesus.

And your many gods? They're nothing. Because there's one God. And He proved it by
conquering death."

So that's what Paul preached.
Not philosophy. Not moralism. Not 'try harder to be spiritual.'
Jesus. Resurrected. Proven. Real.
Now, how did Athens respond?
Because that's where it gets interesting..."

STAGE 4 (WHEE) — "Experiencing the Gospel: Three
Responses
Paul's Proclamation Met With Division
So Paul's standing on Mars Hill. He's quoted their poets. He's hijacked their language. He's built
the bridge.
And then he drops the bomb:
'God has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom
he has appointed; and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead.'
(Acts 17:31)
Resurrection.
Not philosophy. Not principles. Not 'be a good person and the universe will reward you.'
A man. Who died. And walked out of a tomb.
And Luke doesn't sugarcoat what happened next.
Verse 32:
'Now when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked. But others said,
"We will hear you again about this." So Paul went out from their midst. But some men
joined him and believed, among whom also were Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman
named Damaris and others with them.'

Three responses.
Let me show you what they look like."

RESPONSE #1: The Sneerers (v. 32a)
The Text
"When they heard about the resurrection of the dead, some began to sneer."
The Response
The Greek word is ἐχλεύαζον (echleuazon)—it means to mock, to ridicule, to scoff.
It's the sound you make when someone says something so absurd you can't even take it
seriously.
'Wait, you're telling me a dead guy came back to life? Like... physically? Come on, Paul. We
thought you were smarter than that.'
These are the folks who were fine with Paul when he was quoting Aratus and talking about the
divine.
'Oh, the Logos! The poets! Paul, you're so culturally literate!'
But the second he said Jesus walked out of a tomb, they were done.
And here's what I need you to hear: Paul didn't chase them.
He didn't soften the resurrection. He didn't say, 'Well, what I MEANT was it's a metaphor for new
beginnings.'
He didn't compromise. He didn't backpedal.
He let them walk.
Because some people don't want truth. They want their Unknown God. The one who demands
nothing and judges nothing. The one they can control.
And you can't argue someone into the resurrection.
You can't logic them into belief. You can't out-philosophize their skepticism.
All you can do is announce it. And let the Holy Spirit do the rest.
Some people will sneer. And that's on them."

RESPONSE #2: The Procrastinators (v. 32b)
The Text
"Others said, 'We will hear you again concerning this.'"
The Response
"Then there's the second group. They didn't sneer. They just... deferred.
'Paul, this is fascinating. Really. Let's put a pin in this. We'll circle back. Maybe grab coffee
sometime and you can tell me more about this... Jesus situation.'
These are the people who show up at church on Christmas and Easter.
Who say, 'I'm on a journey.'
Who have LOTS of questions but never quite get around to answering THE question:
What will you do with Jesus?
They're not hostile. They're just... not ready.
And 'not ready' has a way of becoming 'never ready.'
Because here's the thing about the resurrection: it's not a philosophy you can sample.
It's a claim that demands a response.
If Jesus actually walked out of that tomb, then everything changes.
The Unknown God isn't good enough anymore.
Your own personal spirituality isn't good enough anymore.
Your 'I'll get to it eventually' isn't good enough anymore.
Because THE God has spoken. And He's not interested in being one option among many.
He's not interested in 'we'll talk about this later.'
He's risen. And He's asking: What will you do with Me?
And 'later' isn't an answer."

RESPONSE #3: The Believers (v. 34)
The Text
"But some men joined him and believed, among whom also were Dionysius the
Areopagite and a woman named Damaris and others with them."
The Setup
And then there's the third group.
Luke gives us two names: Dionysius and Damaris. Not the Greek god. A guy’s name.
Dionysius was a member of the Areopagus—the council Paul was speaking to. He was elite.
Educated. Connected. Influential.
The kind of guy who'd offered sacrifices at multiple altars, probably including the Unknown God,
just to cover all his bases.
And Damaris? We don't know much about her. But in a culture where women weren't usually
named in these accounts, the fact that Luke includes her tells us something.
She mattered. Her belief mattered.
And I want you to imagine Dionysius walking home that night."

The Emotional Story: Dionysius Walks Home
He's walked these streets a thousand times.
Past the Parthenon. Past the altar to Athena. Past the statue of Apollo. Past the Theater of
Dionysus at the base of the Acropolis.
Past that little altar tucked in the corner—the one to the Unknown God.
And all those statues? They'd always felt... safe. Like insurance.
'I don't know which god is real, so I'll honor them all. That way I'm covered.'
But tonight, walking home from Mars Hill, everything looks different.
Because Paul didn't give him another god to add to the collection.
Paul gave him THE God who has a name.
Who made the world.

Who doesn't live in temples.
Who doesn't need anything from him.
Who gives him life and breath and everything.
Who set the times and boundaries of his life.
Who's close enough to touch—close enough that he could grope around and find Him.
