I.

Upsetting the Equilibrium

“Have you ever felt like the enemy was winning?”
Not just having a bad week. I mean that deep, sick feeling that maybe the dark is actually
stronger than the light. That maybe what God promised you has been delayed so long it might
be dead.
To feel that, you are standing in very old footsteps.
If you zoom the camera all the way back to the start of the Bible, you find one simple promise
hanging over all of history: a Seed is coming who will crush the serpent’s head. God says it in
Genesis 3:15 while Adam and Eve are still standing in the wreckage of their first sin. The whole
story after that is the enemy trying to erase that Seed.
Cain murders Abel. The first family is already shattered. It looks like the promised line is over
before it starts. But God gives another son, Seth, whose very name means “appointed.” God
quietly rebuilds what the enemy tried to erase.
Human evil then spreads like a virus. By Noah’s day, the earth is so rotten that judgment comes
in a flood. The waters cover everything. If you freeze the frame there, it looks like the serpent
has finally done it. The Seed is washed away. But in one little floating box, God preserves a
family. The Seed is still alive, bobbing on the waves in a wooden coffin that refuses to sink.
Then comes Babel. Humanity bands together, not to worship God, but to climb over Him. One
language, one tower, one proud project. God scatters them, scrambles their tongues, and it
looks like the line has dissolved into the nations. But out of that scattered mess, God points at
one old man in the back of nowhere and says, “You. Abraham. Through your Seed all the
families of the earth will be blessed.” The line is narrowed, not lost.
Later, Pharaoh stares out over a river full of Hebrew baby boys. He is throwing sons into the Nile
like trash, trying to choke out the future of Israel in its crib. If you stood on that riverbank, it
would be very easy to say, “The enemy is winning.” But there is one crying baby in a basket,
drifting right into Pharaoh’s own house. The boy who should have died is raised in the palace
and becomes the man God uses to walk Israel out of slavery. The Seed line walks out with
them.
Generations later, in Persia, a man named Haman writes a law that will wipe out every Jew in
the empire in a single day. An entire people group scheduled for execution. The gallows are
already built. Again it feels like the serpent finally has the promise by the throat. But God has
already placed a Jewish girl named Esther in the palace. The plot flips. The one who planned
the genocide hangs on his own gallows, and the people of the Seed are given rest.

Over and over the pattern repeats:
Attack.
Judge.
Restore.
The enemy strikes, God answers, and somehow the line of promise keeps breathing.
Then God makes the target even smaller.
He does not just say, “A Seed will come.” He says, “That Seed will come through one family, one
throne, one man’s house.” In 2 Samuel 7, God looks at David and promises, “I will raise up your
Seed after you… I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever.” The Seed now runs straight
through David’s sons and sits on David’s chair.
For roughly four hundred years, that promise holds in public view. Kings come and go. Some are
faithful, many are corrupt. Empires rise around them. Armies batter the walls of Jerusalem. Idols
creep into the Temple. But through all the chaos, there is still a son of David sitting on that
throne. The line is bruised, but not broken. The lamp in David’s house never fully goes out.
That is the equilibrium moment in the story:
“The promise is untouchable. The Seed will come. The serpent can’t stop it.”
If you had lived then, you would have looked at that throne and thought, “We’re safe. No matter
how ugly it gets, this line cannot fail.”
And that is exactly when God lets the story get far more dangerous than anyone expected.

II.

Analyzing the Discrepancy

You have to understand, this wasn't just about a family tree. This wasn't God saying, "I'm firing
the Democrats and hiring the Republicans." This wasn't God saying, "I'm swapping the house of
David for another royal family."
This was God saying, "You are done being your own people."
The promise of 2 Samuel 7 wasn't just that they would have a lineage; it was that they would
have sovereignty. It meant they answered to God, and nobody else. It meant they had their own
borders, their own laws, and their own King.
But in Jeremiah 22, God looks at Jehoiachin—this young, arrogant king, the grandson of the
good king Josiah—and He pronounces a sentence that shakes the foundations of the earth.

God says, "As I live, declares the Lord, though Coniah the son of Jehoiakim, king of Judah, were
the signet ring on my right hand, yet I would tear you off."
Do you know what a signet ring was? It wasn't jewelry. It wasn't a Super Bowl ring you put in a
case. The signet ring was the King’s signature. It was the power of attorney. If you stamped that
ring in wax, it carried the full weight of the authority of the King. It was the credit card with no
limit.
And God looks at the line of David and says, "I am cutting up the card. I am revoking your
authority. I am tearing the ring off my hand."
And then the hammer drops in verse 30:
"Write this man down as childless, a man who shall not prosper in his days; for none of his
offspring shall prosper, sitting on the throne of David and ruling anymore in Judah."
God wasn't saying Jehoiachin wouldn't have biological kids. He had plenty of kids. We have the
records; he had seven sons in exile.
God was saying, "His kids don't count."
God was saying, "The legal right to rule stops here."
And almost immediately, the Babylonians came over the wall.
Nebuchadnezzar didn't just knock on the door; he kicked it in.
He surrounded the city. He starved them out. And when the walls finally breached, he didn't just
replace the king. He ended the kingdom.
He took Jehoiachin in chains to Babylon.
He took the vessels of the house of the Lord.
He took the gold. He took the soldiers. He took the craftsmen.
He left the poor of the land to tend the vines, but he took the sovereignty.
And from that moment on, from 586 BC until the cry of a baby in Bethlehem, there was never a
son of David on the throne.
Think about that.
They came back from exile 70 years later, but they didn't have a King; they had a Governor.

They rebuilt the temple, but the Ark of the Covenant never returned to the Holy of Holies.
They rebuilt the walls, but they still paid taxes to Persia.
Then Persia fell, and they paid taxes to Greece.
Then Greece fell, and they paid taxes to Rome.
For six hundred years, the throne of David sat empty. It gathered dust. It was a relic of a glory
days that were long gone.
The curse of Jeconiah stood like a gate of iron across the entrance to the palace.
"None of his offspring shall prosper, sitting on the throne."
So here is the discrepancy. Here is the problem that kept the old rabbis up at night.
God promised David, "Your seed will rule forever."
But God promised Jeconiah, "Your seed is disqualified."
The line of Messiah had to go through Jeconiah to be the legal heir.
But if it went through Jeconiah, it was cursed.
It looked like the Serpent had won.
It looked like the devil had finally outsmarted God. He corrupted the line so badly that God had
to curse His own promise.
The throne was empty. The ring was gone. And the people of God were living in a land that
belonged to them, paying rent to Caesar.
It looked like the story was over.
But God is cleverer than our sin.
But God is more committed to His promise than we are to wrecking it.
Babylon thought they had won. The Serpent thought he had won.
But they didn't know that God was playing the long game.
They didn't know that God was about to do something in the shadows that would turn the lights
back on.
