From Persecutor to Preacher: Acts 9:19–31
OPENING HOOK — Bernard Nathanson → Saul
Have you ever heard of NARAL? It stood for the National Abortion Rights Action League.
It was founded in 1969 and helped shape the movement that led to the passing of Roe v.
Wade.
One of the co-founders, Dr. Bernard Nathanson, at one time directed the largest abortion clinic
in the world.
But in the 1970s, something happened to Nathanson that he just couldn’t unsee.
A new technology — real-time ultrasound — put a moving picture on the screen.
He saw a baby in the womb sucking its thumb.
He saw a heart beating.
And it broke him.
He later said, “I wasn’t performing a medical procedure. I was taking human lives.”
He didn’t just walk away from abortion — he became one of its most powerful opponents.
He produced a film called The Silent Scream, showing the reality of abortion through ultrasound
images. It rocked the nation.
But Nathanson’s story didn’t stop there.
One cold January morning in 1989, he walked into a Planned Parenthood clinic where
hundreds of pro-life demonstrators were gathered. They stood outside in the freezing air — arm
in arm, singing hymns, smiling at police, and praying… not just for the unborn, but for the
women, the doctors, even the reporters covering the event.
He saw a love he couldn’t explain.
That day, he started to believe God might be real.
Over the next seven years, bit by bit, through reading, conversations, and wrestling with guilt,
Bernard Nathanson went through a desert and came out a follower of Jesus Christ.
At age 69, he was baptized — his sins washed away, his life made new.
He didn’t lose his passion.
God redirected it.

PIVOT TO ACTS 9:

That’s Saul’s story in Acts 9. A man utterly convinced he was right, on a mission to
destroy Christians — until one encounter with Jesus redirected his entire life.
And just like Nathanson, Saul didn’t lose his zeal. God transformed it.

I. From Persecutor to Preacher (Acts 9:19–22)
Reading the Text:
“…and immediately he proclaimed Jesus in the synagogues, saying, ‘He is the Son
of God.’ And all who heard him were amazed…” (Acts 9:20–21 ESV)
Luke says “immediately” — without hesitation — Saul began preaching in the synagogues.
Here’s what’s easy to miss: in that day, a visiting Pharisee from Jerusalem wasn’t just tolerated
— he was a big deal. Synagogue leaders welcomed traveling rabbis or scholars to speak. It was
like getting a headline speaker for your conference.
Word probably spread: “Saul of Tarsus is here — trained under Gamaliel himself!”

Personal Story – My Lee University Preaching Invitation
When I was 19, I was a student at Lee University. One Sunday night, I was invited to preach at a
small church in Rome, Georgia.
To me, it felt like being asked to speak at a mega-church.
I pulled up and saw the church sign out front, and it read:
Sunday Evening
Guest Preacher
Kyle Morrow – Lee College
Now, Lee had been Lee University for a few years — but apparently, word hadn’t made it to this
church sign!
You could tell they had promoted it. People came in expecting something special. Even had hot
dogs in the fellowship hall afterward.
That’s how I picture Damascus. They probably announced it in every synagogue: “This
Sabbath, guest preacher: Saul of Tarsus, Pharisee from Jerusalem!”

To us, having a Pharisee as a guest preacher might sound about as exciting as being asked to
read Old Testament genealogies out loud. But in a first-century synagogue, this was prime-time.

The Moment of Shock
The crowd gathers. Scrolls are ready. They expect Saul to dismantle the Beleivers who’ve been
causing trouble. He stands up, clears his throat… and says:
“Jesus is the Messiah. Jesus is the Son of God.”
Jaws drop. Murmurs spread. This is not what they invited him for.

The Theological Weight
Calling Jesus “Son of God” wasn’t just poetic. It was a claim of divine identity — that Jesus
shares God’s throne (Psalm 2). Luke says Saul proved from the Scriptures that Jesus was the
Christ. This was bold, calculated, and completely unexpected.

Rhetorical Pivot
So what makes a man change completely — overnight?
Not new arguments.
Not a change of mood.
Only an encounter with Christ.
Peter went from denying Jesus to proclaiming Him as Lord.
Thomas went from doubting to worshiping: “My Lord and my God.”
Zacchaeus went from greedy tax collector to radical generosity.
The woman at the well went from hiding in shame to shouting His name.
One encounter with Jesus can take you from:
Denier ➡ Defender
Skeptic ➡ Servant
Self-absorbed ➡ Self-giving
Shamed ➡ Sent
In Saul’s case… Murderer ➡ Missionary.

Transition to Section II
But here’s the thing — just because God changes you doesn’t mean everyone believes it.
If we compare Acts 9 with Galatians 1 and 2 Corinthians 11, we find that the “many days”
in Damascus actually spanned about three years. Saul had an Arabia season — a hidden
time of preparation.
Just like Jesus was led into the wilderness before preaching.
Just like Moses spent years in Midian before confronting Pharaoh.
Just like David hid in caves before ruling Israel.
God didn’t rush Saul from encounter to platform. He shaped him in the quiet before sending him
to the crowd.

II. The Church’s Risk and Reluctance (Acts 9:23–27)
The Basket Escape
After returning from Arabia, Saul preaches in Damascus with greater boldness. Opposition
rises. The city gates are watched day and night, so believers lower him in a large basket
through an opening in the wall at night (Acts 9).
The man who once entered Damascus with the high priest’s authority now leaves crouched in a
basket — completely dependent on the people he once came to destroy.

Arrival in Jerusalem
It’s been three years since his conversion, but the disciples in Jerusalem are still afraid. They
remember him approving Stephen’s death. Some had fled because of him. Acts says they “did
not believe that he was a disciple.”
Conversion can be instant. Trust takes time.

Barnabas the Bridge-Builder
Here comes Barnabas — “Son of Encouragement.” He takes Saul to the apostles and vouches
for him. Barnabas risks his own reputation to give Saul a chance.

Every Saul needs a Barnabas. And sometimes, God calls you to be that Barnabas.

Transition to Section III
Even with Barnabas on his side, Saul’s boldness stirs up the same hostility Stephen faced just a
few years earlier.

III. Same Hostility, Different Target (Acts 9:28–31)
Déjà Vu in the Synagogues
Saul debates the same Hellenistic Jews who argued with Stephen in Acts 6. They couldn’t
out-debate Stephen, so they killed him. They can’t out-debate Saul either… so they plan to kill
him.

The Temple Vision
While praying in the temple, Saul falls into a trance and hears Jesus say:
“Hurry! Leave Jerusalem quickly, because they will not accept your testimony about Me.”
Saul pushes back — “Lord, they know my story; surely they’ll listen.”
But Jesus says, “Depart, for I will send you far away to the Gentiles.”
Sometimes God’s call isn’t to stay and fight — it’s to leave so you can fight another day.

The Rescue to Caesarea and Tarsus
The believers rush Saul to Caesarea, then send him by ship to Tarsus, his hometown. Acts 9:31
says the church enjoyed peace and was strengthened.
Saul’s removal didn’t end his mission — it positioned him for the next chapter.

The Spuris Basket Insight

In Acts 9:25, the Greek word for basket is spuris — the same word used for the large baskets in
Matthew 15 and Mark 8 that held leftovers from the feeding of the 4,000 in Gentile territory.
In the Gospels, spuris baskets carried the overflow of Jesus’ provision for Gentiles. In Acts 9,
the future apostle to the Gentiles is inside a spuris, preserved to carry the Bread of Life to the
nations.

Practical Applications
1. Embrace God’s preparation seasons. Let Him shape you before He sends you.
2. Be a Barnabas to someone. Use your voice to help someone step into their calling.
3. Accept God’s deliverance — even if it’s humbling. The basket may not look glorious,
but it’s God’s way of keeping you for His mission.

Illustration – Louis Zamperini’s Torch
Louis Zamperini was an Olympic runner in 1936. A few years later, he served as a U.S. airman
in World War II. During a mission over the Pacific, his plane was shot down. He survived 47
days drifting in a life raft, only to be captured and sent to a brutal prisoner-of-war camp.
When the war ended, Louis came home broken. But in 1949, he met Christ at a Billy Graham
crusade — and everything changed. Instead of bitterness, he began traveling the world telling
people about forgiveness in Jesus.
In 1998, the Winter Olympics were held in Nagano, Japan — near where he had been
imprisoned. And there, in his late 70s, Louis ran part of the Olympic torch relay through the very
city where he had once been beaten and starved.
The same streets where he had once stumbled as a prisoner were now filled with cheering
crowds as he carried the flame.

Closing Challenge / Altar Call
That’s Saul’s story in Acts 9. God didn’t preserve him just so he could survive. He preserved him
so he could return — not with vengeance, but with the light of the gospel.

Some of you today are in a basket moment. God’s preserving you in ways that feel humbling.
But He’s doing it so one day you can walk back into the very places you once felt defeated —
carrying His light instead of your shame.
Your escape may not look impressive. But if God is preserving you, it’s because your mission is
not finished.
Will you embrace God’s deliverance — even when it’s humbling — so you can step into His
calling with courage?
